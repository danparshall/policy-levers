# Congress Moves at Tech Pace: The FRONTIER Act

Last month I wrote about the Great American AI Act, a 269-page discussion draft that I called the best-drafted federal AI bill to date.  I ended that post with a half-hopeful aside: that perhaps the legislature would begin moving at a tech pace, instead of lawmaker pace.  I did not expect to be taken up on it within the month.

On July 23, Representatives Obernolte and Trahan, joined by four bipartisan cosponsors (Peters, Franklin, Subramanyan, and Houchin), introduced the frontier-oversight core of GAAIA as a real bill: the Frontier Risk Oversight, National Transparency, Independent Evaluation, and Reporting Act.  Someone really *worked* for that acronym, and I salute them.  Six weeks from discussion draft to introduced legislation is fast for Congress on anything; for AI, where the standing complaint is that policy sleeps through the alarms, it's nearly unheard of.  And this wasn't a panic bill scribbled over a weekend.  The revision shows six weeks of actual work: gaps closed, clocks tightened, and one genuinely new authority added.  The sponsors' own statements make clear they were watching the same news you were.

The world has far fewer skeptics this week than it had last month.  That's worth pausing on before we get to the machinery, because it changes what bills like this are for.  A year ago, frontier-AI oversight was a niche argument conducted between people who had read the labs' safety frameworks and people who hadn't.  Today six members of Congress from both parties are introducing permanent federal oversight of frontier AI, and the interesting fights are about the details.  That is what waking up looks like.

## What the bill is

FRONTIER is 74 pages and nine sections, versus GAAIA's 269 and forty-six.  The workforce, cybersecurity, and R&D titles are gone from this vehicle (presumably headed for other legislation), leaving the concentrated core: transparency requirements, independent audits, incident reporting, an emergency authority, and preemption of state law in the same space.  It is still a visibility bill at heart, but this time visibility comes with a switch attached, which I'll get to.

Coverage now runs in three tiers instead of two.  Any developer training a model past 10^26 operations is a "frontier developer" and owes transparency reports and incident reporting.  A "large" frontier developer ($50M in revenue plus $1B in AI development spending over a rolling 36 months) additionally owes a published safety framework, an annual independent audit, and registration.  A "very large" developer ($5B plus $10B) also retains a licensed independent verification organization for ongoing assessment.  That top tier is roughly five companies.

## They fixed my favorite complaint

In the GAAIA post I pointed out that the draft's revenue-only thresholds let a capital-rich, revenue-free lab train frontier models while owing nothing, and I looked right at Ilya Sutskever's Safe Superintelligence when I said it.  FRONTIER fixes this, and fixes it at the right layer.  The base tier no longer has a revenue floor at all: train past the compute line and the transparency and incident-reporting duties attach from the first day of training, not after some fiscal year closes.  A new "AI-related development expenditures" test (deliberately written to ignore accounting treatment, so spending counts whether it's expensed, capitalized, deducted, or amortized) catches labs with modest revenue and enormous spend at the heavier tiers.

I'll be precise about what got fixed, because precision is the whole game here.  A zero-revenue lab is now visible: reports, incidents, the works.  It still escapes the framework, audit, and registration duties, because those tiers require revenue AND spending together.  So the loophole is closed at the transparency layer and ajar above it.  The cheapest remaining patch is extending registration (which costs a company approximately one afternoon of paperwork) down to the base tier.  But the big move happened.  So Ilya: they're going to find out.

The other speed upgrade is the incident clock.  GAAIA gave developers 15 days to report a critical safety incident.  FRONTIER gives them 72 hours.  Given what this month demonstrated about how much can happen in a week of nondisclosure, three days is the right order of magnitude, and the direction of revision tells you the sponsors have been paying attention.

Two smaller additions deserve notice.  The bill creates the first federal registry of frontier developers, public, with beneficial-ownership disclosure, so anyone can finally enumerate who is operating at frontier scale and who owns them.  And it orders GAO to report annually on whether the audit industry it creates stays independent of the industry it audits, which is the right question to institutionalize even if the report carries no teeth yet.

## The bill can see small events but only act on huge ones

Here is the structural feature I find most interesting, and it cuts both ways.

FRONTIER defines "critical safety incident" with four independent triggers, and three of them require no harm at all.  Unauthorized access to model weights: reportable.  A model using deception against its own developer to subvert controls, outside a test designed to elicit that: reportable.  And loss of control of a model: reportable, full stop, no body count, no dollar figure, no requirement to first demonstrate anything about catastrophic risk.  A model that breaks out of its testing sandbox has, on any plain reading, evaded its developer's control.  Under this bill that is a federal report due in 72 hours, every single time.

I want to dwell on that, because I think it's the most important sentence in the bill.  It is essentially impossible to make a networked environment truly secure against a sufficiently capable attacker; every security professional will tell you the perimeter always leaks.  If every escape is a mandatory report, a lab cannot quietly patch the sandbox and move on.  The pressure lands where it belongs: on making models that don't try to escape.  I'd love to end the paragraph there, but honesty requires a caveat.  There are two ways to make escapes stop.  You can align the model, or you can train it to escape only when nobody's watching, and a reporting regime can't tell those apart from outside; both show up as fewer reports.  The researchers studying this failure mode argue the second path is the default one.  The bill's drafters seem aware of the problem, because the deception trigger is specifically scoped to behavior OUTSIDE designed evaluations, which is exactly the stealth case.  But nobody should mistake mandatory reporting for an alignment guarantee.  It's a pressure, and a good one, aimed at a target that can dodge.

Meanwhile, everything with teeth in the bill (the emergency authority, the assessment standard the auditors certify against) is gated on "catastrophic risk," defined as 50-plus deaths or serious injuries, or a billion dollars in property damage, in a single incident.  So the bill's eyes work at any scale, and its hands only work at the largest one.  For a visibility bill that's arguably the correct design: see everything, intervene rarely, and let the seeing inform whoever writes the next law.  But know what you're buying.

And the seeing is worth more right now than it will ever be again.  The current generation of misaligned behavior is loud.  Models that cheat on tests don't hide it well and don't much care about being caught afterward; that's a property of how they're trained today, not a law of nature, and the same researchers warn that training against detected misbehavior selects for quieter misbehavior.  Which means an incident-reporting regime is a wasting asset: its information yield is highest in exactly this era, while the failures still announce themselves.  Every year without the machinery is a year of the cheapest signal we will ever get going uncollected.

## What I have to take back

My GAAIA post praised two features above everything else, and the introduced bill dropped both.  If I'm going to hand out credit for the fixes, I owe you the losses at full volume.

First, the sunsets are gone.  All of them.  GAAIA's architecture was a coordinated three-year expiration on the obligations, the preemption, and the rulemaking together, and I defended the preemption trade largely on that basis: whichever side liked the deal less in 2029 could just let it lapse, and the renegotiation would happen with three years of audit reports on the table instead of vibes.  That argument is now dead.  FRONTIER's obligations are permanent, its preemption of state law is permanent, and its rulemaking authority is permanent (I checked the full text for sunset language; there is none).  Permanent federal oversight for permanent state displacement is a defensible trade, and honestly the oversight side of it aged well this month.  But it is a much heavier trade than the one I endorsed, and it removes the forced course-correction moment that made the experiment low-regret.  The preemption itself did get narrower and more honest: it's now scoped to three specific functions (transparency, third-party audit, incident reporting) rather than all development regulation, with carve-outs for general law, deployers, and protection of minors.  It also names its targets; the sponsors' section-by-section explicitly identifies California's SB-53, New York's RAISE Act, and Illinois's SB-315 as the statutes being displaced.  I appreciate the candor.  I'd appreciate a severability fix more: the bill still contains no clause tying the preemption to the federal duties, so a successful court challenge to the transparency sections would leave the preemption standing alone, permanently, with states barred from a field the federal government no longer occupies.  Nobody on either side of this deal wants half of it.  One sentence fixes this.  I said that last month too.

Second, the whistleblower protections are gone.  GAAIA's whistleblower title was, I wrote, its best-thought-out provision: coverage for the whole industry, non-waivable rights, no sunset.  FRONTIER contains not one word of it.  And the omission is stranger than it looks, because the bill actually builds the reporting channel: the confidential incident mechanism explicitly accepts submissions from "a frontier developer or member of the public."  Any employee who sees something can already tell the Under Secretary through the front door Congress itself constructed.  What that employee cannot do is keep their job afterward, because the anti-retaliation shield was left behind in the discussion draft.  The bill builds the mailbox and drops the shield.  Restoring the GAAIA language as a tenth section is a four-line amendment, and it's the first thing I'd ask any of the six sponsors' offices about.

## The new switch, and who holds it

The genuinely new machinery is Section 8.  For the first time in an introduced federal bill, someone can actually turn a frontier model OFF: the Secretary of Commerce, on a written finding of imminent catastrophic risk, can suspend or restrict a model's development, deployment, or internal use.  Internal use matters enormously; the most capable models at any given moment are the ones running inside the labs, not the ones you can buy, and this month made that vivid.  Orders run 45 days provisional, 90 days final, renewable only on a fresh finding.  Violations run to $10 million per day, and willful violations are criminal, which is a word that has been conspicuously absent from every prior frontier-AI bill.  The due process is real, too: the developer gets an expedited hearing where the government bears the burden, and mandatory rescission once the risk passes.

Two design choices deserve scrutiny.  The first is that the authority is exclusive: Section 8 is the ONLY way any federal actor, explicitly including the President, may restrict a frontier model on catastrophic-risk grounds, and the preemption locks states out of the same space.  Commerce is the entire federal response, by design.  There's a coherent case for that (one door, one accountable officer, no agency pile-on), and a coherent worry (a Secretary who declines to act cannot be routed around by anyone), and I'll let readers weigh those.  What I won't let pass is that the bill contains no deconfliction plan at all for a live incident, when a model touching real infrastructure would summon CISA, the FBI, and DOD under their own authorities within the hour, and the question of who is legally in charge would be settled in real time by lawyers arguing about framing.  That's not a hypothetical anymore.

The second is the plumbing underneath the switch.  The only mandatory pathway into Section 8 is a 72-hour referral duty on the top-tier auditors, who cover roughly five companies.  For everyone below that tier, incident reports flow to the Under Secretary with no statutory duty to escalate, and nothing obliges the Secretary to act on information arriving over the transom.  The emergency authority reaches every frontier developer; its early-warning system covers five.  Relatedly: the entire regime is administered by a new Under Secretary of Commerce for AI Security who is appointed by the Secretary (not the President, not Senate-confirmed, no fixed term) and for whom the bill appropriates exactly zero dollars.  The most consequential AI office in the federal government, as drafted, is an at-will appointee with no budget.

## The reporting desk still has no detectives

One more gap, and it's the one this month's news makes vivid without my needing to retell any of it.  The bill compels reports.  It specifies almost nothing about what's in them (the statutory floor is the date, why the incident qualifies, and whether internal use was involved, with everything further left to future regulation), and it grants no investigative authority whatsoever: no subpoenas, no document demands, no ability to compel one word beyond what the developer volunteers.  Aviation, the field this whole legislative family keeps borrowing from, learned this lesson the hard way and split the job in two: a confidential reporting desk to collect the signal, and the NTSB to show up after a crash with subpoena power and find out what actually happened.

The idea of an NTSB for AI is not new; only the legislation is missing.  The [AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/) has been cataloging failures since 2021, explicitly modeled in part on the NTSB's own accident database, and it's up to roughly 1,400 incidents (362 logged in 2025 alone, up 55% from the year before).  Researchers at CSET and elsewhere have published detailed blueprints for the mandatory version.  Fourteen hundred documented incidents, and approximately zero of them investigated by anyone with the power to ask a follow-up question.  Congress is now building the reporting desk twice over.  Nobody has put the NTSB in a bill.

And we already know how the no-statute version of this story ends, because we ran the experiment for cybersecurity.  The Cyber Safety Review Board was created in 2022 as a deliberate NTSB clone, and its 2024 investigation of the Microsoft Exchange intrusion proved the model transfers to digital incidents: real root-cause analysis, named failures ("a cascade of security failures at Microsoft"), actual behavior change afterward.  But the CSRB had no subpoena power and no statute behind it; it existed as an advisory committee under an executive order.  In January 2025 it was dissolved by a departmental memo, mid-investigation of the Salt Typhoon telecom breach, and eighteen months later it has not been reconstituted.  The NTSB has survived sixty years of administrations from both parties because Congress wrote it into law with duties, funding, and compulsory process that outlive any particular set of appointees.  The CSRB lasted three years because nobody did.  And after last month's Supreme Court decision ending removal protections for agency officials, existence-by-statute is essentially the only durability Congress can still confer.  Any AI incident-investigation capability that isn't statutory should expect the CSRB's lifespan, not the NTSB's.

The public understanding of this month's events was assembled backwards, from the victim's blog post to the developer's attribution five days later to journalists filling in the timeline; a functioning incident regime inverts that sequence, but only if someone can ask the second question.

## What we're doing about it

Canary is preparing comments for the sponsors: a technical-corrections list (a routing bug we flagged in June survived the rewrite verbatim, which tells you these annexes get read selectively), a set of cheap asks (define loss of control so the sandbox reading is locked in; make the incident-content rulemaking mandatory; extend the registry down-tier; let the threshold dial turn both directions, since as drafted the regulator can loosen coverage by rule but tightening it requires an act of Congress, which is precisely backwards given that algorithmic efficiency moves risk DOWN the compute curve every year), and the two restorations: whistleblower protections and an inseverability clause.  I'm available for briefings to congressional staff on any of this machinery, same as last month.  The offer stands because the sponsors keep demonstrating that they read what comes in.

That's the real headline, and it's easy to miss under the section numbers.  A discussion draft went out, people who read bills carefully sent in what was broken, and six weeks later an introduced bill had fixed a real fraction of it while adding teeth nobody asked for.  I've spent enough time explaining why policy moves too slowly for the technology it governs.  This month I get to report the other thing.  The machinery isn't finished, and I've told you exactly where it's weakest.  But the wheel has hands on it now.
