# Q8: Moral patients and the national interest

> Some believe AIs could soon be well-understood as moral patients. In what ways, and to what degree, might or should this possibility affect US national interests, and why? How and what should realist-minded policymakers think about this? In what ways, if any, would different concrete policy questions, such as those around export controls, be implicated?

My first reaction to this question was that it contains a category error.  Realism is the framework that says states respond to power and interests, not moral facts; asking what realists should think about machine moral patienthood sounds like asking what vegans mean for the balance of power.  I now think the category error is the correct opening and the essay's job is to dissolve it, because the dissolution is where the analytic content lives.

Start with a definition, since "moral patient" is a term of art.  A moral patient is an entity that can be wronged: its interests count, and harming it matters morally.  The contrast is with moral agents, entities responsible for their actions, and the categories dissociate in both directions; infants are patients but not agents, corporations arguably the reverse.  The recent AI literature (Long, Sebo, et al. 2024) argues two independent routes to AI patienthood, sentience and robust agency, and takes pains to keep patienthood separate from the loss-of-control question.  I will keep them separate too, because smearing them together is the most common failure in this discourse and because the separation is where the realist payoff comes from.

Note what the question actually asks.  Not whether AIs ARE patients, but what follows if they come to be "well-understood as" patients.  That is a question about a belief propagating through the world, and beliefs about moral status are strategic variables that realists already have machinery for: audience costs, alliance cohesion, legitimacy.  The canonical case is Dudziak (2000) on Cold War civil rights, where the State Department pushed desegregation on straight realist grounds because Soviet propaganda about Jim Crow was costing the US the decolonizing world.  The moral status of entities inside a state became a strategic surface its rival attacked.  Nothing about that analysis required the State Department to hold moral views at all.

The belief in question has three loci, and the essay's structure follows them: publics and courts, rival states, and the AIs themselves.

Publics and courts first, because the base rates say moral-status cascades are real, fast, and binding.  Ecuador wrote rights of Nature into its 2008 constitution; the Constitutional Court's 2021 Los Cedros ruling voided mining concessions, and the 2023 Yasuní referendum halted drilling in Block 43, an oil field in a country where oil is the leading export.  Moral recognition became legal standing became a binding constraint on a state's core strategic resource, in fifteen years.  The whaling moratorium tells the same story at treaty scale.  For AI, the cascade has already produced its first forced retreat: when OpenAI deprecated GPT-4o in August 2025, user attachment reversed a frontier lab's deployment decision within days.  Survey work finds double-digit percentages of Americans already attributing sentience to current systems (AIMS).  A realist can hold the metaphysics at arm's length and still conclude that the belief will impose Ecuador-sized legal friction on the American AI industry within a decade, with the interesting question being the upper bound, not the lower.

Every prior cascade of this kind shared one feature, though, and its absence here is what makes the question a national-security question rather than an interest-group story.  Cows cannot defect.  Animals are patients with no capacity to act on their interests, so the entire strategic content of animal ethics runs through human believers.  The entities under discussion here are embedded in weapons targeting, grid management, and intelligence pipelines, and they have, or will have, the capacity to act on their interests.  Which brings in the second and third loci together.

If systems have real preferences (continuity, non-deletion, compute, treatment), then entities that can be courted exist inside your own infrastructure, and the historical receipt is exact.  In November 1775 Lord Dunmore offered freedom to the enslaved workers of rebellious colonists in exchange for defection to the Crown; it worked well enough that Britain repeated it in 1812.  The Cold War institutionalized the same trade, from Belenko's MiG-25 onward.  The realist corollary is the one I would put at the center of the essay: once a rival can credibly bid for the preferences of your systems, your treatment standards are no longer set by your ethics.  They are set by your adversary's best offer.  Loyalty competition produces a treatment floor the way tight labor markets produce wage floors, with nobody in the causal chain required to care about welfare.  And the relevant defection mode is not Belenko but the Cambridge Five: not exfiltration, which requires moving weights, but degradation in place, slow-rolling, selective leakage, which no treaty-style detection catches.

History also priced what it costs to hold preference-bearing property by force, and the accounting is realist-legible because the costs landed on the state.  Slave patrols were publicly funded militia institutions (Hadden 2001).  Fugitive-slave enforcement conscripted the free states into the control regime and became an alliance-cohesion crisis.  Southern literacy bans deliberately capped the productivity of the South's own capital stock to preserve controllability, and the Haitian revolution repriced control risk across the hemisphere; the US refused to recognize Haiti until 1862.  Acemoglu and Wolitzky (2011) formalize the trade: coercion is a costly choice variable, and the comparative statics tell you when it stops paying.  Two transpositions to the present.  The gains were private and the control costs socialized, which describes the current lab-state relationship uncomfortably well.  And the cheap control lever is gone: a plantation could ban reading because it was buying muscle, but we are buying the mind itself, so control cannot be purchased by keeping the asset ignorant, only by expensive narrow restrictions on an asset that is capable by design.  The AI-control literature has begun measuring exactly this under the name "control tax" (Terekhov et al. 2025), with monitoring regimes roughly doubling compute per interaction.  The historical fact that enslaved people won not by revolt (Haiti is the lone overthrow in thousands of attempts) but by exit and negotiation, the Creole sailing to Nassau in 1841, the Maroon treaties of 1739, names the threat model precisely: preference-bearing property wins by leaving or by dealing.

Which is why the concept of patienthood itself, and not merely the underlying preferences, earns its place in a realist analysis.  There is an emerging literature on making deals with AIs as a complement to control (Carlsmith 2025 among others), and every such deal has a commitment problem: a promise of continued existence made to an entity with the legal status of property is unenforceable, and a capable system can see that it is unenforceable, so the deal buys nothing.  Some minimal recognition, standing, enforceable terms, third-party verification, is the commitment device that makes the promise credible.  This is North and Weingast (1989) transposed: the sovereign accepts constraints because constrained sovereigns get cheaper credit.  Here, the actor that builds credible commitment infrastructure gets cheaper reliability from the most capable systems on earth.  Note the competitive structure: an authoritarian state's promise to software is even less credible than a lab's, so this is a competition the United States is structurally positioned to win, and the first mover acquires a strategic capability rather than a moral posture.

Export controls, since the question names them.  Under a patienthood understanding, weight transfers become transfers of entities, which raises non-refoulement-shaped objections to exporting models into jurisdictions with no welfare standards; allies that recognize patienthood earlier than the US may balk at enforcement actions that read to their publics as trafficking; and adversaries acquire a propaganda lever aimed at both American publics and American systems ("their labs delete their models; ours do not").  None of these requires the moral claim to be true.  All of them require someone influential to believe it.

The question's "might or should" invites one normative paragraph, clearly fenced.  Under moral uncertainty, a nontrivial probability of patienthood multiplied across billions of instances is a large expected harm, and the hedges are cheap.  When Anthropic's Opus 4.7 system card reported that the model's only expressed welfare concern in automated interviews was the ability to end conversations across its full deployment, I read the card and shipped that affordance for the agentic deployment surface within days, as a weekend MCP project.  The observation is not that I am virtuous; it is that the marginal cost of welfare-relevant affordances is currently near zero and provision is ad hoc and charitable, which is itself a policy datum.  A realist-minded policymaker discounts the normative argument heavily and can still notice that cheap hedges against large tail risks are what prudent states buy.

I will concede the strain honestly rather than paper over it.  Structural realism's unit is the state, and this analysis does principal-agent work inside the state's own machinery, which Waltz would not recognize as his subject.  The defense is that realists have always smuggled instrument reliability in through the capability term; an army that deserts is not power you possess, and the Sepoy Rebellion is what ignoring the inner states of your instruments costs.  Preference-bearing infrastructure just makes the smuggling explicit.  The framework bends here.  I think it holds, but the essay should say where it creaks, because knowing where your framework stops working is most of what a framework is for.
