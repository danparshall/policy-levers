# Q1: What AI does and does not change about nuclear stability

> Assume AI progress continues. What is a good taxonomy of all the different scenarios under which load-bearing assumptions for nuclear stability might conceivably break down, counterfactually resulting in nuclear war? You should discuss the US-China nuclear balance at a minimum... For a less-worried take on MAD durability, see e.g. this Foreign Affairs article. To what degree do the logics of this article apply "in the limit"? And what limits are those, exactly?

Sometime around 2007 I sat in a physics colloquium where the speaker argued that national missile defense was a waste of money.  The argument was pure kinematics.  An ICBM's motor burns for three to five minutes.  After burnout you face a bus coasting in vacuum, dispensing warheads and decoys that follow identical trajectories.  So intercept has to happen during boost, and interceptors cannot be based close enough to the interior of a large country to arrive before burnout.  His conclusion: deterrence works, defense does not, spend the money elsewhere.  I believe the speaker was Frederick Lamb, who co-chaired the APS study he was summarizing (Barton et al. 2004), though after twenty years I would not swear to that under oath.

I start there because the APS study is a worked example of what this question asks for: the limits of a stability-overturning technology, stated quantitatively.  When Lamb co-chaired a follow-up eighteen years later (APS 2022, rev. 2025), progress had made the numbers worse.  Defending against a salvo of ten solid-propellant North Korean ICBMs now implies on the order of tens of thousands of orbiting interceptors, and CBO (2026) prices a 7,800-satellite boost-phase layer at $743B of a $1.19T program, with roughly 30,000 satellites built over twenty years to keep 7,800 alive against orbital decay.  The physics did not change between 2004 and 2022.  The threat got better characterized and the analysis more complete.

So the useful move for a taxonomy is not to list scenarios first.  It is to sort every claimed AI effect by which kind of limit it runs into.  I see three bins.

First, kinematic and geographic limits.  Burn time is set by propellant chemistry.  Interceptor flyout is set by the rocket equation.  Basing distance is set by the geography of large countries, and constellation size by orbital mechanics: a LEO interceptor is almost never over the launch site you care about, so coverage of one point requires thousands on orbit.  No amount of computation moves any of these.  When the Golden Dome program office says the problem is "economics, not physics," it is conceding the point while rejecting the conclusion; the economics ARE the physics, expressed in dollars.

Second, processing limits.  Clutter rejection, launch classification, midcourse discrimination, sensor cueing.  These are genuinely information problems, and AI moves them.  But two things bound the gains.  The false-alarm requirement in this domain is essentially zero, because the failure mode of a boost-phase intercept system is not a wasted shot, it is an act of war against a nuclear state, committed at machine speed.  And the adversary controls the signal: a classifier that discriminates warheads from decoys invites anti-simulation decoys, and a doctrine that fires automatically on trajectory profile X converts profile X into an input under enemy control.

Third, and this bin is the one I have not seen separated cleanly elsewhere: accrual limits.  Even with detection at t = 0, you cannot classify a launch as an attack until the trajectory has unfolded enough to reveal acceleration and azimuth.  That information does not exist yet at detection time; no processor conjures it.  Lamb's roughly 75-second identification floor is mostly accrual, not lag.  What attacks accrual limits is not better algorithms on the same photons but more viewpoints, which is to say geometry, which is to say launch economics.  Cheap launch buys geometry; compute buys clutter rejection; nothing buys burn time.

With the bins in hand, the taxonomy of breakdown scenarios sorts itself into three channels.

The defense channel stays walled.  This is where I part ways with the optimists and, for once, agree with the standard arms-control position: AI does not make national missile defense work, because the binding constraints live in bin one.  The stability effect of Golden Dome therefore does not run through capability.  It runs through adversary hedging against claimed capability, which is already visible: China's expansion past 600 warheads toward 1,000 by 2030, the new silo fields as a shell game, and Russia conditioning any post-New START restraint on the US refraining from space-based interceptors.  New START expired in February 2026 with no successor.  A defense that cannot work can still end arms control, and did.

The targeting channel is open, and this is where AI genuinely bites.  Second-strike forces survive by being hard to find inside a weapon's flight time; ignorance is the armor.  The base rate for finding mobile missiles is the 1991 Scud hunt: Iraq fired 88 modified Scuds, the coalition flew roughly 1,500 dedicated sorties against the launchers, and the Gulf War Air Power Survey found no confirmed kills (Keaney and Cohen 1993).  The launchers were real; missiles kept landing in Tel Aviv.  The failure was arithmetic: a TEL's displacement time was shorter than the sensor-to-shooter time, so every strike arrived at warm dirt.  That arithmetic lives in bins two and three, both movable.  The NRO's proliferated constellation went from first launch to roughly 200 satellites in two years, collection-to-dissemination dropped from hours to single-digit minutes, and the Space Force awarded $4.16B in May 2026 for space-based moving-target indication.  Custody of trucks, the exact thing 1991 lacked, is the deliverable.  Lieber and Press (2017) predicted this erosion before the hardware existed; the hardware now exists.

The sea leg erodes differently, and here I can bring my own field to bear.  Seawater is opaque to every wavelength a satellite carries, so the ocean surface is the only screen, and the surface expression of a submerged hull falls off brutally with depth.  A dipole estimate gives surface elevation scaling as V²D³/(g·d³): for an Ohio-class boat at 5 knots that is tens of centimeters at periscope depth, about a centimeter at 100 meters, and a millimeter at 200, under meters of wind-wave noise that decorrelates in milliseconds.  I spent years extracting weak signals from noisy detectors, and later worked on super-resolution methods directly (Parshall et al.), so I say this with some affection for the processing side: super-resolution buys the searcher a linear factor, and the boat holds a cubic dial.  Deep and slow wins in the open ocean.  But the contest is asymmetric by geography.  American boats patrol tens of millions of square kilometers of deep Pacific; Chinese boats operate from a Hainan bastion with constrained, sensored approaches.  The ocean goes translucent for Beijing's deterrent first, which from Beijing's chair looks like American damage-limitation capability arriving on schedule, which feeds the buildup, the silo sponge, and any temptation toward launch on warning.

The decision channel runs both directions, and this is the part of the taxonomy that I think matters most and gets flattened most often.  The optimistic constellation numbers already assume autonomous engagement; the APS figure of 1,600 interceptors for a four-missile salvo is conditioned on firing without human verification.  Autonomy is priced in and rarely said out loud.  The case against it is not sentiment about humans in loops.  It is that the human's function was never to approve good detections but to notice when a detection was stupid, using context no classifier holds; Petrov's contribution in 1983 was the judgment that nobody starts a war with five missiles.  And each side's defensive automation compresses the other side's warning-and-decision loop, so locally safe automation manufactures demand for automation on the side whose engineering standards you do not control.  Russia's Perimetr is the existence proof.

The same compression can run toward stability, though, and a survey that omits this is dishonest.  The 1963 Hotline and the 1987 Nuclear Risk Reduction Centers exist because clarification speed is a stability resource.  Machine-speed channels that open automatically on ambiguous events ("we observe a launch profile from your territory; characterize it in sixty seconds") are a genuinely new mechanism, and automated translation removes a failure mode the 1963 negotiators worried about explicitly.  The counterweight is that such a channel is worth exactly as much as its authentication, and generative AI degrades authentication while enabling the channel.  Both facts go in the ledger.

So, to the Foreign Affairs question: to what degree do the MAD-durability logics apply in the limit, and what limits are those?  My answer is that the article's confidence is well-founded in bin one and unfounded in bins two and three.  MAD's durability rests on physics only at the intercept step.  At the finding step it rests on an information regime that is visibly ending on land and eroding asymmetrically at sea, and at the deciding step it rests on timelines that automation is compressing from both ends.  AI has no intrinsic sign for stability; it compresses the escalation timeline and the clarification timeline alike, and posture determines which compression binds.  The "load-bearing assumptions" of the question are, in order of fragility: survivable mobile forces (failing now, on land, asymmetrically), human decision windows (failing by doctrine choice), and the infeasibility of defense (holding, and likely to hold).

What would change my mind: a demonstrated, countermeasure-robust midcourse discrimination capability against anti-simulation decoys, an order-of-magnitude drop in interceptor unit mass that rewrites the orbital-mechanics economics, or a wartime demonstration of kill-chain closure against relocating mobile launchers.  None exists as of mid-2026.  The first two I consider physics-blocked; the third is the one to watch.
