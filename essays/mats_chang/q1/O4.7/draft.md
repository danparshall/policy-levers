# AI and MAD: a response to Winter-Levy and Lalwani

*"a Mutual Assured Destruction [posture] as a goal is, almost literally, mad. MAD."* - Donald Brennan

Thinking about MAD is like a trip through the looking-glass: defense is unstable, offense is pointless, and insanity is rational. That doesn't make it WRONG, but it's disconcerting nevertheless. Six decades of stable coexistence between nuclear peers is a reasonable evidence base for the doctrine's basic durability, and it's why Sam Winter-Levy and Nikita Lalwani's August 2025 Foreign Affairs essay reads as sober. They argue that AI, whatever else it does, does not dismantle the three pieces of MAD that make it work: second-strike capabilities remain functional, command-and-control networks can't be taken out in one blow, and missile defense faces a physics- and economics-locked asymmetry that favors the attacker. On each of these I think the authors are too sanguine, and beyond them I think they miss two developments that matter more than any of the three.

## On second-strike

The authors' argument on TELs and SSBNs runs through concealment inside a weapon's time-of-flight. TELs move; oceans hide submarines; both defeat continuous tracking. This was true when the coalition flew ~1,500 sorties against Iraqi Scuds in 1991 and got zero confirmed mobile kills against real launchers in open desert. It remained true through the 2000s when satellite revisit rates were measured in hours.

It is much less true now. NRO's proliferated architecture has ~200 low-Earth-orbit satellites on missions the director describes in plain English as "making it harder for our adversaries to hide, while reducing time to insights for our customers from minutes to seconds." Space Systems Command awarded SpaceX $4.16B in May 2026 for the Space-Based Airborne Moving Target Indicator program. As inter-satellite gap time falls, the survivable displacement window for a shoot-and-scoot TEL falls with the square of transit time. That is arithmetic, not assertion. On the submarine side, seawater is EM-opaque at any band a satellite can carry and hulls stay hidden at depth. The searcher's handles are surface expressions: Bernoulli hump, internal-wave wakes, chokepoint egress, mast exposure. I have done work on sensor linking, in Physics and in Aviation (not easy, but possible), and the shape of the problem is that fusion across cheap sensors at high revisit rates buys you tracking no single sensor could produce. Uber built a road-quality map covering every 50 m of American roads using nothing but accelerometer traces from driver phones. No single measurement carries any signal; the fused product carries one that would have been an intelligence coup in 2010.

The physics-lock the authors correctly identify is real. It is also ASYMMETRIC. Ohio-class SSBNs patrol tens of millions of km² of deep, stratification-messy Pacific and Atlantic: unlayerable, unlockable, safe. China's 094s operate from Hainan through constrained approaches under a US sensor architecture already concentrated on the Chinese near-seas: layerable, lockable, exposed. The ocean does not go transparent uniformly. It goes transparent in the direction that threatens the weaker deterrent first, which reads from Beijing's chair as the US approaching damage-limitation capability, which is exactly the pressure driving the 350-plus new silo build. The authors report the constraints correctly and do not report the asymmetry. The asymmetry is the stability-relevant fact.

## On command and control

The authors argue that C3 is buried, redundant, satellite-based, and requires persistent access to compromise. All true. What they miss is that AI progress shifts Beijing's THREAT MODEL on classical C3 assumptions across the board, and cannot be verified from the outside. Airgap, custom hardware, physical isolation, hardware-random authentication, human-in-loop verification: each was a reliable assumption a decade ago; each is now conditional on the adversary's AI capability, which is uncertain even to the adversary within tight bounds. Mythos did not have to pwn Chinese NC3 for the destabilization to fire. It DEFINITELY makes them scared of being pwned.

The response to unverifiable erosion of a foundational assumption is doctrine-level, not technical. Chinese planners cannot patch away Mythos-class exposure risk because they cannot bound it; they can only reduce their reliance on the assumption. Reducing reliance means launch-on-warning, pre-delegation, dead-hand. These are the exact commitment devices that make deterrence work in Schelling's frame, arriving not through choice but through defensive necessity. Stuxnet's actual lesson was that adversaries harden, diversify, and treat their arsenal as potentially compromised, which produces use-it-or-lose-it pressure. Cyber counterforce is destabilizing precisely because states will believe it might work, whether or not it does.

## On missile defense

The authors are right that no missile-defense system can cost-effectively beat a peer's saturation attack, and they are right that decoys, chaff, and adversarial injection make discrimination a real problem. Their argument is that this asymmetry is stable because it is physics- and economics-locked.

The physics part is correct at peak intercept. The economics part is much less certain, because AI plausibly changes the discrimination side of the ledger in ways the authors underweight. The Uber road-quality result generalizes: sensor fusion over many cheap incidental measurements can extract signals that look physics-forbidden at any single measurement. The acceptable false-alarm rate for autonomous intercept is near-zero against a heavy-tailed adversarially-injectable clutter distribution, and I don't want to overstate how much AI moves the operating point on the discriminator ROC. But the direction of travel is real, and the authors settle the question prematurely.

## Where I agree, mostly

On perception, the authors and I agree: the mere prospect of AI progress can change default postures whether or not the underlying capabilities materialize. Improved algorithms (nearly certain if AGI arrives), improved sensor architectures (certainly possible), and improved cyber tooling (well underway) all lower the technical threshold for launch-on-warning. Where I extend the argument: LOW becomes ADOPTABLE by second-tier nuclear states that could not previously execute it. Pakistan and DPRK do not have the C3 infrastructure to run a genuine LOW posture with acceptable false-alarm discipline. AI-drafted response templates and AI-assisted early warning bring it in reach. The second-tier states move first because they have the fewest resources to adapt any other way, and they are also the states with the thinnest assessment pipelines. The authors correctly note that perceptions matter; they then treat it as a caveat when it is the load-bearing thesis.

## Further one: AI as prize

The authors do not consider AI itself as a strategic prize, and this is where their analysis is most incomplete. If we are on a path to artificial superintelligence, first-mover gains are enormous and possibly decisive. Compute is the bottleneck for AI capability. Fabs are the bottleneck for compute. Fabs are physically concentrated: TSMC Taiwan primary, TSMC Arizona ramping, SMIC pushing 7nm without EUV, Samsung and Intel trailing.

The US, worried about Chinese AI progress, cut off leading-node chip exports starting October 2022 and tightened them since. China responded by accelerating domestic fab development, which is coming online now. The US, worried in turn about supply-chain concentration in Taiwan, began domestic fab production, which is also ramping. Both sides are running toward domestic self-sufficiency and both have windows that will close.

China's window is the period before domestic capability arrives, during which TSMC is a Chinese strategic asset (as prize if taken intact, as leverage if denied to the US) and destruction is undesirable. The US window is the period before its own ramp reduces the cost of losing TSMC access, during which extended-deterrence credibility toward Taipei remains high. The dangerous regime is when they close on different timelines. The side whose window closes later has strong incentive to act while the other side's leverage is still binding.

Once Beijing has domestic capability, the payoff matrix on Taiwan inverts. TSMC intact benefits Beijing marginally; TSMC intact in US hands benefits Washington strongly; TSMC destroyed is a good Beijing outcome relative to TSMC-in-US-hands. The "silicon shield" assumption breaks. It assumed both parties would inherit the fabs if they won; the assumption held while both parties still needed them. The US side of this generates a Schelling commitment paradox. Every visible US measure that hedges against TSMC loss (Arizona ramp acceleration, allied fab diversification, airlift contingency planning) reduces vulnerability AND reduces credibility to defend Taiwan simultaneously, because visible hedging signals reduced willingness to bear the cost. Every hedge move is read in Beijing as a credibility discount on the commitment.

None of this changes the nuclear balance directly. What it does is put pressure on a conventional-force flashpoint whose escalation pathway to nuclear runs through conventional-nuclear entanglement (DF-21D and DF-26 are dual-capable; US Navy assets that would engage are SIOP-connected; JASSM and LRHW strike packages carry discrimination problems the adversary has to solve under time pressure). The load-bearing assumption breaking is the deepest one MAD relies on: great powers avoid direct conflict because nuclear risk exceeds any conventional prize. Historically no conventional prize was worth the risk. AGI plausibly is worth the risk, or is believed to be worth the risk in Beijing and in Washington, which is analytically the same thing. That is the argument the authors do not have.

## Further two: HITL to AITL

The other development the authors underweight is the replacement of human-in-the-loop by AI-in-the-loop as the operative model for military decision support. Schelling, in *Strategy of Conflict*, notes that an imperfect decision system is a stability RESOURCE. The threat that leaves something to chance is credible precisely because it is not fully under the leader's control. AITL is the technical apotheosis of that idea: a decision system with genuine autonomy, pre-scripted responses, and a rehearsed commitment structure.

The military wants this, and Schelling would in principle endorse the logic. Pre-delegation, launch-on-warning, and dead-hand are all commitment devices, all preferred by military planners over civilian ratification lag, and AI accelerates the technical feasibility of every one of them. Automation-bias literature converging over three decades tells us that human-in-the-loop empirically collapses to human-as-ratifier. The Biden-Xi November 2024 joint statement affirming human control of launch decisions was worth extracting from Beijing precisely because it was slipping.

And aaaand: the same demand signal that drives AITL in nuclear command drives AITL in autonomous weapons broadly, in intelligence pipelines, in targeting systems. The military's preference for commitment devices pulls AI development toward capability profiles that trade robustness for speed, interpretability for confidence, and human oversight for autonomous action. This is destabilizing at two layers. At the nuclear-doctrine layer, AITL adoption compresses the decision loop below the timescale at which classification error can be caught: the accrual bin on early-flight classification says the discriminating information does not exist in the photons yet, so a faster evaluator does not get the right answer sooner, it gets a confident answer sooner. Petrov survived his 1983 event because a slow correct instinct beat a fast confident wrong system. And at the AI-development layer, the military-adjacent demand for AITL pulls commercial AI in profoundly unsafe directions. The authors treat AI-in-nuclear-decisions as a symptom to warn against. Under the operative clause of continued AI progress, it is a driver: of doctrine, of arsenal posture, and of the AI development trajectory itself.

## Close

The physics-locks the authors correctly identify protect capabilities. Stability was always epistemic and doctrinal, and both are what AI attacks. On their three primary points I think they underweight the actual erosion: sensor linking, cyber-belief, and discrimination all move against them under continued progress. On perceptions they concede the ground and then act as if they had not. And they miss the two developments that together determine whether the load-bearing MAD assumptions survive the next decade: that AI is a prize whose pursuit changes the payoff matrix on conventional flashpoints, and that AI's own development is being pulled toward postures that make the doctrinal side of stability worse. Six decades of stable coexistence is a good evidence base for the pre-takeoff regime. The pre-takeoff regime is not the regime the question asks about.
