# The looking-glass holds, but the glass is getting thin

"To think that a Mutual Assured Destruction posture is a good goal is, almost literally, mad." Donald Brennan coined the acronym in the 1960s as an insult, and the insult stuck because it was accurate. Thinking about MAD is a trip through the looking-glass: missile defense is destabilizing, offense is pointless, and the arrangement that keeps everyone alive is a mutual promise to commit suicide if provoked. None of that makes MAD wrong. Deterrence has held for seventy years and the logic is sound. It is just disconcerting, and the disconcerting parts are exactly where a new technology can do damage without anyone deciding to.

In a July 2025 Foreign Affairs essay, Winter-Levy and Lalwani argue that AI will not dramatically destabilize this arrangement. Their case rests on three loadbearing claims, and I think each is right as far as it goes. Second-strike forces stay survivable: finding mobile launchers and ballistic-missile submarines remains a matter of probability, and no state bets its survival on a probability. Command and control stays intact: the networks are too redundant and too buried to decapitate in one stroke, and any overt attempt looks like strike preparation and invites the launch it was meant to prevent. And missile defense stays a loser's game: the physics favor the attacker, who can always add cheap warheads and decoys faster than the defender can add expensive interceptors. On that last point I side with them without reservation, because the asymmetry is kinematic and no amount of computation moves it. That is also, in the looking-glass way, the reassuring pillar: because defense cannot work, no one is tempted to think they can survive going first.

I am far less sanguine about the first two, and I think the essay misses two developments entirely. Let me take the three pillars first, then the two omissions.

## The pillars are softer than they look

**Finding things.** The authors treat the hunt for mobile launchers and submarines as a contest that stays roughly balanced, because decoys and concealment scale as cheaply as sensors do. What this misses is that custody of a moving target is a sensor-fusion problem, and sensor fusion is precisely what has become cheap. The base rate is the 1991 Scud hunt: the coalition flew roughly 1,500 sorties against Iraqi mobile launchers and scored zero confirmed kills, because a launcher's displacement time was shorter than the time from detection to shooter. Detecting a launch was never the problem; holding the truck was. That gap is now closing from the sensing side on a visible schedule. The US operates around 8,000 of the roughly 12,000 active satellites in orbit and adds more monthly, and in May 2026 the Space Force put $4.16 billion into space-based moving-target indication, which is the "hold custody of the truck" capability 1991 lacked.

I want to be concrete about why I take this seriously rather than waving at it, because I have done this work, in physics and later in aviation: stitching intermittent, individually-worthless detections into a coherent track is hard but not mysterious, and it yields to more looks and better fusion rather than to any single breakthrough. The civilian proof of scale is almost comic. Uber reconstructed the road-surface condition of essentially every fifty-meter segment of American road out of nothing but the accelerometers in drivers' phones, a far weaker signal than a launcher's, extracted at national scale from sensors nobody deployed for the purpose. The military version has purpose-built sensors and a decade of persistent-surveillance doctrine behind it. Decoys still scale cheaply, and discrimination is genuinely hard, but "the decoys stay ahead forever" is an assertion about a contest whose inputs are changing by an order of magnitude, and it is the assertion the essay never defends.

The submarine leg is the one place physics actually protects the second strike, and it protects it unevenly. Seawater is opaque to every wavelength a satellite carries, so a hull at patrol depth is invisible from orbit except through faint surface expressions that fall off roughly as depth cubed, and the acoustic contest turns on terms the evader controls: a boat that runs slow and deep drops its radiated noise below an ambient ocean that machine learning cannot see through, because the signal simply is not there to be learned. But this floor protects the boat that can use open ocean. American submarines patrol millions of square kilometers of deep Pacific; Chinese boats operate from a constrained, already-instrumented bastion near Hainan and are noisier. So the ocean goes translucent asymmetrically, against Beijing's deterrent first, which the essay files under "a concern for the United States" without noticing that it cuts the other way.

**Command and control.** Here the authors price the wrong thing. They show, correctly, that you cannot physically decapitate a modern nuclear command system, and then treat that as the end of the argument. But the system does not have to be decapitated to fail. It only has to be *believed* to be penetrated. Nuclear command increasingly shares sensors, satellites, and networks with conventional command, so an intrusion meant for espionage or conventional war looks, from the target's side, exactly like preparation for a disarming strike, and the target reacts to its own worst case. This is where the frontier-model dimension lands, and it lands through fear rather than capability. The relevant fact is not whether anyone has actually compromised Chinese nuclear command. It is that the Chinese may reasonably worry a system like Mythos already has, and cannot prove otherwise, because the absence of an intrusion is exactly the thing you cannot verify. A capability that is cheap to fear and impossible to disprove moves a rival's posture whether or not it exists, and it moves it toward exactly the hair-trigger the essay assumes away.

## Where I actually agree with them, and why it is worse than they say

The authors' strongest move is their concession: none of the three pillars has to *actually* fall for AI to be dangerous, because a state that merely *believes* its forces are more findable will hedge, disperse, delegate, and shorten its own launch loop. I agree completely, and I think it is the most important sentence in their essay, which is why it is strange that they use it to reassure. China spent decades in the most relaxed nuclear posture of any major power: a few hundred warheads, no launch-on-warning, warheads stored separately from missiles. That relaxation was affordable because concealment was trusted. As sensing erodes that trust, China is visibly buying back survivability the expensive way, with 300-plus new silos as a warhead sponge and open movement toward early-warning counterstrike, which is launch-on-warning under another name. Better algorithms are nearly certain if AI progress continues, and better sensors are at least possible, and together they put launch-on-warning within reach of even small nuclear states that previously could not afford it. The mere prospect is enough to change the default posture. That is the authors' own mechanism, and it points somewhere darker than they take it.

## What they leave out entirely

Both omissions share a feature: neither requires AI to touch a single warhead.

**AI as the prize.** The essay treats AI as a tool acting on arsenals and never considers AI as the object worth fighting over. If we are on a path to artificial superintelligence, the gains to the first mover are enormous and possibly decisive, and that turns the race itself into a source of preventive-war pressure on a visible clock. The dynamics are already in motion and they run straight through Taiwan. Fearing that China would move too fast, the US made the cutting-edge chips needed for training almost entirely unavailable except by specific license. That control did the predictable thing: it forced China to build indigenous leading-edge fabrication, which is coming online now. The US, in turn, worried about depending on Taiwanese fabs in a crisis, has begun onshoring production of its own. Watch what that does to the "silicon shield." Taiwan's fabs deterred a Chinese attack only while both sides needed them intact. Once China has domestic capacity and the US does not yet, Taiwan's fabs stop being an asset China would lose by attacking and become a lever China can deny to Washington by destroying. The shield does not merely weaken in that window; it reverses, and a rational Beijing becomes *more* willing to wreck the island's chip base, not less, because the destruction now falls mainly on its rival. The reshoring meant to buy safety opens the most dangerous window on the way there, and the timing of the two countries' buildouts is the whole game.

**AI in the loop.** The second omission is the replacement of the human in the loop by an AI in the loop, and it is where deterrence logic and safety logic collide head-on. Schelling saw the shape of this in 1960: he argued that it can be rational to give up control of your own response, because a threat you cannot climb down from is more credible than one you can, and he grounded it in the observation that rationality is assembled from external parts, including "the rationality of one's agents." Delegating your retaliation to an automated agent is precisely that move, and it makes the threat more believable by making it less controllable. The military wants this for the ordinary reason that automation is faster and removes a hesitating human from a time-critical loop. Schelling explains why a coldly rational state would *want* it too. And that is the problem, stated at full strength: the thing deterrence rewards is the thing safety must prevent. Every alignment goal we care about, corrigibility, an off-switch, a human veto, predictable behavior, is anti-commitment. A deterrent's credibility is maximized by an agent that is docile until provoked and then genuinely uncontrollable, which is the exact system safety engineers are trying to make impossible. Putting AI in the nuclear loop does not just import the accident risk of an immature technology; it places a strategic bounty on building that technology in its least safe form. The delegated humans of the Cold War were unreliable in a way that once saved us: Vasili Arkhipov refused to authorize a launch nobody had authorized him to refuse. An AI built for maximum deterrent credibility is the first agent in that chair with no capacity to refuse designed in.

## In the limit

So how far does the Foreign Affairs logic carry? Cleanly on the one pillar that is physics: defense stays a loser's game in the limit, because the limit is kinematic. Partly on the others, and the failures are not symmetric. Their command-and-control argument is right about the bunker and wrong about the belief, and belief is what the entanglement between nuclear and conventional systems runs on. Their targeting argument treats the armor as constant when it is eroding, and eroding first against the weaker of the two arsenals. And their frame has no room at all for the two developments that need no warhead: the race for AI itself, and the delegation of the decision to it. The authors are calibrated on the physics and too comfortable on the posture, and posture is the part AI is quietly rewriting. AI has no fixed sign for stability. It compresses the time to escalate and the time to reassure with the same hand, and doctrine, not physics, chooses which one binds. Their essay is a careful study of the physics. The harder question is the posture, and there the looking-glass logic holds only until someone, reasonably and out of fear, decides to stop waiting.
