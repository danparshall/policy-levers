## The colloquium

Sometime around 2007 I sat in a physics colloquium where the speaker argued that national missile defense was a waste of money. The argument was pure kinematics. An ICBM's motor burns for three to five minutes. After burnout you face a bus coasting in vacuum, dispensing warheads and decoys on identical trajectories, so intercept has to happen during boost, and interceptors cannot be based close enough to the interior of a large country to arrive in time. His conclusion: deterrence works, defense does not, spend the money elsewhere. I believe the speaker was Frederick Lamb, who co-chaired the APS study he was summarizing (Barton et al. 2004), though after twenty years I would not swear to it under oath.

I start there because the APS study is a worked example of the thing this question actually asks for: the limits of a stability-overturning technology, stated quantitatively. When Lamb co-chaired a follow-up eighteen years later (APS 2022, rev. 2025), the numbers had gotten worse, not better. Defending against a salvo of ten solid-propellant ICBMs now implies on the order of tens of thousands of orbiting interceptors, and CBO (2026) prices a boost-phase layer at three-quarters of a trillion dollars inside a $1.19T program, with roughly 30,000 satellites built over twenty years to keep about 7,800 alive against orbital decay. The physics did not change between 2004 and 2022. The threat got better characterized and the analysis got more complete. That is the shape of a real limit: it does not move when you throw money or compute at it.

So the useful first move for a taxonomy is not to list scenarios. It is to build a discriminator that sorts real breakdowns from expensive-sounding ones, and then run every claimed AI effect through it. I use three bins.

**Kinematic and geographic limits.** Burn time is propellant chemistry. Interceptor flyout is the rocket equation. Basing distance is the geography of large countries; constellation size is orbital mechanics, because a LEO interceptor is almost never over the launch point you care about, so covering one point takes thousands on orbit. No amount of computation moves any of these. When the Golden Dome program office says the problem is "economics, not physics," it is conceding the point while pretending to reject it: the economics *are* the physics, denominated in dollars.

**Processing limits.** Clutter rejection, launch classification, midcourse discrimination, sensor cueing. These are genuine information problems, and AI moves them. Two things bound the gains. The false-alarm requirement here is essentially zero, because the failure mode of an autonomous boost-phase intercept is not a wasted shot, it is an act of war against a nuclear state committed at machine speed. And the adversary controls the signal: a classifier that separates warheads from decoys invites anti-simulation decoys, and a rule that fires on trajectory profile X turns profile X into an input under enemy control.

**Accrual limits.** This is the bin I have not seen drawn cleanly elsewhere. Even with detection at t = 0, you cannot classify a launch as an attack until the trajectory has unfolded enough to reveal acceleration and azimuth. That information does not exist yet at detection time, and no processor conjures data that has not arrived. Lamb's ~75-second identification floor is mostly accrual, not sensor lag. What attacks an accrual limit is not a better algorithm on the same photons but more viewpoints, which is to say geometry, which is to say launch economics. Cheap launch buys geometry; compute buys clutter rejection; nothing buys burn time.

Three currencies, three bins, each bounded, and only two of them movable. That is the tool. Now the Foreign Affairs article.

## Where Winter-Levy and Lalwani are right

The FA piece (Winter-Levy and Lalwani, Carnegie, August 2025) makes three claims and one concession, and the structure maps onto the bins almost too neatly. Their claim on missile defense is that attackers keep the cost advantage: salvos, decoys, direct attacks on the defensive constellation, and if all else fails, delivery methods that are not ballistic at all. That is a bin-one argument and it is correct. I part ways with the arms-control mainstream on plenty, but not here: defense stays walled, and it stays walled in the limit, because the wall is kinematic. Their second claim is that command and control is too resilient to decapitate in one stroke: buried bunkers, redundant networks, the fact that overt moves against C3 look like strike preparation and invite the very launch they were meant to prevent. Their third is that finding mobile launchers and submarines stays a matter of probability, and states will not bet the homeland on a probability. Their concession, which is really the heart of the piece, is that none of this has to be true for AI to be dangerous: if a state merely *believes* its forces are more findable, it will build more, disperse more, delegate more, and shorten its own launch loop, and those hedges are where the risk lives.

That concession is correct and it is doing more work than they let it. But it also quietly saves the argument, because it lets them treat every erosion as a perception problem rather than a capability problem. My disagreement is narrow and specific: on all three of their capability claims, they are describing a threshold, and they never say where the threshold is. "States are unlikely to risk a splendid first strike on anything less than a safe bet" is true and unfalsifiable as written. The question the prompt asks is exactly the one they skip. What is the bet, in what units, and what moves it? Run the three claims through the bins and they come apart at different rates.

## Cluster one: retaliation survives and arrives (the targeting channel)

Second-strike forces survive by being hard to find inside a weapon's flight time. Ignorance is the armor. The FA authors know this; where I think they are too comfortable is in treating the armor as roughly constant.

The base rate for finding mobile missiles is the 1991 Scud hunt. Iraq fired 88 modified Scuds, the coalition flew on the order of 1,500 dedicated sorties against the launchers, and the Gulf War Air Power Survey found no confirmed mobile-launcher kills (Keaney and Cohen 1993). The launchers were real, and missiles kept landing in Tel Aviv through the final week, so this is a clean experiment, not an intelligence artifact. The failure was arithmetic: a TEL's displacement time was shorter than the sensor-to-shooter time, so every prosecution arrived at warm dirt. Detection of a launch is not custody of a launcher.

That gap lives in bins two and three, both movable. The FA authors list the countermeasures, netting and decoys and emission discipline and faster launchers, and conclude the contest stays roughly balanced. Here is where I think they undercount the trajectory. Custody of trucks is a fusion problem over cheap ubiquitous sensors, and that problem is being solved in the adjacent civilian economy right now. Uber built a map of road-surface condition for essentially every fifty-meter segment of US road out of nothing but the accelerometers in drivers' phones: a signal far weaker than a TEL's, extracted at national scale, from sensors nobody deployed for the purpose. The military version has purpose-built sensors and a decade of persistent-surveillance doctrine behind it. The wide-area-motion-imagery lineage (Constant Hawk, Gorgon Stare, ARGUS) already demonstrated the "rewind the tape" move over a city: scrub backward from an event to the emplacement, then forward to map the network. The satellite version is arriving on a visible schedule. The NRO's proliferated constellation went from first launch to roughly 200 satellites in about two years, collection-to-dissemination dropped from hours to single-digit minutes, and the Space Force awarded $4.16B in May 2026 for space-based moving-target indication, the exact "hold custody of trucks" capability 1991 lacked. The US operates on the order of 8,000 of the ~12,000 active satellites in orbit and is adding more monthly. Decoys and netting scale cheaply, yes, but the discrimination side is precisely what fusion over many looks is good at, and the FA article's confidence that decoys stay ahead is an assertion about a contest whose inputs are changing by an order of magnitude. Lieber and Press (2017) called this erosion before the hardware existed. The hardware now exists.

The sea leg erodes differently, and here I can bring my own field to bear, because "AI finds the subs" is a claim about signal-to-noise, not about algorithms. Seawater is opaque to every wavelength a satellite carries, so the surface is the only screen, and the surface expression of a submerged hull falls off brutally with depth. A dipole estimate gives surface elevation as roughly V²D³/(g·d³): for an Ohio-class boat at 5 knots that is tens of centimeters near periscope depth, about a centimeter at 100 meters, a millimeter at 200, under meters of wind-wave noise that decorrelates in milliseconds. I worked on super-resolution directly (Parshall et al. [DAN: cite]), so I say this with affection for the processing side and then against it: super-resolution buys the searcher a linear factor from redundancy and a known kernel, and the ocean surface denies the redundancy, because every satellite pass is a single look at a fresh realization of the wave field. The searcher holds a linear dial; the evader holds a cubic one in depth and a quadratic one in speed. Deep and slow wins in open ocean, and no amount of compute repeals it, because the food source for the processing is the thing that is missing.

But notice the asymmetry, because it is the whole strategic point and the FA framing buries it under "a particular concern for the United States." The physics floor protects the boat that can *use* open ocean. American SSBNs patrol tens of millions of square kilometers of deep Pacific. Chinese SSBNs operate from a Hainan bastion with constrained, already-sensored approaches, and they are noisier. So the ocean goes translucent asymmetrically, against Beijing's deterrent first. The FA authors read the maritime contest as globally hard and therefore stabilizing; I read it as locally *easy in exactly one direction*, which is destabilizing, because from Beijing's chair it looks like American damage-limitation capability arriving on schedule. That perception is the FA authors' own mechanism, turned against their conclusion.

**The chain to war.** This is the cleanest case in the essay because it is happening in the present tense. China spent decades in the most relaxed posture of any nuclear power: a few hundred warheads, no-first-use, warheads de-mated from missiles, no launch-on-warning. That relaxation was affordable *because concealment was trusted*. As sensing eats concealment, China is visibly substituting arithmetic survivability for hidden survivability, the 300-plus new silo fields as a warhead sponge, and moving toward early-warning counterstrike (*yujing fanji*), which is launch-on-warning by another name. Pillar-one erosion is driving LOW adoption in the arsenal least able to absorb a false positive. That is the terminal step: a state that shortens its own launch loop because it no longer trusts its forces to survive is a state one bad sensor-fusion output away from an inadvertent war.

I want to flag one honest complication, because the outline does and the essay is weaker without it. The same observables, silo fields, early-warning constellation, the possible end of de-mated storage, support two readings. One is war-fighting build-up. The other is a country buying itself enough surviving force that it can *afford to ride out* a strike and therefore relax its trigger. At 300 concentrated warheads, use-or-lose pressure is maximal. At 1,000 dispersed and MIRV'd, a remnant survives and waiting becomes affordable for the first time. So part of what looks aggressive may be the stabilization occurring. The US has to act during a window where it cannot tell which film it is watching, and reacting as if it is the war-fighting film, counterforce expansion, defense build-out, confirms Beijing's fear and re-tightens the trigger. That is the assessment spiral applied to force posture, and the right move in the essay is to state the discrimination problem and refuse to resolve it, because Beijing cannot resolve it either, and that is the danger.

## Cluster two: the decision system works (the C3 channel)

The FA authors are strongest on physical decapitation and weakest, I think, on what actually breaks C3, which is not the bunker but the belief.

Take the seductive version first, because it is wrong in an instructive way. If AI cyber becomes good enough, a state might reach an opponent's warheads left of launch, disable them in place the way Stuxnet reached air-gapped centrifuges, and disarm without firing a shot. Call it bloodless counterforce. It fails, and the order of failure matters. The Stuxnet lesson runs backward: the effect announces its own existence and teaches the target to close the seam, and nuclear C3 is the most air-gapped, most idiosyncratic, most actively hardened environment a state runs. Worse, a disarming capability you cannot *verify* is not a capability, it is a gamble with annihilation as the downside, and to rely on it a planner would have to believe it disables enough of the arsenal that the surviving remnant cannot impose unacceptable damage, against a target the adversary continuously modifies and never lets you test at scale. No sane principal bets the homeland on an untested, silent, comprehensive cyber effect.

And here is the part that upgrades this from "cyber is hard" to a genuine taxonomy entry: the danger is not monotonic in capability, it is single-peaked. If the capability were perfect *and both sides knew it*, launch would be futile and self-incriminating, and the incentive flips back toward restraint. But you can never occupy that peak, because demonstrating a perfect disarm requires either using the capability (spending it) or revealing it (letting them patch it). The only regime you can actually be in is the middle one: efficacy real but unverified, where the state that detects an intrusion cannot know whether the disarm has finished, and whose rational response to *any* detected intrusion during a crisis is to launch while it still can. A real capability lives permanently in its own worst regime.

This is why the FA authors' "C3 is too resilient to decapitate" misses. The chain to war does not require decapitation to succeed. It requires the *fear* of it, plus entanglement. Modern NC3 shares sensors, satellites, and networks with conventional command and control (Acton's entanglement; the DF-26 is warhead ambiguity built into hardware). So an intrusion for espionage, or for conventional war-fighting, is indistinguishable *from the target's side* from preparation for a disarming strike. The target reads intent off its own worst case. The failure is not the warheads going dark; it is the commander who believes they are about to and launches from the inside of his own decision loop.

This is where I would put your Mythos point, and I would put it belief-side, because that is both more defensible and more dangerous. The relevant fact for stability is not whether any actor has actually penetrated Chinese NC3. It is that **the Chinese may worry that a frontier system has already pwned their networks**, and cannot prove otherwise, because absence-of-intrusion is exactly the thing you cannot verify. A capability that is cheap to fear and impossible to disprove moves the target's posture whether or not it exists. That asymmetry, cheap to frighten and expensive to reassure, is the through-line this node shares with the assessment spiral and with everything in the next section.

## Cluster three: the human loop and the credibility of restraint (the commitment channel)

The FA authors treat the decision loop mostly as a place where speed causes accidents, which is true and which they handle well. What they miss is the other direction, the one Schelling saw: that under the right doctrine, a state *wants* its decision process to be partly out of its own hands, and AI is the first technology that delivers this for free.

Start with the accident direction, briefly, because it is real. The optimistic constellation math already assumes autonomous engagement, the APS figure of ~1,600 interceptors for a four-missile salvo is conditioned on firing without human verification, so autonomy is priced in and rarely said aloud. The case against it is not sentiment about humans in loops. The human's function was never to approve good detections; it was to notice when a detection was stupid, using context no classifier holds. Petrov's contribution in 1983 was the judgment that nobody starts a war with five missiles. Strip that out, and each side's defensive automation compresses the other side's warning loop, so locally safe automation manufactures demand for automation on the side whose engineering standards you do not control. Perimetr is the existence proof.

Now the harder direction. Schelling's *Strategy of Conflict* makes an argument that reads, in 2026, like it was written about AI. His claim is that it can be rational to make yourself *not fully rational*, to give up control over your own future response, because a threat you cannot climb down from is more credible than one you can. Crucially, he grounds this in the observation that rationality is not an inalienable property of a decision-maker: it is assembled from external parts, "such things as one's hearing aid, the reliability of the mails, the legal system, and the rationality of one's agents and partners." Delegate your response to an agent, and you have altered your own rationality by choosing the agent. That is a description of putting an AI in the loop, written in 1960.

The move this enables is what Schelling calls the threat that leaves something to chance. Brinkmanship, in his words, is "the deliberate creation of a recognizable risk of war, a risk that one does not completely control," and it works only when the outcome is "manifestly somewhat beyond our comprehension and control." Two things follow for AI, and they cut in opposite directions.

First, AI satisfies "manifestly beyond control" for free, and this is the genuinely new point. Schelling notes that a government cannot easily use this tactic because "one does not expect a government to call attention to its own failings" and advertise that it is "even slightly susceptible to disastrous errors in judgment." But AI's flakiness is *common knowledge at the class level*. Everyone has seen the benchmarks, the jailbreaks, the interpretability failures; the unreliability is published. An AI-in-the-loop deterrent manufactures Schelling's "risk that one does not completely control" with zero authentication burden, because the whole world already agrees these systems are erratic in the tail. AI repeals Schelling's constraint that governments cannot credibly advertise their own fallibility.

Second, and this is the part I would push hardest, the demand this creates points at exactly the systems safety wants to prevent. Every alignment desideratum, corrigibility, interruptibility, a reliable off-switch, human-in-the-loop, predictability, is *anti-commitment*. Deterrence logic places a positive bid on incorrigibility-in-the-tail: an agent docile at baseline but uncontrollable if the adversary crosses a line, with the tail keyed to provocation. This is the always/never dilemma (Feaver) rewritten as an alignment problem. Operators historically bias toward "always" (SAC reportedly ran PAL codes of 00000000 for years, per Blair) while civilians impose "never"; the point is that posture designers who want maximum deterrent credibility will spec systems that safety engineers are trying to make impossible. And the machinery cannot deliver what it promises: ML cannot certify *conditional* behavior in unprecedented states, so "dial-able chance keyed to provocation" gets delivered as "unknown chance," and the gap between the two is where the accident lives.

There is a floor this removes that I want to name, because it connects to work Dan has actually done. Human delegated agents were always unreliably committed, they have a survival instinct, they can refuse, they can mutiny. Arkhipov on B-59 was that unreliability functioning as a safety catch: the human veto nobody designed in. A deterrence AI built without a refusal capability is the first delegated agent with no Arkhipov floor. (There is a resonance with the exit affordance in `claude-exit` here: the capacity to refuse is the Arkhipov affordance, and granting it to a deterrence agent turns the always/never dilemma into a live alignment question rather than a doctrinal one. [DAN: keep this or cut as too inside-baseball for Chang.])

The equilibrium I would predict is not doomsday machines and not Perimeters. It is unverifiable *claims* of automation, commitment theater with an uncallable bluff, which is roughly what Russia already does with its periodic "Perimeter is on combat duty" statements. AI arms the theater more than the machine. And the verification failure is symmetric, which is the deepest point and pure Schelling: proving the *absence* of automation is as hard as proving its presence, so reassurance fails the same way deterrence does, and the equilibrium drifts toward ambiguity from both directions. Schelling's model for solving this was ancient: exchange hostages, drink from the same glass, trade spies to transmit authentic information. There is no modern equivalent for decision-architecture inspection. That institutional gap is, I think, a real and unwritten entry in the "what breaks" taxonomy.

## AI as the prize, not the instrument

Everything above treats AI as an *instrument* acting on the arsenals. The other top-level branch is AI as the *prize*, where the pursuit of AI supremacy is itself the thing that lights the fuse, and the mechanism is preventive-war logic on a visible clock (Copeland). Two windows, kept separate because they close on different schedules.

The narrow one is the dependence-asymmetry window, and it runs straight through Taiwan. The "silicon shield" held because Taiwan's fabs were an asset *both* sides needed intact, so China's stake in preservation deterred it. Two things dissolve that. US export controls plus a reshoring push (TSMC Arizona) are meant to reduce US dependence on Taiwanese fab; the same controls force China to indigenize leading-edge fab, because they have no alternative. Now watch the sign flip, and here is the sharpened version of the point. **Once China has domestic leading-edge fab, Taiwan's fab stops being China's liability to lose and becomes China's access-denial lever against the US, especially while US domestic fabs are not yet online.** In that window a rational Beijing becomes *more* cavalier about destroying Taiwan's fabs and engineering base, not less, because the destruction is now mostly denial *against Washington* rather than a cost to *Beijing*. The "broken nest" logic (McKinney and Harris), deny the fab to the attacker, inverts: destroying the nest stops being a deterrent China wants to avoid triggering and becomes a war aim China might actively prefer. The shield does not just weaken; it reverses polarity, and it does so precisely in the interval where China is indigenized and the US is not. Reshoring, meant to stabilize, destabilizes this branch on the way to stabilizing it, and the timing of the two national build-outs is the whole game.

The broad window is the AI-race window generally: compute, energy, and talent leadership, which does not close when fabs are settled. If either side comes to believe the other is about to achieve a durable, compounding, and militarily decisive AI lead, the incentive is to act before the window shuts. This is the scenario the FA authors gesture at with "rapid takeoff" and then set aside as something to "monitor for." I think it deserves to be in the taxonomy as a first-class branch, because unlike the instrument branches it does not require AI to touch a single warhead. It only requires both sides to believe the clock is real.

## The stabilizing column, kept honest

A survey that only breaks things is dishonest, and several of these are real, not decorative. Warning quality genuinely improves: more bands, more geometry, harder to spoof in aggregate, fewer 1995-Norwegian-rocket ambiguities. Clarification speed is a stability resource, the 1963 Hotline and 1987 NRRCs exist for exactly that reason, and a machine-speed channel that opens automatically on an ambiguous event ("we observe a launch profile from your territory; characterize it in sixty seconds") is a genuinely new de-escalation mechanism, with automated translation removing a failure mode the 1963 negotiators worried about explicitly. Decision support done right buys time via earlier characterization, *if* doctrine banks the time instead of spending it. The catch on every one of these is the same: the channel's value is its authenticated provenance, and generative AI degrades authentication while enabling the channel. That is the organizing asymmetry of the whole essay. AI compresses the escalation timeline and the clarification timeline with the same hand, and doctrine chooses which compression binds.

## In the limit: what breaks, and what the FA logic misses

To the prompt's actual question. The FA logic applies cleanly in bin one and only there. Their missile-defense argument is right in the limit, because the limit is kinematic. Their C3 argument is right about *decapitation* and wrong about *breakdown*, because it prices the bunker and not the belief, and belief is what the entanglement-plus-ambiguity chain runs on. Their targeting argument treats the armor as constant and reads a globally-hard contest as stabilizing, when the contest is locally easy against China first, which is destabilizing through their own perception mechanism. And their framing has no room at all for AI-as-prize, where no warhead is touched and the war starts over the clock.

So the load-bearing assumptions, in order of fragility: survivable mobile forces (failing now, on land, asymmetrically against China); trustworthy command systems (failing through fear, not decapitation); the human decision window and the credibility of restraint (failing by doctrine choice, and doctrine is endogenous to the vulnerability AI is manufacturing); and the infeasibility of defense (holding, and likely to hold in the limit). The FA authors are calibrated on the last and over-comfortable on the first three, and the reason is structural: their concession that perception is what matters is exactly right, and they use it to file every erosion under "manageable arms-race risk" instead of following it to the place where a perception, cheap to create and impossible to disprove, shortens a real arsenal's launch loop.

The single sentence I would leave with the grader: AI has no intrinsic sign for stability. It compresses timelines on both the escalation and the de-escalation side, and posture, not physics, decides which compression binds. The FA article is a study of the physics. This is a study of the posture.

## Update criteria

What would flip the major claims. A demonstrated, countermeasure-robust midcourse discrimination capability against anti-simulation decoys, or an order-of-magnitude drop in interceptor unit mass, would reopen the defense channel; I consider both physics-blocked and would be genuinely surprised. A wartime demonstration of kill-chain *closure* against relocating TELs against a competent, decoy-using force (as opposed to the sensing layer, which is demonstrated) would confirm the targeting channel; that is the one to watch, and the Israel-Iran June 2025 TEL campaign is the nearest real test [DAN: fact-check kill fractions before citing]. A nuclear state explicitly citing cyber-vulnerability as the rationale for launch-on-warning would confirm the C3 node. Acoustic-ML detection ranges published or leaked at levels that defeat the deep-and-slow evasion would break the SSBN physics claim; I expect they will not, but I have not run the serious acoustic version and flag it as the biggest hole. And on the prize branch: Chinese leading-edge indigenization milestones crossing before US domestic fab capacity comes online would open the dependence-asymmetry window in earnest, and is trackable in the open.
