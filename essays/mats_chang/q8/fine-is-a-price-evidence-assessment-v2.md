# "A Fine Is a Price": An Evidence Assessment of the Credibility Controversy (v2, corrected)

*Revision note (v2): corrects a compression error in v1, which conflated the original 1998 study with the 2005 "second study" in describing the offered-access / lost-names episode. The corrected two-study sequence is now given explicitly, with the source ambiguity flagged.*

## TL;DR
- The critique the user remembers is real and identifiable: it originates with Israeli game theorist **Ariel Rubinstein** (2005–2006), was amplified by **Andrew Gelman's blog quoting data-sleuth Nick Brown** (Sept 2024), and centers on institutional implausibility, a data-collection procedure Rubinstein calls unreliable, and lost participant contact information — but there is **no proof of fabrication**, no retraction, and no misconduct finding.
- The best-verified facts are: (1) Rubinstein was a pre-publication critic who found the setup implausible and was refused access to the original study's teachers; (2) Gneezy reported he had **lost the names** of the kindergarten teachers — most plausibly those of the original 1998 study, since access to the *second* (2005) study's daycare managers was separately offered; (3) the effect **fails to replicate** in the one published robustness study (Metcalf et al. 2020, which states in its abstract, "Our survey results do not replicate the original findings"). The specific claim that "someone contacted the daycares and they had no record" is **not established** — no sourced account of anyone reaching the Haifa daycares exists.
- Current scholarly status: the paper is **disputed but standing** — never retracted or corrected, cited **more than 2,000 times** (per the authors of the 2020 replication). Careful commentators (Gelman, Rubinstein) treat it as *unreliable/unverifiable* rather than *proven fraudulent*. Gneezy's adjacency to the Gino and Ariely fabrication scandals is guilt-by-association, not evidence against him personally.

## Key Findings

**The paper.** Uri Gneezy (then Technion–Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa; now UC San Diego) and Aldo Rustichini (then CentER, Tilburg University; now University of Minnesota), "A Fine Is a Price," *Journal of Legal Studies* 29(1), January 2000, pp. 1–17 (DOI 10.1086/468061). The byline confirms these affiliations verbatim: "Technion—Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa, Israel, and CentER for Economic Research, Tilburg University, The Netherlands, respectively." It was a field study conducted, per the paper, "in 10 private day-care centers in the city of Haifa from January to June 1998. All of these centers are located in the same part of town." Six treatment centers introduced a fine — described in the CORE Econ textbook as going "from zero to ten Israeli shekels (about $3 at the time)" for pickups more than 10 minutes late, per child — at the start of week 5; four were controls. The fine was removed at the start of week 17; the study ran 20 weeks total. Per CORE Econ, "after the fine was introduced, the frequency of late pickups doubled," with late-pickup counts in treatment centers rising from roughly 7–11 to roughly 16–21, and the increase did **not** reverse after the fine's removal. The authors interpreted this as a fine "completing an incomplete contract" and crowding out a social norm ("once a commodity, always a commodity"). It became a signature anecdote of behavioral economics — Chapter 1 of *Freakonomics*, Dan Ariely's *Predictably Irrational*, and Camerer/Loewenstein/Rabin's *Advances in Behavioral Economics*.

**The critique's true origin: Ariel Rubinstein, not a blog.** The "that's not how Israel works" argument is Rubinstein's, delivered as a discussant at the Econometric Society World Congress (Aug 20, 2005) and published in his written discussion of Camerer's and Rabin's behavioral-economics papers (and echoed in "Dilemmas of an Economic Theorist," *Econometrica* 2006). His verbatim objections: "I know Israel quite well. It is a country where rules are rarely enforced… It is hard for me to believe that teachers would really fine a parent who is ten minutes late… it is impossible for me to imagine that Israeli teachers would have kept even roughly accurate records of late arrivals with noisy parents crowding around the entrance." On data collection: "an RA went to the schools once a week and asked the assistant teacher who was late the previous week. There was no attempt to control the accuracy of the RA's records. Oddly, I was not allowed to talk with the teachers." Rubinstein states he raised these concerns before publication and advised the authors to withdraw or delay the paper; they declined ("Eventually, the paper was published as is").

**The "lost the list" fact — corrected sequence.** This is the most concrete element of the controversy, and it is not internet rumor — it comes from Rubinstein's own published account. His full sentence: "Although the authors report that they are still working on the data and that they have asked the teachers for permission to be interviewed by me, the author who directed the experiment has just informed me that he has lost the names of the kindergarten teachers who participated in the experiment." This is the kernel of the "couldn't produce the data" claim. **Which experiment's names were lost is ambiguous in the source.** Rubinstein writes only "the experiment." The charitable reading — and the more plausible one — is that the lost names belong to the original 1998 study (seven years old by then, predating data-preservation norms), while the access on offer was to the *second* study's daycare managers: the second-study draft states the authors made "changes as suggested by Rubinstein, most notably, we got the approval of the managers of the daycares that he will call them and ask about the study." Under that reading there is no self-contradiction by Gneezy — just an original study that can no longer be independently verified. The uncharitable reading — access offered and names "lost" for the same study — is the one Rubinstein's deadpan "Although…" construction invites, but the text does not establish it. **Important distinction either way:** what was lost is a list of *participant contacts*, not the tabulated data (the raw weekly late-pickup counts are printed in Table 1 of the published paper).

**Gneezy & Rustichini's response.** They published a rebuttal ("Reply to Ariel Rubinstein's critique of 'A fine is a price'"). Key claims: the NIS 10 fine was an official option offered by the private kindergarten organization the daycares belonged to ("They all belong to a private kindergarten organization, which recommends to the managers either to give no fine or a NIS 10 fine"); managers and assistants already recorded latecomers as routine practice (fines were added to the monthly bill); the RA collected these records every Friday; and neither the managers nor the RA knew the research hypothesis. They also state they ran a follow-up ("second") daycare study that "fully replicated" the original; per that study's draft, the verification offer was specific to the *second* study's daycare managers, not the original 1998 teachers.

**The "second study" was never formally published.** The replication Gneezy and Rustichini cite ("The Second Day-Care Center Study," dated September 2005; 10 daycares, 18 weeks, fine introduced week 4 and removed week 16, four controls) exists only as an unpublished working-paper draft hosted on Rubinstein's own website (arielrubinstein.tau.ac.il/papers/WC05/GR1.pdf). No peer-reviewed journal version appears in Crossref, Scopus, Semantic Scholar, or the University of Minnesota publication database. A commenter on Gelman's post confirmed this independently: the paper "looks like it was never published aside from this rough draft posted on rubinstein's website." So the authors' main empirical defense against Rubinstein is itself an unrefereed manuscript — and the one study for which third-party verification was explicitly offered is the one that never entered the peer-reviewed record.

**The published replication failed.** Metcalf, Satterthwaite, Dillbary & Stoddard, "Is a Fine Still a Price? Replication as Robustness in Empirical Legal Studies," 63 *International Review of Law and Economics* 105906 (2020) (DOI 10.1016/j.irle.2020.105906; SSRN 3659604, posted Aug 4, 2020), ran MTurk vignette experiments in both a daycare and a tax-compliance frame. Their abstract states plainly: "Our survey results do not replicate the original findings." Fines *reduced* anticipated non-compliance and showed "very little evidence of fines crowding out social motivations," with effects that were transitory. **Caveat:** this is a survey/vignette study, not a field replication, so it tests the *generality* of the effect rather than directly refuting the original field result.

**The Gelman/Nick Brown amplification (2024).** On September 14, 2024, Andrew Gelman's "Statistical Modeling, Causal Inference, and Social Science" ran a post (on Freakonomics and academic-fraud incentives) quoting psychologist/data-sleuth **Nick Brown** by name. Brown wrote: "I have difficulty in believing that this study took place exactly as described; for example, the number of children in each centre appears to remain exactly the same throughout the 20 weeks of the study, with no mention of any new arrivals, dropouts, or days off due to illness or any other reason." Brown then cited Rubinstein and recounted the "lost the list" episode. Notably, a commenter on Gelman's own post disputed Brown's reading — "I'm not sure why Nick Brown said the number of children was constant over the time period – I did not see that assumption in the paper. All I saw was a single value for the number of children and then the average number of parents late over time" — and Gelman added a P.S. saying he had not investigated this particular case in detail. This is very likely the "extensive blog post + Twitter controversy" the user recalls, though its substance traces back to Rubinstein.

## Details

**Institutional-plausibility claims — assessment.** The paper addresses several of Rubinstein's points internally: daycares held a maximum of 35 children; the fee was NIS 1,400/month (~$380); the NIS 10 fine is described as "small but not insignificant" (vs. NIS 75 for illegal parking, NIS 1,000 for running a red light); fines were added to the monthly bill paid to the owner, not the teacher. Gneezy's rebuttal adds that all centers belonged to a single private kindergarten organization that recommended either no fine or a NIS 10 fine. **Verdict:** the described setup is internally coherent, and the authors have a specific, non-evasive answer for each institutional objection. Rubinstein's "that's not how Israel works" is an expert intuition/prior about enforcement culture, not documented evidence that the setup was fabricated. It is a legitimate plausibility concern, not proof.

**Did anyone contact the daycares and find "no record"?** This specific claim, as the user remembers it, is **not supported** by the primary sources. What is documented is a two-study sequence: Rubinstein asked to interview the original study's teachers and was refused ("Oddly, I was not allowed to talk with the teachers"); when the authors ran the second study in 2005, they offered him contact with *that* study's daycare managers; and Gneezy separately reported having lost the names of the kindergarten teachers from "the experiment" — unspecified in the source, but most plausibly the original 1998 one. There is no sourced account of anyone reaching the specific Haifa daycares and being told the study never happened, and no sourced account of whether Rubinstein ever exercised the second-study access offer. The user's memory most likely compresses "Gneezy lost the original contact list / Rubinstein was refused access" into "the daycares had no record." The net effect, however it is parsed: **no independent party is documented to have verified either experiment with its primary participants.**

**Citation impact.** Heavily cited. The authors of the 2020 replication state the paper has been cited "more than 2000 times including in popular non-fiction for general audiences." Structured databases give a confirmed floor: Semantic Scholar ~1,941, Scopus 1,336, Crossref 1,308 (Google Scholar is typically substantially higher but was not directly verified here). Its SSRN page (abstract 180117) records 22,989 abstract views. It anchors the "motivational crowding-out" literature (Frey/Jegen; Bénabou/Tirole), fines-and-fees policy debates, and contract-design arguments.

**Gneezy's broader track record — careful distinctions.**
- *Gino scandal (2023):* Gneezy is **not** among the four Gino papers with alleged fabricated data, and is not a defendant or accused party. The Data Colada trio (Simonsohn, Simmons, Nelson) and the Many Co-Authors Project (138 Gino papers, 143 co-authors) concern Francesca Gino, not Gneezy. Any linkage is topical (behavioral economics), not evidentiary.
- *Ariely adjacency:* Gneezy co-authored "Large Stakes and Big Mistakes" (Ariely, Gneezy, Loewenstein, Mazar, *Review of Economic Studies* 76(2), 2009), whose India experiments have drawn scrutiny; Dan Ariely and Nina Mazar have separately been at the center of the 2021 "signing-at-the-top" honesty-study fraud allegations (also via Data Colada). Again, Gneezy is a co-author on adjacent work, **not** personally accused of fabrication.
- The only direct, on-the-record accusation against Gneezy specifically is Rubinstein's — and Rubinstein "all but accused" rather than formally charging misconduct; the EconJobRumors framing ("accused gneezy of lying") overstates the formal record. Rubinstein himself explicitly disclaims doubt about the *conclusion* ("I do not doubt the truth and originality of the conclusion that introducing a small fine might be counter productive") while doubting whether it "was indeed confirmed experimentally in Haifa kindergartens."

## Recommendations

1. **Treat "A Fine Is a Price" as a disputed-but-standing single field study, not settled fact.** Cite it as an illustrative *hypothesis* about crowding-out, always paired with (a) Rubinstein's data-reliability critique and (b) the Metcalf et al. (2020) non-replication. Do not present the doubling effect as a robust, replicated finding.
2. **Do not repeat the "the daycares had no record / it was fabricated" claim as fact.** The defensible statements are narrower: the data-collection procedure was informal and unaudited; the authors' own follow-up "replication" was never peer-reviewed; and the original study's teacher contacts were reported lost, precluding independent verification of the 1998 experiment.
3. **For the crowding-out phenomenon more broadly, rely on the wider literature, not this one study.** Lab and field evidence for motivation crowding-out exists (e.g., Heyman & Ariely 2004 — which Rubinstein himself cites approvingly; the Frey & Jegen review), but is heterogeneous; the daycare result sits at the vivid-anecdote end, not the robust-evidence end.
4. **Benchmarks that would change this assessment:** a *published, peer-reviewed* field replication (would strengthen the finding); a formal correction/retraction or an institutional inquiry (would move it toward "discredited"); or recovery and independent audit of the original records (would resolve the reliability question either way). None of these has occurred as of this writing.

## Caveats
- **"Failed to replicate / data unavailable" ≠ "fabricated."** No source establishes fabrication. The evidence supports "unverifiable and non-robust," which is a weaker and materially different claim than fraud.
- Strength of the three specific user-recalled claims: (1) *institutional implausibility* — a sincere expert intuition (Rubinstein) met by the authors' point-by-point rebuttal on the record; moderate and contested. (2) *daycares had no record* — **not supported as stated**; the real facts are a lost contact list (most plausibly for the original study), refused access during the original study, and an unexercised-or-undocumented access offer for the second. (3) *Gneezy couldn't produce data* — partially true, and weaker than it sounds: what was lost was contact information for teachers of a then-seven-year-old field study, not the tabulated data — the raw weekly pickup counts are printed in the published paper (Table 1) — and there is no documented formal data request that he refused.
- **Source ambiguity flag:** Rubinstein's "lost the names" sentence does not specify which experiment; both this document's charitable reading (original study) and the uncharitable one (second study) are consistent with his wording. The charitable reading is favored here because the second-study draft explicitly documents manager approval for contact.
- The Metcalf et al. non-replication is a vignette/survey, not a field study; it limits but does not annihilate the original result.
- Rubinstein is a longtime, pointed critic of behavioral economics generally; his critique should be weighted as expert but not disinterested.
- The exact Google Scholar citation count was not directly verified; the "more than 2,000" figure comes from the 2020 replication authors, and structured-database figures (~1,300–1,900) are the confirmed floor.
- Primary sources for the controversy: Rubinstein's "Discussion of 'Behavioral Economics'" (arielrubinstein.tau.ac.il/papers/behavioral-economics.pdf) and "Dilemmas of an Economic Theorist" (*Econometrica* 2006); Gneezy & Rustichini's "Reply to Ariel Rubinstein's critique" (arielrubinstein.tau.ac.il/papers/WC05/GR.pdf) and "The Second Day-Care Center Study" (arielrubinstein.tau.ac.il/papers/WC05/GR1.pdf); Andrew Gelman's post of Sept 14, 2024 (statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu); Metcalf, Satterthwaite, Dillbary & Stoddard (2020, *IRLE* 63:105906 / SSRN 3659604); and Bryan Caplan's 2005 EconLog write-up "Revenge of the Game Theorist," which first popularized Rubinstein's critique online.
