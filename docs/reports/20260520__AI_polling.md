# US Public Opinion on Artificial Intelligence in 2026: A Barometer for Policy Briefings

## TL;DR
- **Through mid-May 2026, US public sentiment on AI is the worst it has ever been in any survey series we track**: roughly half of Americans view AI negatively vs. roughly a quarter positively (NBC News, Feb 27–Mar 3, 2026: 46% negative / 26% positive; net –20); 71% say AI is moving "too fast" (Economist/YouGov, May 9–11, 2026); 80% are very or somewhat concerned about AI (Quinnipiac, March 19–23, 2026); and 57% say the risks of AI outweigh the benefits (NBC News, Feb–Mar 2026).
- **Support for AI regulation is the strongest bipartisan consensus in US tech policy**: a Vanderbilt Unity poll (Feb 20–23, 2026) found 61% of Republicans and 56% of Democrats favor regulating AI; AIPI's January–February 2026 battleground polls found 81% support "regulatory guardrails" over no regulation in both competitive House districts and Senate states; the Institute for Family Studies' January 2026 N=6,200 survey found voters oppose federal preemption of state AI laws by roughly 3-to-1, including a plurality of Trump voters, and prefer protecting children from AI harms over advancing the AI industry by a 9-to-1 margin.
- **But salience is still low.** Through the first five months of 2026, no major "most important problem" poll (Gallup, Harvard CAPS-Harris) shows AI as a volunteered top-tier issue — voters continue to name inflation, immigration, the Iran war, and the economy. The "AI is less popular than Nixon on the day he resigned" framing is **overstated** as a literal popularity claim (Nixon's Gallup approval in early August 1974 was 24%, against disapproval in the mid-60s — a net of roughly –42; AI's worst 2026 net favorability is about –20), but it is **directionally accurate as a favorability claim**: in the NBC News February–March 2026 poll, only the Democratic Party and Iran scored worse than AI on a 14-item favorability battery.

## Key Findings

1. **AI sentiment deteriorated meaningfully between mid-2025 and Q1 2026.** The Pew anchor — "more concerned than excited" — climbed from 37% (2021) to 50% (June 2025). YouGov tracking shows negative views of AI have risen from 34% in May 20–23, 2023 (when 34% told the Economist/YouGov poll AI's societal impact would be "bad" vs. 19% "good") to just over 50% by May 2026. Quinnipiac (March 19–23, 2026) shows 80% concerned and 55% saying AI does more harm than good in daily life, an 11-point worsening since April 2025.

2. **Regulation enjoys super-majority, bipartisan support.** Across pollsters with very different ideological priors — Vanderbilt Unity (academic), AIPI (AI-safety advocacy), Common Sense Media/Echelon (kids-safety advocacy), IFS/YouGov (family-policy advocacy), Ipsos, Pew — supermajorities want federal AI regulation. The lowest topline we observed in 2026 is the Vanderbilt 56–61% (Feb 2026). The highest are AIPI's 81% (Jan–Feb 2026 battleground) and Common Sense Media's 70% (May 2025).

3. **Federal preemption of state AI laws is unpopular, including among Trump voters.** This is robust across three IFS/YouGov polls (June 2025, September 2025, November 2025, repeated again in January 2026 N=6,200). In the November 2025 IFS/YouGov poll, voters opposed an NDAA preemption provision by a 3-to-1 margin (57% oppose / 19% support). Trump voters split 43% oppose / 25% support / 32% unsure; Harris voters split 70% oppose / 14% support. Younger voters (18–34) opposed the measure 61–22, despite being the highest AI users.

4. **Pre-deployment safety evaluation (the model in the Hawley-Blumenthal Artificial Intelligence Risk Evaluation Act, S. 2938, introduced September 29, 2025, which would create an Advanced AI Evaluation Program at the Department of Energy and prohibit deployment of advanced AI systems until developers comply with its requirements) is one of the most popular AI policies ever measured.** AIPI's January 2026 House battleground poll: 76% support / 10% oppose requiring Congress-mandated pre-release safety tests. AIPI's February 2026 Senate battleground poll: 73% support / 9% oppose. IFS January 2026: by roughly 20-to-1, Americans support White House safety-testing of AI before deployment, with strong majorities supporting safety pre-evaluation for all 11 specific harms tested.

5. **AGI/superintelligence specifically commands restrictionist majorities.** FLI/YouGov October 2025 (N=2,000): 64% say superhuman AI should not be developed until provably safe and controllable, or should never be developed at all; only 5% say develop it "as quickly as possible." AIPI February 2026 Senate poll: 82% agree "we should not build AI systems smarter than humans without knowing how to control them"; 88% want a guaranteed "off switch" for the most powerful AI; 84% want Congress to act on loss-of-control risk.

6. **Salience is low but the issue is "up for grabs."** AI does not yet appear in volunteered "most important problem" polling from Gallup (Feb and April 2026) or Harvard CAPS-Harris (Jan, Feb, April 2026). However, NBC News pollster Bill McInturff explicitly characterizes AI as "up for grabs" — neither party owns it (20% R / 19% D / 33% neither / 24% same).

7. **Age, party, and gender divides are real but smaller than the topline gap.** Younger voters use AI more but are not more positive about it — Gallup's February–March 2026 Gen Z survey shows hopefulness about AI at just 18% (–9 pts YoY) and excitement at 22% (–14 pts YoY); anger about AI rose 9 pts to 31%. NBC News February–March 2026: net AI favorability is –44 among 18–34s. Partisan gaps are small at the level of "is AI good or bad" but widen sharply on "which firms do you trust" — the 2026 Axios Harris Poll 100 shows Republicans' opinion of AI companies improving in 2025–2026 while Democrats' worsened.

8. **The "AI is less popular than Nixon" claim is overstated but directionally true on favorability batteries.** AI's net favorability of –20 in NBC News (Feb–Mar 2026) is bad but not historically catastrophic; it is in the same range as ICE (–18) and Trump (–12). It is better than the Democratic Party (–22) and Iran (–53). Nixon's Gallup approval the week he resigned (August 1974) was 24%, with disapproval in the mid-60s — a net of roughly –42. AI is well above that floor.

## Details

### 1. Overall sentiment: concern, distrust, and growing opposition

**The Pew "concerned vs. excited" trendline (the most-cited single AI sentiment measure).** Pew has tracked this question annually since 2021. The most recent Pew fielding is **June 9–15, 2025, N=5,023** (American Trends Panel; probability-based). 50% of US adults are more concerned than excited about increased use of AI in daily life; 10% more excited than concerned; 38% equally concerned and excited. That 50% is up from 37% in 2021 — a 13-point worsening over four years. Pew has not yet released a 2026 wave of this exact question; the March 12, 2026 Pew short-read is a compilation of prior surveys. Pew's November 6, 2025 short-read separately confirms that Republicans and Democrats are now equally concerned (50% vs. 51%) — concern has converged across parties, with Democrats' concern rising and Republicans' falling slightly since 2023.

**Economist/YouGov May 9–11, 2026 (N=1,549 US adults; weighted).** 71% say AI is moving "too fast"; 27% "about right"; 2% "too slow." Partisan breakdown: Democrats 77%, Independents 69%, Republicans 68% say "too fast." Age breakdown: 64% of 18-29s, 72% of 45-64s, 79% of 65+ say "too fast." Twice as many Americans are AI pessimists as optimists. Counterintuitive finding: 33% of adults under 30 say AI is moving "about right" — the highest of any age group — even though their majority view is still "too fast."

**Quinnipiac March 19–23, 2026 (N=1,397 US adults; live-interviewer RDD).** Of the surveys we examined in 2026, Quinnipiac shows the deepest concern levels:
- 80% are very (37%) or somewhat (43%) concerned about AI; 18% are not very or not at all concerned.
- 76% trust AI rarely or only sometimes; 21% trust AI most or almost all the time.
- 55% say AI will do more harm than good in their day-to-day lives (up 11 points from April 2025); 33% more good than harm.
- 6% are "very excited" about AI; 62% are "not so excited" or "not at all excited."
- 70% believe AI will lead to fewer job opportunities; among Gen Z respondents (born 1997–2008), 81% say jobs will suffer.
- 64% say AI will do more harm than good in education; 45–43 split on healthcare.
- Only 30% of employed Americans are concerned AI will obsolete their own job — though that is up from 21% a year earlier.

**NBC News (Hart Research / Public Opinion Strategies) Feb 27 – March 3, 2026 (N=1,000 RV).** 46% negative view of AI, 26% positive, net –20. 57% say risks of AI outweigh benefits; 34% say benefits outweigh risks. On handling AI: 20% Republicans better, 19% Democrats better, 33% "neither," 24% "about the same" — the lowest combined party-ownership of any issue NBC tested.

**YouGov tracker (multiple 2026 waves).**
- May 15–18, 2026 (N=1,549): negative views of AI now exceed 50%, up from 34% in May 2023.
- February 13–16, 2026: 58% don't trust AI much or at all; 35% trust it a fair amount or a great deal. 45% say AI will have a negative overall effect on the economy vs. 16% positive.
- February 2026 YouGov reported by Roll Call: 63% of Americans think AI will lead to fewer jobs.

**Fox News (Beacon Research / Shaw & Co.) March 20–23, 2026 (N=1,001 RV).** 66% concerned about AI (up from 63% in December 2025 and 56% in 2023). 59% think AI will eliminate more jobs than create over the next 5 years; 7% think the reverse.

**Ipsos (March 2026 Consumer Tracker).** Americans rank AI as having a greater societal impact than cybersecurity, advanced phones, electric vehicles, GLP-1s, or robots. Only 40% agree the potential benefits of AI outweigh job-loss potential. Nearly three in four want government action to prevent AI-driven job loss.

**Vanderbilt Unity Poll Feb 20–23, 2026 (N=1,032 US adults via SSRS; ±3.5%).** 61% of Republicans and 56% of Democrats favor regulating AI. Bipartisan agreement across ages (56% of 18–29s; 58% of 65+).

**AIPI (AI Policy Institute / YouGov) battleground polling, January–February 2026.** Two surveys focused on competitive 2026 districts and states.
- House battleground (Jan 17–21, 2026; N=2,830 likely voters across 10 swing districts — IA-03, PA-07, VA-01, CA-48, NY-17, MI-07, WI-03, CO-08, AZ-06, NJ-07; ±2.4%): 65% more concerned than excited about AI vs. 19% more excited; 81% support "regulatory guardrails" / 8% "no regulation"; 76% support pre-release safety testing required by Congress (10% oppose); 68% support liability for AI companies for serious harms (15% oppose); 71% support a right to human review of AI decisions.
- Senate battleground (Feb 2–8, 2026; N=3,969 likely voters across 8 swing states — AK, ME, NC, OH, IA, GA, MI, MN; ±2.1%): 84% agree Congress should ensure loss-of-control risk is addressed; 82% agree we should not build AI smarter than humans without knowing how to control them; 88% want a guaranteed "off switch" on the most powerful AI; 73% support required pre-release safety testing; 65% support holding AI companies legally liable for serious harms; 81% prefer "regulatory guardrails" over "no regulation"; 62%–11% (6-to-1) margin for Musk's "build AI infrastructure while managing catastrophic risks" over Andreessen's "hands-off" approach.

**FLI/YouGov October 2025 (N=2,000; field 29 Sept – 5 Oct 2025).** Late-2025 anchor confirmed: 64% say superhuman AI should not be developed until proven safe and controllable, or should never be developed; only 5% say develop expert-level or superhuman AI "as quickly as possible." 73% support "slow, heavily regulated" AI development; 64% want an immediate pause on advanced AI development until proven safe.

**Institute for Family Studies "Artificial Politics of Artificial Intelligence" (December 2025 fielding; published January 2026; N=6,200 with oversamples in FL/KY/LA/TN/UT/MI).** 71% of Americans hold a negative view of AI's effect on society (36% "big threat," 35% "concerning"); only 29% positive (21% "intriguing," 8% "exciting"). 80% want Congress to hold AI companies legally liable for harms to children. 62% say state governments should be free to regulate AI; only 38% support federal preemption. 76% say the federal government should require employers to report when layoffs are caused by AI deployment. From a randomized vote-shift experiment: "There are absolutely no pro-AI positions that are winners for elected officials." Federal preemption is a strong net negative for Republican candidates in red states; the more conservative the state, the worse it polls.

### 2. Salience: AI is the dog that hasn't barked

Through mid-May 2026, AI **does not appear** as a volunteered top-tier issue in any of the standard "most important problem" series we examined:
- **Gallup** "most important problem" polls (Feb 2–16, 2026, N=1,001; April 1–15, 2026, N=1,001): Government/poor leadership leads (~29–32%); immigration 11–20%; economy/inflation aggregate 19–32%; Middle East war 8%; AI is not reported as a top category.
- **Harvard CAPS-Harris** monthly polls (Jan 28–29, Feb 25–26, April 23–26, 2026; N≈2,000–2,745): The top four issues are consistently inflation, immigration, the economy, and healthcare. The U.S.–Iran conflict has risen to a top-5 issue since the February 2026 strikes (cited by 20% in the April wave). AI is not in the top five.
- **Reuters/Ipsos Core Political (Feb 2026)**: a "fragmented landscape" of issues with no single dominant concern; inflation tops the list. AI is not separately listed.
- **Economist/YouGov** asks "most important political issue" weekly. Across 2026, inflation has been the consistent top issue; AI is not a separate option in their standard battery.

When prompted on AI specifically, however, voters express intense concern. NBC News pollster Bill McInturff explicitly characterized AI as "up for grabs" politically — neither party owns it.

**Vote-relevance for the 2026 midterms (IFS January 2026 vote-shift experiment, N=6,200):**
- Candidates supporting AI company liability for harms to children gain net support across all six oversampled states and both parties' bases — strongest among Trump voters in red states and Harris voters everywhere.
- Candidates supporting federal preemption of state AI laws lose net support among Republican voters in red states (most pronounced in MI and LA); roughly neutral effect among Republicans in blue/purple states.
- Candidates supporting accelerated permitting for AI data centers lose net Democratic support; modestly negative among Republicans in red states.

**Yale Youth Poll (Spring 2026)**: For voters aged 18–22 and 23–29, the most important issues are cost of living/affordability, corruption, democracy, healthcare, and housing — AI is not a top-tier issue, though young voters are more concerned than the general public about AI's environmental impact.

**Bottom line on salience:** AI is high-concern, low-salience. Voters are deeply worried when asked, but rarely volunteer it as their top voting issue. This is the gap policy advocates need to understand — the latent intensity is there, but the issue does not currently drive vote choice in the way inflation, immigration, or the Iran war do.

### 3. Age breakdowns

**The counterintuitive finding: younger AI users are NOT more positive — they're more ambivalent and increasingly angry.**

**Gallup / Walton / GSV "Voices of Gen Z" Feb 24 – Mar 4, 2026 (N=1,572 ages 14–29; probability-based Gallup Panel; ±3.6%).**
- Excitement about AI dropped from 36% (2025) to 22% (2026), a 14-point decline.
- Hopefulness fell from 27% to 18%, a 9-point decline.
- Anger rose from 22% to 31%, a 9-point increase.
- Anxiety remained at 42%; curiosity is the most-felt emotion at 49%.
- AI usage is plateauing: 51% use AI weekly or more (same as 2025); 19% never use it.
- Only 18% of 14–29s feel hopeful about AI.
- Among daily users: 69% curious, 44% excited, 38% hopeful (still well below curiosity).
- Among non-users: 60% anxious, 59% angry.

**Economist/YouGov May 9–11, 2026 age breakdown on "AI moving too fast":**
- 18–29: 64% too fast
- 45–64: 72% too fast
- 65+: 79% too fast

Concern about pace rises with age, but a majority of every age group says AI is moving too fast.

**Quinnipiac March 2026 (Gen Z–specific findings):**
- 78% of Gen Z very or somewhat concerned about AI; 81% say AI will reduce job opportunities (vs. 66% of Boomers; 57% of Silent Generation).
- Younger Americans report the highest AI familiarity but the lowest labor-market optimism. As Tamilla Triantoro, Ph.D., Associate Professor of Business Analytics and Information Systems at Quinnipiac University's School of Business, put it in the March 30, 2026 release: "AI fluency and optimism here are moving in opposite directions."

**NBC News Feb–Mar 2026:**
- Net AI favorability is –44 among voters 18–34 (the worst age cohort).
- Net AI favorability is –41 among women 18–49.
- Older men and upper-income voters are net positive.

**Pew (March 2025 wave, the most recent age-detailed Pew on AI):**
- 18–29s use AI tools much more (76% ever-used vs. 51% of older Americans; 50% weekly+ vs. 23%).
- But younger adults are only marginally more excited than older adults; majorities of every age group are wary of AI's effects on creativity and relationships.

**IFS Nov 2025 / IFS Dec 2025 / IFS Jan 2026 preemption polling:**
- 18–34s are the strongest opponents of federal preemption (61% oppose / 22% support in November 2025).
- This finding — younger voters most use AI and most oppose deregulation — is robust across IFS's three preemption polls.

**Implication for briefings:** The "kids are AI natives, so they'll accept it" story is wrong. The cohort that uses AI most is also the angriest and most worried about labor-market consequences. Their support for regulation is at least as strong as the general public's.

### 4. Party breakdowns

**The big picture: AI regulation is not a partisan issue in the standard sense.** Multiple 2026 polls find Republican–Democrat gaps in the single digits on most regulation questions, with both parties strongly favoring guardrails.

**Pew November 2025 short-read on partisan convergence:**
- 50% of Republicans (and R-leaners) and 51% of Democrats (and D-leaners) say they are more concerned than excited about AI — essentially identical.
- This is a notable shift: in 2023, Republicans were more concerned than Democrats. Republican concern has fallen 9 points since 2023 while Democratic concern has steadily risen.

**Economist/YouGov May 9–11, 2026:**
- 77% of Democrats, 69% of Independents, 68% of Republicans say AI is moving too fast.

**Vanderbilt Unity Poll Feb 2026:**
- 61% of Republicans and 56% of Democrats favor regulating AI (Republicans slightly more pro-regulation in this fielding).

**Axios Harris Poll 100 (released May 19, 2026):**
- 44% of Republicans say their opinion of AI has become more positive in the past year vs. 35% of Democrats. AI companies as a group have shifted right in reputation between 2024 and 2026 (TikTok, Nvidia, Meta, X all show widening Republican-favored gaps; Anthropic is the exception, with a 1-point gap).
- Republicans are more positive toward AI firms but not less supportive of regulation — these are separate dimensions.

**NBC News Feb–Mar 2026 on AI favorability by party:**
- Republicans: 33% positive / 33% negative (even split).
- Independents: 26% positive / 48% negative.
- Democrats: 20% positive / 56% negative.
- This is the rare issue where Democrats hold the more negative view than Republicans.

**Federal preemption — the cleanest partisan story in 2026 AI polling.**
- **IFS/YouGov November 2025 (and replicated in IFS December 2025 / January 2026 N=6,200):** Voters oppose AI preemption in the NDAA by 3-to-1 (57% oppose / 19% support).
  - Trump voters: 43% oppose / 25% support / 32% unsure (net -18).
  - Harris voters: 70% oppose / 14% support (net -56).
- The pattern is "asymmetric bipartisan opposition" — Democrats overwhelmingly opposed, Republicans modestly opposed.
- IFS's January 2026 vote-shift experiment found preemption is electorally damaging for Republican candidates in **red** states — Republican voters in places like Louisiana and Michigan said they would be less likely to vote for a Republican who supported preemption. In blue states, Republican voters were more receptive to candidates supporting preemption (likely because they prefer the federal government to "blue-state regulation").

**MAGA vs. traditional Republican split:**
- Vanderbilt Unity Poll: 47% of MAGA Republicans agree "strong leaders sometimes have to break the rules," vs. 19% of Democrats; the broader Republican coalition is more cautious about authoritarianism.
- IFS analyses repeatedly find Trump voters in red states are more skeptical of AI accelerationism than the GOP base in blue states or the GOP donor class — the "MAGA populist" wing skews more toward AI restriction than the "tech-libertarian" wing represented by Marc Andreessen / David Sacks.

**Progressive vs. moderate Democrats:**
- Less rigorously polled in 2026; data here is fragmentary. Pew's November 2025 short-read shows Democrats overall now nearly as concerned as Republicans, with Democrats moving toward stronger regulatory positions over 2023–2025.

**Existential risk: surprisingly bipartisan.**
- AIPI Senate battleground (Feb 2026): 82% agree we should not build AI smarter than humans without knowing how to control them; this includes 80%+ in both parties' crosstabs.
- FLI/YouGov October 2025: 64% want AGI development paused until proven safe — bipartisan majorities.

### 5. The "AI is less popular than Nixon" claim — assessed

The Reddit/social-media meme has two variants:
1. "AI is less popular than Nixon on the day he resigned."
2. "AI is less popular than ICE / Trump / the Democratic Party / [other unpopular thing]."

**On (1) — strictly wrong.** Per Gallup's own 1999 anniversary article, "An early August 1974 Gallup poll showed Nixon with a job approval rating of only 24%," taken just a few days before he stepped down on August 9; Gallup describes overall disapproval as in "the mid-60s" — a net of roughly –42. The Roper Center for Public Opinion Research at Cornell confirms 24% as the approval figure. AI's worst comparable favorability in 2026 (NBC News, Feb–Mar 2026) is 26% positive / 46% negative — net –20. AI is more popular than Nixon at resignation by about 22 net points. The claim is overstated as a literal popularity comparison.

**On (2) — directionally true, depending on the comparator.** In the NBC News February–March 2026 favorability battery (14 items), AI's –20 net favorability was the third-worst, behind only the Democratic Party (–22) and Iran (–53). AI was less popular than ICE (–18), President Trump (–12), the Republican Party, sanctuary cities, and most named political figures. So "AI is less popular than ICE" — yes, in that one poll. "AI is less popular than Trump" — yes, in that one poll. "AI is less popular than Nixon" — no, not literally.

**Important caveats:**
- The 26/46 number is one poll. Other 2026 polls show different breakdowns (Data for Progress Feb 2026: 48% favorable / 46% unfavorable — net +2). The NBC numbers are at the unfavorable end of the 2026 range.
- "Concern" vs. "opposition" are different. 80% concerned (Quinnipiac) is not 80% opposed. Many Americans use AI while saying they're concerned about it — Quinnipiac found use rising even as trust falls.
- Favorability batteries are sensitive to context. NBC tested AI alongside polarizing entities (Iran, ICE, parties); favorability comparisons to "anti-popular" reference points are not the same as approval ratings.

**Bottom line for briefings:** Use the actual numbers — 46% negative / 26% positive (NBC News Feb–Mar 2026); 80% concerned (Quinnipiac Mar 2026); 71% say "too fast" (Econ/YouGov May 2026). These are stronger and more defensible than the Nixon meme.

### 6. Where polls disagree, and why

- **Sentiment direction.** Pew and Quinnipiac show steeply rising concern over 2024–2026. Echelon Insights (Aug 2025 vs. July 2023) found Americans becoming *more* optimistic about AI on some questions (net "helpful for humanity" shifted from –3 to +10 over two years). Reconciliation: Echelon's questions are abstract ("helpful for humanity"); the Pew/Quinnipiac/NBC questions tap immediate, personal-life feelings, where pessimism dominates. People can simultaneously think AI will be helpful for humanity and worry about it.
- **Favorability magnitude.** NBC News (Feb–Mar 2026) shows AI at 26/46. Data for Progress (Feb 13–17, 2026) shows 48/46 favorable. The difference is partly sample (NBC: RVs via Hart/POS phone+online hybrid; DFP: likely voters via online panel) and partly question wording — DFP asked a standard favorability question; NBC asked within a battery alongside political figures, which may have primed political associations.
- **Partisan direction.** Pew November 2025 says Republicans and Democrats are now equally concerned. NBC News February–March 2026 says Democrats are more negative. Vanderbilt February 2026 says Republicans are slightly more pro-regulation. These are different measures (concern vs. favorability vs. regulation support) and not contradictory in principle — but they show the partisan signal on AI is weak and noisy.
- **Preemption polling.** IFS finds 3-to-1 opposition; AIPI finds 52–33 majorities for state authority; Echelon/Common Sense Media (May 2025) found 70% opposition. All three are advocacy-funded polls. The Common Sense Media question used "ban states from enforcing AI laws" — strong wording. IFS used "Congress adding a provision to a military spending bill that would block states from regulating AI" — more specific. AIPI used a head-to-head federal-vs-state choice. All three converge on the same direction; the specific magnitudes vary.
- **Salience.** This is where polls **don't** disagree — AI consistently fails to appear as a volunteered top issue across Gallup, Harvard CAPS-Harris, and Reuters/Ipsos in 2026. The robustness of this finding should be flagged in briefings: voters want regulation but don't yet vote primarily on AI.

## Policy Implications for the Canary Institute Briefing

The three policy asks in the briefing (oppose federal preemption, support S. 2938, distribute a Dear Colleague letter) all map cleanly to majority public opinion as measured in 2026:

- **Oppose federal preemption (December 11, 2025 EO "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" and the March 20, 2026 White House Legislative Framework).** The December 11 EO directed the DOJ to establish an "AI Litigation Task Force" to challenge state AI laws and tasked Commerce with identifying "onerous" state statutes for potential defunding under the BEAD broadband program. Public opinion is strongly against this approach: IFS/YouGov November 2025 (3-to-1 opposition in the general public; net –18 among Trump voters; net –56 among Harris voters); Common Sense Media/Echelon May 2025 (70% opposition, including 68% of Republicans); AIPI January–February 2026 battleground (52–33 majority for state authority in House battleground; analogous in Senate). The IFS January 2026 vote-shift experiment shows preemption is *electorally* damaging for Republican incumbents in red states. This is the strongest single message a Dear Colleague letter could carry to swing-state Republican members.

- **Support S. 2938 (Artificial Intelligence Risk Evaluation Act, Hawley-Blumenthal, introduced September 29, 2025, creating an Advanced AI Evaluation Program at the Department of Energy and prohibiting deployment of advanced AI systems until developers comply with its requirements).** Pre-deployment safety evaluation polls at 73–76% support / 9–10% opposition in AIPI's January–February 2026 battleground polls — among the highest support levels for any tech policy ever measured. The 20-to-1 IFS finding on safety-testing approval reinforces this. Notably, support is bipartisan (D 77%, R 76%, I 75% in the AIPI House poll).

- **Dear Colleague letter framing.** The most defensible quantitative case combines: (a) Pew June 2025's 50% "more concerned than excited" (probability-based, rigorous); (b) Economist/YouGov May 9–11, 2026's "71% say AI moving too fast, including 68% of Republicans" (cross-partisan, very recent); (c) AIPI January 2026 battleground 76% support for pre-deployment safety testing in 10 swing House districts (district-level relevance); (d) IFS November 2025 3-to-1 opposition to federal preemption (Trump-voter relevance); (e) FLI/YouGov October 2025 64%/5% on frontier-AI moratorium (existential-risk framing). These five numbers together make a unified case for the three asks.

## Recommendations

For the Canary Institute briefing book aimed at congressional staff:

### Stage 1 (immediate, May–June 2026): Lead with the strongest, most-defensible numbers
- Use **Pew June 2025** (50% more concerned than excited, up from 37% in 2021) as the long-trend anchor. This is the most methodologically rigorous, probability-based number available and has the longest comparable trendline.
- Use **Economist/YouGov May 9–11, 2026** ("71% say AI moving too fast, including 68% of Republicans and 77% of Democrats") for cross-partisan framing.
- Use **AIPI January 2026 House battleground (76% support pre-release safety testing)** and **AIPI February 2026 Senate battleground (73% support)** as the headline number for **S. 2938 pre-deployment safety evaluation**. Note that AIPI is an advocacy organization and provide the IFS January 2026 N=6,200 "20-to-1 support for safety-testing" finding as corroboration.
- Use **IFS November 2025 / IFS January 2026 N=6,200** for **federal preemption opposition (3-to-1, including a plurality of Trump voters opposed)**. This is the most policy-relevant number for opposing the December 11, 2025 EO and the March 20, 2026 White House Framework. Pair with **Common Sense Media / Echelon Insights May 2025 (70% opposition, including 68% of Republicans)** for bipartisan corroboration.

### Stage 2 (after June 2026): Watch for these benchmarks that would update recommendations
- **If AI rises in volunteered "most important problem" polls** (Gallup, Harvard CAPS-Harris) to ≥3% — AI becomes a real midterm issue and the political calculus for fence-sitting members shifts. Currently not occurring.
- **If the Pew "more concerned than excited" number rises above 55% in the next fielding** — concern is accelerating, not plateauing; existential-risk framing becomes more viable.
- **If partisan convergence on regulation reverses** (i.e., Republicans become noticeably more pro-AI than Democrats again) — bipartisan coalition strategy needs reassessment.
- **If a major incident (e.g., a chatbot-linked teen suicide verdict, a deepfake election event) occurs** — expect salience to spike; have ready-to-deploy talking-point updates.

### Stage 3 (drafting the Dear Colleague letter)
- Anchor on the **80% concerned (Quinnipiac March 2026)** + **76% support pre-release safety testing in House battlegrounds (AIPI January 2026)** + **3-to-1 opposition to federal preemption (IFS November 2025)** triad. ## Caveats

1. **Methodology matters and varies widely.** Pew (probability-based ATP panel), Gallup, AP-NORC (AmeriSpeak), and Quinnipiac (RDD live-interviewer) are gold-standard probability samples. AIPI, IFS, Data for Progress, FLI, Common Sense Media polls are typically online opt-in panels (YouGov or Lucid) — high-quality but methodologically different. Where probability-based and opt-in polls disagree, weight the probability-based number more.

2. **Advocacy-funded polls have framing.** AIPI is a pro-safety-regulation think tank; IFS is a family-policy organization with strong anti-Big-Tech priors; FLI advocates for AI moratoriums; NetChoice/TechNet represent industry. Question wording matters: "regulatory guardrails" sounds friendlier than "burdensome regulation"; "block states from protecting children" sounds worse than "preempt patchwork laws." When citing advocacy polls, also cite a non-advocacy comparator if possible.

3. **Salience is genuinely low.** No major MIP poll in 2026 has shown AI breaking the top 5 issues. This means voters say they care a lot when asked but don't yet vote on it. Briefings should be honest about this — congressional members who are skeptical of constituent pressure on AI are not entirely wrong, even though policy support is broad.

4. **Methodological dispute over AI in polling itself.** Pew's May 12, 2026 Q&A explicitly warned that AI-generated and bot-fraudulent survey responses are a growing threat to opt-in polls. This is a particular concern for online-panel surveys (YouGov, Lucid). Pew's recommendation is to use probability-based panels for high-stakes inference.

5. **The "AI is less popular than Nixon" claim is overstated and should not be used.** Nixon's approval in early August 1974 was 24% (Gallup/Roper Center), against disapproval in the mid-60s — net roughly –42. AI's worst 2026 favorability is ~26/46 (net –20). The accurate version of the claim is: "In NBC News's February–March 2026 favorability battery, the only items more unpopular than AI were the Democratic Party and Iran" — and that should be used with the explicit comparator named.

6. **Distinguish concern from opposition.** 80% concerned (Quinnipiac) does not mean 80% opposed. Roughly 56–63% of Americans use AI tools, and use is rising even as trust falls. The right framing is "concerned but adopting" — voters are not Luddites; they want the technology with guardrails.

7. **The age finding is counterintuitive and important.** Young voters use AI most but are not more positive about it; they are angrier and more anxious. The Gallup Feb–Mar 2026 data showing Gen Z hopefulness at 18% is the most striking single age-related finding.

8. **The Iran war is currently squeezing political bandwidth.** AP-NORC, Reuters/Ipsos, Gallup, and Harvard CAPS-Harris all show the Iran conflict (started Feb 2026 with US/Israeli strikes) absorbing political attention. AI's already-low salience may be temporarily even lower for this reason. This is a transient effect.
