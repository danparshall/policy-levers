# Does China Want to Race to Superintelligence — or Would It Join a "Plan A"-Style Deal?

## TL;DR
- **The ubiquitous US assumption "we can't afford to lose the race to China" is probably too strong, but the opposite hypothesis — "China would join a viable coordination deal" — is only partly supported and rests on a much weaker foundation than its proponents imply.** The best-supported reading as of August 2026 is: China's leadership does *not* appear to be pursuing an all-out AGI/superintelligence moonshot the way leading US labs are; it has authentic, regime-driven reasons to want AI "controllable"; and it has repeatedly signed and proposed cooperative frameworks. But its cooperation is concentrated in *cheap-to-moderate-cost* signals (statements, institution-building, scientist dialogues), while its *revealed* behavior (compute buildout, chip smuggling, self-reliance drive, military-civil fusion) is fully consistent with racing, and it has shown essentially no willingness to accept the *intrusive, trust-displacing verification* that a Plan A deal actually requires.
- **On the specific plans: a Plan A-style deal (total transparency + intrusive verification + mutually assured compute destruction) faces a China-acceptance bar that current evidence does not clear.** A weaker, "AI safety in parallel" arrangement — non-binding best-practice exchange, red lines on misuse/nuclear command-and-control, capability-sharing — is where China is actually engaging, and that gap is the crux. A Plan S-style indefinite halt is even less likely to attract China unless it perceived an imminent loss-of-control catastrophe.
- **What should update you:** the decisive, currently-missing evidence is Chinese acceptance of *reciprocal intrusiveness* — inspectors, hardware-enabled mechanisms, compute declarations. Track that, not rhetoric. China's arms-control history (accepts monitoring stations and Chemical Weapons Convention industry inspections; resists intrusive nuclear verification and trilateral talks) is a moderately pessimistic prior for the Plan A verification bar.

## Key Findings

**1. Anchoring the plans.** The AI Futures Project's *AI 2040: Plan A* (Thomas Larsen, Romeo Dean, Brendan Halstead, Eli Lifland, Ryan Greenblatt, Daniel Kokotajlo) is explicitly a *recommendation, not a prediction*; it delays superintelligence from a ~2030 default to ~2040. Plan A = a US-China-led international deal, beginning ~2029, involving total AI R&D research transparency, trustless verification (inference-only retrofits via network taps + partial recomputation; compute declarations covering >99% of world compute), multiple firms scaling slowly together, superintelligence deferred to ~2040, and "mutually assured compute destruction" (datacenters/fabs in deliberately vulnerable locations that self-destruct on defection). Plan S = an *indefinite halt* on frontier capabilities progress, lasting years, with resumption conditioned on alignment/control breakthroughs (e.g., lie detectors, human uploads, intelligence enhancement). The authors themselves assign Plan A low probability — the six-author estimates for "Plan A happens" range from 3% (Thomas) to 15% (Daniel), clustering mid-single-digits — and estimate ~48% cumulative "deal decline" risk by 2040 via base-rate methods (rising to ~62% via cause-decomposition), i.e., they treat durability of both sides' commitment as a central fragility. Crucially, Plan A's feasibility rests on China accepting *intrusive transparency*, which the authors concede is a far higher bar than signing statements.

**2. "China" is not a unitary actor, and the actors diverge.** The evidence strongly supports disaggregation:
- **Xi/top Party leadership**: Frames AI as a "strategic technology" and a whole-nation priority. At the CCP Politburo's 20th collective study session (April 25, 2025 — the first on AI since October 2018; guest lecturer Zheng Nanning of Xi'an Jiaotong University), Xi, per CSET's translation of the Xinhua readout, stressed being "self-reliant" (自立自强) and building an "autonomously controllable" (自主可控) chip-and-software ecosystem and "seizing the initiative" — but *also* flagged AI's "unprecedented risks and challenges," called for laws, "systems for technical monitoring, early risk warning and emergency response," and "safety, reliability, and controllability," and described AI as a potential "international public good." At WAIC in July 2025 Xi made what Concordia AI and MERICS's Rebecca Arcesati identify as his first *public* mention of AI-specific "loss of control" (失控).
- **Technocratic ministries (MOST, MIIT/CAICT, NDRC, CAC)**: Focused on *diffusion* ("AI+"), standards, and content control; MOST's expert committee has invoked "human control" since its 2021 "Ethical Norms for New Generation AI."
- **Frontier labs**: DeepSeek's Liang Wenfeng is the clearest AGI-and-recursive-self-improvement believer — in his leaked May 2026 investor-call transcript he said the model's "first job is to raise DeepSeek's own work efficiency... That's the fastest path to AGI," and Reuters (citing Yicai, July 23, 2026) reported he framed self-improving AGI research as a priority over profit after DeepSeek's ~$52B-valuation funding round. But most Chinese labs are deployment-focused; Chinese AI-governance researchers commonly view DeepSeek (and Zhipu/Z.ai) as the *only* labs chasing AGI, and AGI does not appear in the 15th Five-Year Plan executive summary.
- **Senior scientists** (Yao Qizhi/Andrew Yao, Zhang Ya-Qin, Xue Lan, Zeng Yi, Zhou Bowen): The most safety-forward actors, signing IDAIS statements and building CnAISDA and the Shanghai/Beijing safety-institute ecosystem.
- **The security state and PLA**: Pursuing "intelligentized warfare," military-civil fusion, and treating frontier AI as a strategic asset; also the source of controls reportedly barring Chinese AI leaders from US travel.
These actors' revealed priorities conflict: the diffusion-focused technocrats, the AGI-focused labs, and the safety-focused scientists are not pursuing the same thing.

**3. The three evidence classes diverge in an informative pattern.**
- **STATED preferences lean cooperative.** Global AI Governance Initiative (Oct 2023); a China-sponsored UN capacity-building resolution (2024, backed by 140+ countries); the Global AI Governance Action Plan plus a proposed World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization (WAICO) unveiled by Premier Li Qiang at WAIC July 2025. WAICO was formally established July 16, 2026, when 29 countries signed an agreement in Shanghai creating "an independent intergovernmental international organization headquartered in Shanghai" (per Xinhua), with Foreign Minister Wang Yi signing on behalf of China, founders including Kazakhstan, Laos, Pakistan, Russia and Indonesia, and UN Secretary-General Guterres attending. Add the Nov 2024 Xi-Biden statement on human control of nuclear weapons and consistent "AI as international public good" rhetoric.
- **REVEALED preferences lean toward racing (or at least determined fast-following).** Epoch AI (report "Diversion and resale: estimating compute smuggling to China") estimates that between 290,000 and 1.6 million H100-equivalents were smuggled to China through 2025, with a median of ~660,000 — "roughly a third of China's total compute" — indicating willingness to circumvent controls to acquire frontier compute; aggressive SMIC/Huawei Ascend self-sufficiency; large datacenter and energy buildout; the 2017 New Generation AI Development Plan's 2030 "world leader" goal; and military-civil fusion.
- **COSTLY signals are real but concentrated at the low-to-medium end of the cost spectrum.** IDAIS-Beijing (March 2024), IDAIS-Venice (Sept 2024), IDAIS-Shanghai (July 2025) with senior Chinese scientists; the China AI Safety and Development Association (CnAISDA, Feb 2025); the Shanghai AI Lab + Concordia "Frontier AI Risk Management Framework" (v1.0 July 2025, v1.5 Feb 2026); 16+ Chinese firms' voluntary safety commitments; Zhipu becoming the first Chinese company to sign the Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments; and domestic regulation with real teeth on *content/deployment* (algorithm/genAI registration, TC260 framework, 3,500+ non-compliant products removed). The *highest-cost* signal — accepting reciprocal intrusive verification — is essentially absent.

**4. The scholarly hypotheses, tested.**
- **Jeffrey Ding's diffusion thesis is well-supported and is the single most important corrective to the race framing.** China's policy apparatus prioritizes *diffusion/application* (AI+), not an AGI moonshot; AGI is absent from the 15th Five-Year Plan executive summary; officials such as Gong Ke (dean of the Chinese Institute of New Generation AI Development Strategies) explicitly prioritize delivery over "the grand narrative of AGI." Ding's "training for the wrong race" argument implies China may not *want* the specific superintelligence sprint US hawks fear — while noting the US currently leads on actual economy-wide diffusion.
- **The regime-stability/authentic-controllability hypothesis is moderately supported.** The CCP's interest in "controllable" (可控/可控) AI is genuine but *primarily* about political/social control and misuse, not (yet) alignment/loss-of-control in the Western x-risk sense — though Xi's 2025 "loss of control" language and the September 2025 AI Safety Governance Framework 2.0 (warning of "loss of control over knowledge and capabilities of nuclear, biological, chemical, and missile weapons") show movement toward the latter. The Chinese term 可控 is used for *both* political control and technical corrigibility, which is analytically important: this authentic control interest is a real basis for cooperation on *controllability*, but does not straightforwardly translate into willingness to accept *external* verification of China's own labs.
- **Jessica Chen Weiss's self-fulfilling-race argument is credible and directly relevant:** US race-framing plausibly manufactures some of the very Chinese behavior it fears, and Weiss notes that when the US frames the relationship as winner-take-all, "there's not very much interest in the Chinese side in establishing guardrails."
- **The skeptical case is serious and cannot be dismissed.** Its strongest version (articulated by hawkish analysts such as CFR's Chris McGuire and in War on the Rocks/AEI writing): China free-rides on Western frontier AI (distillation, harvesting, subject to the National Intelligence Law), uses safety rhetoric partly to slow US racing, calibrates messaging by audience, and treats dialogues partly as intelligence/relationship management. Documented audience-calibration supports the caution: CnAISDA orders "safety" before "development" in English but reverses the order in Mandarin; Matt Sheehan and colleagues note Chinese domestic framing increasingly uses *ke xin* (trustworthy/reliable) rather than *an quan* (safety), pointing toward CCP-auditable deployment and controllability rather than frontier capability caps.

**5. Verification is the binding constraint — and the thinnest part of the cooperative case.** Named Chinese scientists (Yao, Zhang Ya-Qin, Xue Lan) signed IDAIS statements endorsing "verification methods" (Shanghai 2025, which called to "commit to global verifiable behavioural red lines" and mutually enforce verification via market-access incentives) and even "hardware-enabled mechanisms on AI chips" (Venice 2024) — but in aspirational, non-committal language ("eventually," "could take place"). WAIC 2025 panels (Zhang Ya-Qin, Gao Wen, Stuart Russell) recommended "hardware-level safety mechanisms" generically. However, there is essentially no Chinese technical co-authorship of the HEM/flexHEG/compute-verification literature — the "six layers" (Baker et al.), flexHEG (Petrie et al.), and related technical work are overwhelmingly Western — and no Chinese *government* endorsement of intrusive inspection regimes. The Shanghai AI Lab/Concordia Frontier AI Risk Management Framework, while genuinely technical, addresses *developer self-governance and risk classification*, not treaty verification or hardware compliance. China's official diplomacy (WAICO, the Action Plan) favors norm/institution-building, capacity-sharing, and sovereignty — arguably the *opposite* posture from intrusive verification. China's arms-control record reinforces this: it accepts non-intrusive monitoring (five-plus CTBT stations certified; 700+ CWC on-site inspections received, 500+ of civilian industrial facilities) but, per Sandia and Carnegie analyses, has historically treated trust as a *precondition* for verification rather than a product of it, and has resisted intrusive nuclear verification and trilateral arms-control talks.

**6. The concrete diplomatic trajectory (2023→2026).** The US-China intergovernmental AI dialogue began in Geneva May 2024, then stalled — Brookings attributes this to a delegation mismatch (US technical experts vs. Chinese political/foreign-policy officials, which pushed talks toward export controls). At the May 2026 Beijing Trump-Xi summit, both sides confirmed agreement to *relaunch* an intergovernmental AI dialogue: Treasury Secretary Bessent said they would "set up a protocol... best practices for AI to make sure nonstate actors don't get a hold of these models," and China MFA spokesperson Guo Jiakun (May 19, 2026) said the leaders "agreed to launch an intergovernmental dialogue on artificial intelligence." First talks were reportedly targeted for around September 2026 (unconfirmed/not finalized as of July 2026). This is explicitly non-binding and does not involve verification/inspection — it lives in the "AI safety in parallel" space (Matt Sheehan's framing), not the Plan A space.

## Details

**Strongest honest answer to "Does China want to race?"** China wants to *not lose* — which is different from wanting to *win a superintelligence sprint*. The dominant, best-evidenced posture at the leadership level is diffusion-first techno-industrial competition plus hedged fast-following at the frontier, coupled with an authentic (regime-rooted) desire to keep AI "controllable." The all-out-AGI-race framing best fits a subset of actors (DeepSeek, possibly Z.ai; parts of the PLA's aspirations) rather than the Party center. So the US "we must win the race" premise is built on a partial misread — but "China would happily stop racing and join a deal" is also unproven, because China's racing-consistent behavior (acquiring frontier compute by any means, self-reliance at near-any-cost) is exactly what you would see from a determined competitor, and its cooperation has not been tested at the price point that matters. The user's hypothesis is therefore *directionally plausible but not established*: the honest verdict is "China may well prefer a coordinated off-ramp to a reckless race, but there is no evidence yet that it would pay the specific, sovereignty-piercing price Plan A demands."

**Conditions under which China would/wouldn't join.** China is *most* likely to join arrangements that (a) are framed as multilateral/UN-legitimated rather than a US-dictated condominium; (b) preserve sovereignty and avoid intrusive external inspection of Chinese facilities; (c) confer status/leadership (the WAICO template); (d) address misuse/CBRN/nuclear-command-and-control and loss-of-control risks the CCP genuinely fears; and (e) do not require it to freeze in a position of permanent inferiority. It is *least* likely to accept: reciprocal intrusive verification, on-chip governance it does not control, transparency that exposes its labs/military programs, or a freeze locking in US advantage — all of which Plan A requires. A Plan S halt is plausible for China *only* under a shared perception of imminent catastrophe (a "Sputnik-in-reverse" loss-of-control demonstration). Net: **Plan A as specified is a poor fit for revealed Chinese preferences; a weaker deal (GPU-arms-control-lite, red lines on misuse and autonomous replication, best-practice exchange, or a multilaterally-framed "CERN for AI") is a plausible fit.** Note the asymmetry the user should weigh: Plan A's transparency is intended to *substitute* for trust, which is precisely the Chinese red line (China wants trust *first*), so the very mechanism designed to make the deal work is the one China is most likely to reject.

## Recommendations
- **Treat verification acceptance — not rhetoric — as the load-bearing indicator.** Adopt the operating rule: cooperative statements are weak evidence; acceptance of *reciprocal intrusiveness* is strong evidence. Weight accordingly in policy conversations, and explicitly discount signals whose audience is Western.
- **Stage the test over 6-18 months.** Watch for: (1) whether the relaunched US-China AI dialogue moves from "best practices" toward any *mutual monitoring* commitment; (2) whether Chinese institutions (CAICT, Shanghai AI Lab, CnAISDA, Tsinghua I-AIIG) begin *technical* verification/HEM work rather than signing aspirational statements; (3) whether China accepts any compute-declaration or inspection pilot; (4) whether domestic Chinese regulation adds *capability/compute thresholds* (not just content rules); (5) whether AGI language enters top-level plans; (6) whether nationalist triumphalism or loss-of-control caution dominates after the next major capability jump.
- **Benchmarks that would move the assessment toward "China would join a viable deal":** Chinese-co-authored verification R&D; a Chinese-accepted reciprocal inspection or on-chip pilot; explicit Chinese endorsement of compute thresholds; Track-1 (not just Track-2/scientist) engagement on red lines with teeth; Chinese willingness to constrain its own labs in exchange for reciprocal US constraint.
- **Benchmarks that would move toward "cooperative talk while racing":** continued/rising smuggling; acceleration of Ascend/SMIC scale-up with no transparency offers; the dialogue being used to extract export-control concessions without safety reciprocity; domestic framing that treats "safety" purely as content/political control; refusal to discuss any verification mechanism.

## Caveats
- **Source asymmetry.** Much of the cooperative-signal evidence is curated by safety-motivated intermediaries (Concordia AI/Brian Tse; SAIF/IDAIS; Carnegie's China AI team, Sheehan and Singer). These are high-quality but not disinterested; the known Western failure mode is charitable reading of CCP signaling because cooperation is desired. The racing-side evidence (Epoch on smuggling, CNAS, CSET translations) is more independent. The AI 2040 materials are themselves advocacy for Plan A.
- **Primary vs. secondary.** Xi's speeches, the WAIC Action Plan, and the WAICO founding are primary (via CSET/gov.cn/Xinhua translations); judgments about *authenticity* are secondary and contested.
- **Confidence grading.** *High confidence:* diffusion-first leadership posture; reality of low/medium-cost cooperative signals; smuggling scale; absence of Chinese intrusive-verification acceptance to date. *Medium confidence:* authenticity of the controllability concern; that Plan A is a poor fit for Chinese preferences. *Low/contested confidence:* whether China would accept a *weaker* multilateral deal in practice; the durability of any commitment (the AI 2040 team's own ~48-62% decline estimate applies to the US side too).
- **Moving target.** The 2023→2026 trajectory is toward *more* Chinese safety engagement, but simultaneously *more* capability and *more* compute — averaging over the trend would mislead. Post-DeepSeek nationalist confidence and the US turn toward "dominance" (Vice President Vance at Paris 2025; the July 2025 America's AI Action Plan) both push against Plan A-style coordination, meaning the binding constraint may currently be as much on the US side as the Chinese side.
