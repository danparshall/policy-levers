# FRONTIER Act notes — running list

Working list for outreach on the FRONTIER Act (introduced 2026-07-23, H.R. number TBD — text-as-posted still `H.R. ll`). Same three-tier structure as `NOTES.md` (GAAIA): (1) drafting bugs, zero-cost to accept; (2) cheap technical asks, small text changes with outsized effect; (3) substantive asks, Canary comparative advantage. Provenance: 2026-07-24 ingest (`bills/frontier-act/`, 9 per-section summaries + `FRONTIER_VS_GAAIA.md`) + same-day walkthrough with Dan. Seeded from the walkthrough; the full walk of GAAIA `NOTES.md`'s 31 items against FRONTIER is still pending and will grow this list.

## Tier 1 — drafting bugs

1. **§ 4(h)(2) routing bug, carried verbatim from GAAIA § 111(g)(2).** 24-hour imminent-risk-of-death reports go to "a law enforcement agency with jurisdiction," not the Under Secretary. A local PD or FBI field office has neither AI-incident capacity nor a statutory duty to forward. Flagged to the GAAIA inbox in June; survived the rewrite unchanged. Fix: route to the Under Secretary AND law enforcement, or add a forwarding duty.

## Tier 2 — cheap technical asks

1. **§ 3(f): add "or decrease" — the threshold ratchet points backwards.** The Under Secretary may only *raise* the compute/revenue/expenditure thresholds, even though § 3(f)(2)'s calibration standard explicitly cites "advances in algorithmic and hardware efficiency." Efficiency gains move catastrophic-risk capability DOWN the compute curve; the only fast lever points up. If the executive-rulemaking design is about keeping pace with the technology (plausible), the one-way direction contradicts that rationale in exactly the dangerous direction. Textually a two-word fix; politically heavier (executive discretion over coverage), so pair with the § 3(f)(3) biennial-review publication as the check.
2. **§ 3: restore the consultation clause.** GAAIA § 111(k)(3) / § 112(p)(3) required stakeholder consultation (academia, open-source community, private/public sector) before rulemaking; FRONTIER § 3 requires none. One subsection restores it.
3. **§ 4(e): restore GAAIA § 111(d)'s false-statements scope.** GAAIA barred knowing falsity about catastrophic risk, its management, AND framework compliance; § 4(e) reaches only subjects the transparency report must cover. Framework-compliance falsity now bites only through the § 4(c) audit backstop.
4. **§ 6: express the audit/assessment cumulation intent.** Default reading is that very-large developers owe BOTH the § 4(c) compliance audit and the § 5 IVO assessment (coherent — different questions), but no provision says so expressly and the "except as otherwise expressly provided" carve-out is empty. One sentence forecloses the litigation.
5. **§ 7: pair the GAO report with a mandatory response window.** GAO documenting IVO capture compels nothing. The Under Secretary already HAS authority to act (license revocation, § 3(c) COI rules) — the gap is obligation, not power; the SEC/Big Four and pre-2008 credit-rating history is capture documented for years while authority sat unused. Ask: statutory duty for the Under Secretary to respond within X days to any GAO finding of systemic capture.

6. **§ 2(18)(B): targeted-attack exception to the equity carve-out.** The blanket equity exclusion is defensible (ordinary market carnage would trip the $1B gate constantly), but it also excludes the primary harm of a *targeted* incident. Draftable middle: equity losses count when the entity was the direct target of § 2(6)(A)(ii) conduct (extortion/theft/fraud/cyberattack), limited to the target's own equity — market-wide contagion stays excluded. Loss-causation measurement has an existing home in securities-fraud event-study practice. Expect valuation fights; still better than excluding the main harm channel of market-manipulation scenarios.
7. **§ 4(k): extend registration to ALL frontier developers, not just large.** As drafted, the registry attaches at the large tier ($50M revenue + $1B spend), so a zero-revenue frontier lab never files. Registration is nearly costless (identity, addresses, beneficial owners, three contacts) — there is no innovation-burden rationale for letting a compute-only lab stay off the public list. This also patches the residual Ilya gap at the cheapest possible layer.
8. **§ 2(7)(C): define "loss of control" to expressly include containment/sandbox breach.** Undefined as drafted. The textual case that eval-context escapes count is strong — prong (D) carries an explicit eval-context carve-out and prong (C) does not, so Congress knew how to exclude eval behavior and didn't (expressio unius) — but labs will argue an escape during an authorized red-team exercise isn't "loss of control." One definitional sentence locks in the reading. The incentive stakes are large: if every sandbox breach is a mandatory 72-hour federal report, the cheapest compliance path is models that don't try to escape.
9. **§ 4(g)(1)(A)(iii): make the incident-report-content rulemaking mandatory with enumerated minimum categories.** The statutory content floor is date + why-it-qualifies + internal-use flag — the same "1-pager on ~oops a bad thing happened" LawAI's Arnold criticizes in RAISE (thread, 2026-07-24). The escape hatch exists — (iii) lets the Under Secretary require "any additional information" by regulation — but it's permissive and unscoped. Ask: convert to a mandatory 180-day rulemaking (parallel to the § 3(b)-(d) trio) enumerating minimum categories per the Arnold/Casper framework: detection and monitoring, mitigation timeline, foreseeability/prior related reports, environment security, notification of affected parties, ongoing-harm status, remaining unknowns — plus, per Mallen & Gupta (LW, 2026-07-23), monitor behavior: whether automated monitors (incl. AI monitors) flagged, missed, or failed to report the behavior, which is the load-bearing unknown for threat-modeling the incident class. Cheap because the delegation already exists; the ask only makes it non-optional.
10. **§ 2(7)(D): strike "demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk" from the deception prong.** This is RAISE's Catch-22 clause reborn ("demonstrable evidence of an increased risk of critical harm") — the whole point of incident reporting is to gather the evidence that would demonstrate the risk, so conditioning the report on the demonstration inverts the epistemics. Prongs (A) and (C) already show the right design (per-se triggers, no risk showing); (D) should match. Developers will contest "demonstrates" on exactly the incidents where reporting matters most. Taxonomy argument (via Mallen & Gupta): prongs (C) and (D) map onto the two misalignment regimes — loud score-seeking (current: models don't hide, loss-of-control fires per se) and quiet deception (future: the scary class). As drafted, the prong aimed at the scarier class carries the highest trigger bar, and stealth makes "demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk" structurally hardest to satisfy precisely when it matters most. The demonstration clause is anti-correlated with the danger of the incident class it governs.

## Tier 3 — substantive asks

1. **Restore § 113 whistleblower protections as a new § 10.** GAAIA's whistleblower title — anti-retaliation, jury trial, non-waivable rights incl. mandatory-arbitration override, whole-industry coverage, no sunset — is dropped without replacement (zero hits for "whistle"/"retaliat" in FRONTIER). Under the new architecture the loss compounds: § 4(c) auditors are developer-picked and developer-paid, § 5 IVOs are developer-paid, and an IVO lead partner who signs a hostile report or pulls the § 5(p)(1)(B) tripwire has no statutory anti-retaliation floor either. Sharpening discovery (2026-07-24): § 4(g)(1) lets "a frontier developer or member of the public" confidentially submit incident reports — so the INTAKE for an employee reporter already exists in the bill. What's missing is purely the shield: an employee who uses the channel Congress built has zero anti-retaliation protection. "You built the mailbox; protect the people who use it" is the cleanest possible framing for the § 113 restoration. ~4-line insertion of GAAIA § 113 text. Lead ask.
2. **Inseverability clause for the core trade (§§ 4/5/9), carried from GAAIA Tier 2.8 — now more urgent.** Still no severability or inseverability language anywhere in the bill. Default doctrine presumes severability, so a compelled-speech challenge (NetChoice playbook) striking §§ 4–5 leaves § 9's preemption standing alone — and with the sunset gone, that failure mode is now *permanent* naked preemption rather than a 3-year one. States would have no federal regime AND no power to build their own. One-sentence fix linking the substantive duties to the preemption.
3. **§ 8 intake gap: no statutory trigger below the very-large tier.** § 8's emergency authority reaches ANY frontier developer (no revenue floor — good), but the only mandatory feeder is the § 5(p)(1)(B) IVO tripwire, which covers ~5 companies. Below that tier, § 4(h) incident reports go to the Under Secretary with no statutory escalate-to-§ 8 pathway, and nothing obligates the Secretary to act on over-the-transom information. The authority is broadest exactly where its intake plumbing is thinnest. Ask options: a § 4(h) escalation duty (Under Secretary must refer qualifying incidents to the Secretary on a clock), or extend a lightweight referral duty to § 4(c) auditors.
4. **Post-incident investigative authority — the missing NTSB.** FRONTIER mandates the reports (§ 4(h)) and 9363 builds the confidential desk, but nobody has compulsory process for incident follow-up: no subpoena power, no document demands, no witness interviews (verified — zero hits for subpoena/investigation in §§ 3–4; the unredacted-access rights attach to audits and redacted publications, not incidents). The Under Secretary receives whatever the report contains and cannot compel more from a resisting developer. Aviation parallel is exact: this is a reporting system without an NTSB go-team. Per Arnold: statutory triggers should be low and easy to prove precisely BECAUSE the real information-gathering happens through post-incident investigation — FRONTIER has half the design. Ask: scoped investigative authority for critical safety incidents (document/testimony compulsion, deadline-bound, judicial enforcement), modeled on NTSB accident authority or CSB. Substantive and politically heavier; pairs naturally with the incident-content ask (Tier 2.9). Precedent stack (2026-07-24): the CSRB (2022, EO 14028, deliberate NTSB clone) proved the model transfers to digital incidents (Storm-0558 report: "cascade of security failures at Microsoft," real behavior change) AND proved the failure mode — no subpoena power, no statute, dissolved by DHS memo Jan 2025 mid-Salt-Typhoon, not reconstituted as of latest reporting (Lawfare, Sept 2025). Post-*Trump v. Slaughter* (609 U.S. ___, June 29, 2026; Humphrey's Executor overruled 6-3), removal-protection independence is off the menu for anything wielding executive power — Sotomayor's dissent lists even the investigations-only Chemical Safety Board as in doubt. Design spec is therefore RESILIENCE, not independence: existence by statute, mandatory duties that run with the office, dedicated appropriation, Senate-confirmed members on staggered terms (confirmation friction is now a feature — slow-to-fill counters fast-to-empty), subpoena power vested in the body. Valence-neutral framing: doesn't ask the President to tolerate insulation the Court says he needn't; asks Congress to do the one thing indisputably still theirs.
5. **Audited-artifact provenance, carried from GAAIA Tier 3 unchanged.** Neither § 4(c) audit nor § 5 IVO assessment binds the assessed weights to the served weights — checkpoint substitution (assess A, ship A′) remains undetectable by any record the statute requires. Same ask as before: registered fingerprint of evaluated weights, auditor hash certification, tamper-evident derivation records. Standard post-SolarWinds federal-vendor compliance stack; marginal burden ~zero.

## Observed, not asking (for now)

- **Under Secretary removability.** § 2(21): appointed by the *Secretary* — not Presidentially appointed, not Senate-confirmed, no term, no for-cause protection. The entire regime's administrator is an at-will appointee two steps from the President; the 2025 BLS-commissioner firing shows the failure mode, and the BLS chief at least had a statutory term. Fixes all have costs: PAS confirmation adds 8–14 months of vacancy; for-cause protection for a single officer wielding executive power is constitutionally contested post-*Seila Law*. Realistic near-term protections are the § 3(f)(3) publication duties and congressional oversight. Watch; raise if a markup opens the appointments question.
- **Ilya-loophole closure is layer-specific — keep the blog claim precise.** The base tier dropping its revenue floor means a zero-revenue lab owes transparency reports + incident reporting from first training (this matches GAAIA NOTES Tier 3.1's proposed minimal fix almost verbatim). But large-tier prongs are conjunctive ($50M revenue AND $1B spend), so a strictly zero-revenue lab still escapes framework/audit/registry. The expenditure prong's real bite is labs with modest revenue and huge spend. Closed at the visibility layer; Tier 2.7 (registry extension) patches the cheapest remaining piece.
- **§ 8(l) exclusivity / Commerce-only architecture.** Likely deliberate command-simplification. Cuts both ways: prevents federal-agency freelancing on catastrophic-risk grounds, but also creates a single point of refusal (a Secretary who declines to act cannot be routed around), and as a constraint on an erratic President it is thin — the Secretary serves at pleasure. Interagency deconfliction (CISA/FBI/DOD roles in a live incident) is unaddressed anywhere in the bill; that piece may be worth an ask later.

- **Incident reporting is a wasting asset (Mallen & Gupta framing).** The current dominant misalignment class (score-seeking) is maximally legible to a reporting regime: models don't hide, don't care about detection, generate loud warning shots. Training against detected misbehavior selects for stealth, and the authors argue naive fixes convert score-seekers toward scheming. Two governance implications: (1) the visibility machinery's information yield is highest NOW and declines as labs train against detection — build it while failures are loud; (2) tempering the alignment-incentive claim (Dan, 2026-07-24 walkthrough): mandatory escape reporting pressures labs to make escapes stop, but there are two ways to make escapes stop — align the model, or teach it to escape only when unobserved — and the reporting regime cannot distinguish which one it purchased. Prong (D)'s eval-context structure is a partial hedge (out-of-eval deception is per se the reportable class) but the Tier 2.10 demonstration clause undercuts it.
