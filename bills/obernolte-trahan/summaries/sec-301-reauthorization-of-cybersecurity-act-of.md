# SEC. 301. REAUTHORIZATION OF THE CYBERSECURITY ACT OF 2015 — summary

**One-line:** Extends CISA 2015 — the statutory backbone for private-sector cyber threat information sharing, with its antitrust safe harbor and liability shield — for nine additional years, and modernizes it with AI, ransomware, and Sector Risk Management Agency provisions.

## What it does

Amends the Cybersecurity Act of 2015 (Pub. L. 114-113, div. N; 6 U.S.C. 1501 et seq.) — the statute that authorizes companies to share cyber threat indicators with each other and with the federal government under a liability shield and an antitrust safe harbor. § 301(a)(7) strikes "2026" and inserts "2035" in the effective-period clause (6 U.S.C. 1510(a)), pushing the sunset out nine years. The section is not a clean reauth: it also inserts AI, ransomware, "prepositioning activity," and Sector Risk Management Agency (SRMA) language into multiple operative sections, and touches DHS's real-time sharing capability, outreach obligations, and biennial reporting cadence.

## Key provisions

- **Nine-year extension** to Sept. 30, 2035 (§ 301(a)(7), amending 6 U.S.C. 1510(a)).
- **New defined terms in 6 U.S.C. 1501:** "artificial intelligence" (borrowing 15 U.S.C. 9401), "critical infrastructure" (borrowing 42 U.S.C. 5195c(e)), and "Sector Risk Management Agency" (borrowing 6 U.S.C. 650) — § 301(a)(1).
- **AI carve-out (three places):** Nothing in the Act "precludes the use of artificial intelligence that is developed or strictly deployed for cybersecurity purposes" — inserted at 6 U.S.C. 1503(c)(3)(C) and 1507(c)(4), and referenced in DHS's technical capability at 1504(c)(1)(D). § 301(a)(3), (a)(4)(C), (a)(5).
- **Ransomware and "prepositioning activity"** added throughout the 6 U.S.C. 1508 threat report — § 301(a)(6). Reflects the post-Volt Typhoon threat landscape.
- **Outreach obligation:** DHS must, within 90 days, build and continuously run an outreach plan for small/rural critical-infrastructure operators, with annual briefings to House Homeland and Senate HSGAC (§ 301(a)(4)(C), new 6 U.S.C. 1504(c)(2)(C)–(D)).
- **Copyright added** alongside trade secret in the exemption clause at 6 U.S.C. 1504(d)(1) — protects shared indicators from copyright claims.
- **Antitrust safe harbor** (6 U.S.C. 1507(e)) and **liability shield** (6 U.S.C. 1505) are **not amended** — the extension simply lets them keep operating through 2035.

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** Any entity — no threshold — that shares cyber threat indicators or defensive measures under CISA 2015. In practice, ISACs, cloud providers, telcos, banks, utilities, and increasingly frontier AI labs.
- **Empowered actors:** DHS (Secretary and CISA), Attorney General, Sector Risk Management Agencies (Treasury for finance, EPA for water, HHS for health, etc.).
- **Beneficiaries:** Small/rural critical-infrastructure operators (via outreach), federal SRMAs (via new sharing authority), AI-for-cyber vendors (via the "not precluded" carve-out).

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** "artificial intelligence" — imported from 15 U.S.C. 9401 (NAII Act § 5002), *not* GAAIA § 101. This section pre-dates GAAIA's own AI definitional stack and lives in a Title III silo.
- **Depends on / paired with:**
  - **§ 311** (CISA grants for critical open-source software maintainers) — same cyber-ecosystem play; § 311 assumes a functioning CISA-2015 sharing regime for the maintainers it funds.
  - **§ 321** (GAO report on model weight, data center, and OSS security) — assesses the security posture that this reauth's sharing regime is supposed to support.

## Notable statutory language

> "to preclude the use of artificial intelligence that is developed or strictly deployed for cybersecurity purposes in carrying out the activities authorized under paragraph (1)."
> — new 6 U.S.C. 1503(c)(3)(C), added by § 301(a)(3)(B)(iv).

Load-bearing because it settles, at the statutory level, that CISA 2015's authorizations do not restrict AI-based cyber tooling. Parallel language appears in the construction/preemption section (6 U.S.C. 1507(c)(4)).

## Drafting notes & open questions

- The bill strikes "2026" (not "2025") in 6 U.S.C. 1510(a) — consistent with the stopgap extension Congress passed after CISA 2015's original 2025 sunset. So the practical extension is from the *current* Sept. 30, 2026 sunset out to Sept. 30, 2035, not from a 2025 baseline.
- The section imports "artificial intelligence" from the NAII Act (15 U.S.C. 9401), which is a broader, older definition than GAAIA § 101's frontier-model-focused one. This is fine for cyber-sharing purposes but creates a definitional patchwork within a single bill.
- "Strictly deployed for cybersecurity purposes" (the AI carve-out qualifier) is undefined. A dual-use model that is *also* used for cybersecurity would arguably not qualify. No enforcement or interpretation mechanism is specified.
- The § 105(d)(1) "copyright or trade secret" insertion is quietly consequential — it forecloses a class of IP objections to threat-indicator sharing that hadn't been addressed in the 2015 baseline.

## Policy conversation angles

- **National security:** The core sell. CISA 2015 is the operative statute for public-private cyber threat sharing; without reauth the liability shield and antitrust safe harbor lapse, chilling exactly the sharing the government wants. Ransomware and "prepositioning activity" additions directly track the Volt Typhoon / Salt Typhoon threat picture. This is the section most likely to draw bipartisan support on its own merits.
- **Innovation / anti-patchwork:** The AI carve-out language ("not precluded... for cybersecurity purposes") preempts a narrow reading of CISA 2015 that could have restricted AI-based defensive tooling. Supporters of federal-preemption framing will note this is a targeted, mechanism-specific preemption — distinct from § 121's broad state-law preemption.
- **Honest framing:** This section is somewhat orthogonal to AI. CISA 2015 reauth has been the cyber community's top legislative priority since the 2025 lapse, and the substance would work as standalone legislation. Bundling it into a bipartisan AI vehicle is a smart legislative move (adds Democratic-caucus cyber hawks to the whip count), but Members and staff should recognize the AI framing is partly a hitchhike. The AI-specific carve-outs are real but modest — most of the section is a straight CISA 2015 modernization.
