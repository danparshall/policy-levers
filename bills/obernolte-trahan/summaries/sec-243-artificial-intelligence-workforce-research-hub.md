# SEC. 243. ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE WORKFORCE RESEARCH HUB — summary

**One-line:** Stands up an AI Workforce Research Hub inside DOL within 90 days of enactment to conduct recurring analyses of AI's labor-market impact — but with no new money, and with the whole section sunsetting after 4 years.

## What it does

Within 90 days of enactment, the Secretary of Labor must establish an "Artificial Intelligence Workforce Research Hub" inside the Department of Labor, working in collaboration with the Census Bureau, Bureau of Economic Analysis (both in Commerce), and BLS (§ 243(a)). The Hub must conduct research and "help lead the Federal effort" on evaluating AI's workforce impact, produce recurring analyses, run scenario planning across "a range of potential artificial intelligence impact levels," and generate actionable policy insights for training programs, unemployment mitigation, and other employment impacts (§ 243(b)). Staffing is via detail from federal, private-sector, or state/local sources — the Hub has no independent hiring authority. Funding must come from existing DOL discretionary accounts (ETA, BLS); no new authorization is provided.

## Key provisions

- **90-day standup** after enactment (§ 243(a)).
- **Mandatory duties** (all "shall"): recurring analyses, scenario planning across impact levels, actionable insights for training / unemployment / other employment effects (§ 243(b)).
- **Detail authority** for federal employees including those appointed under § 242 (§ 243(c)(1)), private-sector employees under 5 U.S.C. §§ 3701–3704 (§ 243(c)(2)), and state/local employees under 5 U.S.C. §§ 3371–3376 (§ 243(c)(3)).
- **Funding source:** any DOL amount "made available… for the purpose of providing, or conducting research on, training programs, or for conducting research on employment impacts, including… the Employment and Training Administration or the Bureau of Labor Statistics" (§ 243(d)(1)).
- **No new appropriations:** "No additional amounts are authorized to be appropriated to carry out this section" (§ 243(d)(2)).
- **Four-year sunset:** requirements "shall not apply beginning on the date that is 4 years after the date of enactment" (§ 243(e)).

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** None directly — the Hub is a research entity, not a regulator.
- **Empowered actors:** Secretary of Labor (establishes and runs the Hub); Census, BEA, BLS (statutory collaborators, though the section imposes no reciprocal duty on Commerce). ETA and BLS are named as fungible funding wells.
- **Beneficiaries:** Congress, DOL policymakers, and downstream users of the Hub's outputs — the § 257 policy-options report, § 256 state in-demand occupation lists, and § 254 grantmaking review.

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** "Artificial intelligence" (§ 101(a)(3)). "Secretary" is used unmodified throughout Title II Subtitle B and consistently means Secretary of Labor (cf. § 241(a)(1)).
- **Depends on / paired with:**
  - **§ 241** — public request for comment and expert workshops feed the Hub's initial research agenda.
  - **§ 242** — 20 excepted-service AI experts explicitly eligible for Hub detail (§ 243(c)(1)).
  - **§ 244** — job-to-job worker flows pilot for ≥15 AI-sensitive occupations; primary new data stream the Hub would analyze.
  - **§ 245** — NIST prize competition on task/occupation automation benchmarks.
  - **§ 246** — voluntary AI adoption reporting via BLS.
  - **§ 247** — new/revised AI questions in ABS, CPS, ORS, ATUS.
  - **§ 252** — annual prediction-interval employment forecasts for AI-sensitive occupations.
  - **§ 257** — the Hub itself (with BLS) authors the AI Workforce Policy Options Report. This is the section's biggest single deliverable and is unscheduled by § 243.

## Notable statutory language

> "The Hub shall conduct research, and help lead the Federal effort, to evaluate the impact of artificial intelligence on the workforce… The Hub shall produce recurring analyses, conduct scenario planning for a range of potential artificial intelligence impact levels, and generate actionable insights to inform policy…" (§ 243(b))

> "No additional amounts are authorized to be appropriated to carry out this section." (§ 243(d)(2))

> "The requirements of this section shall not apply beginning on the date that is 4 years after the date of enactment of this Act." (§ 243(e))

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **Coordinating shell, not new capacity.** § 243(d)(2) is the tell. Combined with the detail-only staffing model (§ 243(c)), the Hub is legally a coordinating overlay atop ETA/BLS, not a new research entity with its own line item. The section-by-section paraphrase — "The Hub conducts recurring analyses… conducts scenario planning… generates actionable policy insights" — describes ambitions the funding model cannot underwrite unless DOL reallocates existing appropriations toward the Hub, which is a discretionary choice each fiscal year, not a statutory floor.
- **No publication schedule.** "Recurring analyses" is undefined — no cadence (annual, quarterly), no format, no publication venue, no dissemination requirement. Contrast § 252, which specifies annual forecasts with 2-/4-/8-year horizons, prediction intervals, and a public machine-readable archive.
- **No reciprocal duty on Commerce.** § 243(a) requires the Hub to work "in collaboration with" Census and BEA, but imposes no corresponding obligation on the Secretary of Commerce to detail staff, share microdata, or prioritize Hub requests. Data-access is the operative constraint on any labor-market research capability and this section does not touch it. (§ 247 handles new survey questions but on a 1-year timeline separate from the Hub.)
- **Sunset undermines longitudinal work.** The 4-year sunset (§ 243(e)) is a hard cliff. Longitudinal AI workforce measurement — the thing this Hub is nominally for — needs a decade-plus time series to distinguish signal from cyclical noise. A 4-year Hub that dies before its first panel refresh cannot deliver the epistemic product the section describes. Either this is a placeholder awaiting a reauthorization vehicle, or the drafters expect the § 257 policy-options report (due before sunset) to consume the Hub's output and render it disposable.
- **"Help lead the Federal effort"** (§ 243(b)) is imprecise. Neither the White House OSTP AI role nor Commerce's existing AI economic-measurement work is displaced or subordinated. Interagency lead authority is asserted without a mechanism.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Worker / labor:** The Hub is the analytic keystone of the whole GAAIA labor-data architecture — §§ 244, 246, 247, 252 generate the inputs; § 257 is the flagship output. If the Hub is under-resourced (as § 243(d)(2) essentially guarantees absent DOL reallocation), the entire measurement stack degrades to whatever ETA and BLS were already doing, dressed up with a coordinating letterhead. Real independent capacity would require an authorization + appropriation and removal of § 243(e).
- **Innovation / anti-patchwork:** Federal-preemption framing does not load here — this section neither preempts state action nor limits it. States and localities can detail staff in (§ 243(c)(3)) but nothing prevents parallel state labor-market AI monitoring.
- **Safety / catastrophic-risk:** Loads only weakly. "Scenario planning for a range of potential artificial intelligence impact levels" (§ 243(b)) is the one hook for high-displacement scenarios, but the section imposes no requirement to model tail cases — a Hub that scenario-plans only within a ±10 % employment band would satisfy the text.
