# SEC. 132. AI IMPERSONATION OF FEDERAL OFFICIALS — summary

**One-line:** Adds an AI-use sentencing enhancement to 18 U.S.C. § 912 (impersonating a federal officer or employee) — quadruples the individual fine cap to $1M but leaves the three-year prison cap unchanged.

## What it does

Amends the venerable federal impersonation statute (18 U.S.C. § 912) by tacking a conditional clause onto the existing penalty phrase: if the § 912 violation "is committed with the assistance of artificial intelligence," the maximum fine rises to $1,000,000 and the maximum term of imprisonment is "not more than three years" (§ 132). The elements of the underlying offense — falsely assuming or pretending to be a federal officer or employee and either acting as such or demanding money/documents/things of value — are untouched. "Artificial intelligence" is pulled by cross-reference from § 5002 of the National AI Initiative Act of 2020 (15 U.S.C. § 9401), the broad OECD-derived machine-based-system definition.

## Key provisions

- **Fine cap: $1,000,000** for AI-assisted violations (§ 132). Current § 912 defaults to the title 18 fine schedule at 18 U.S.C. § 3571 — $250,000 for individuals, $500,000 for organizations — so the enhancement is roughly a 4x jump for individuals.
- **Prison cap: unchanged at 3 years** (§ 132) — same as the base § 912 penalty.
- **AI definition by reference:** § 5002 of the National AI Initiative Act (15 U.S.C. § 9401), which reaches text chatbots, voice cloning, image/video generation, and general-purpose ML systems alike; there is no requirement that the AI produce synthetic media of the impersonated official's likeness.
- **Predicate is § 912's existing scope:** any "officer or employee acting under the authority of the United States or any department, agency or officer thereof" — includes elected, appointed, and career civil-service personnel; excludes state, local, tribal, and private-sector figures.

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** Individual bad actors who use AI tools while impersonating federal personnel — a scam-caller cohort, not AI developers. § 132 imposes no duty on model builders, deployers, or platforms.
- **Empowered actors:** DOJ (existing § 912 enforcement authority); no new agency role.
- **Beneficiaries:** Federal agencies whose personnel are impersonated (IRS, SSA, FBI, ICE, USCIS — the traditional impersonation-scam targets).

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** "Artificial intelligence" via 15 U.S.C. § 9401 (§ 5002 of NAIIA 2020) — not the § 101 definitions of this bill, so the frontier-developer thresholds do not apply.
- **Companion section:** § 131 (AI Fraud Deterrence subtitle) applies a parallel AI enhancement to mail, wire, bank, and money-laundering statutes (fine caps up from $1M to $2M).

## Notable statutory language

> "…or, in the case that such violation is committed with the assistance of artificial intelligence (as such term is defined in section 5002 of the National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act of 2020 (15 U.S.C. 9401)), shall be fined not more than $1,000,000, or imprisoned not more than three years, or both." (§ 132, inserted into 18 U.S.C. § 912)

"With the assistance of artificial intelligence" is undefined — the section does not specify whether the AI must have generated deceptive content (deepfake voice/video) or whether any AI use in the course of the scheme (e.g., a chatbot drafting the pretext email) triggers the enhancement.

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **Prison cap unmoved.** If the drafting theory is that AI makes impersonation more dangerous and scalable, only the fine moves — the incarceration lever, which typically drives deterrence for individual scammers with limited assets, is unchanged. This is a strange asymmetry given § 131's parallel doubling.
- **Federal-only.** Section-by-section calls this "impersonation of government officials," but 18 U.S.C. § 912 is federal-only. State officials, election officials at the state/local level, and private figures (the Biden-robocall and celebrity-deepfake fact patterns) are outside scope. Election-impersonation deepfakes targeting local officials fall through this gap.
- **"With the assistance of" is load-bearing and undefined.** Prosecutors will need to argue what quantum of AI involvement qualifies — voice-cloned call, yes; generic LLM-drafted email pretext, unclear. Expect judicial construction to do the work Congress declined.
- **No mens rea addition.** § 912 is a general-intent statute; there is no requirement that the defendant know the AI tool was AI, only that they used it in committing the impersonation.

## Policy conversation angles

- **National security / fraud:** Cleanest sell — IRS/SSA/FBI impersonation scams targeting seniors are a bipartisan concern, and voice-cloned "your grandson was arrested" calls are the archetypal use case. Ask offices to confirm they see the federal-only scope as sufficient given the prevalence of state-official and election-official deepfake incidents.
- **Free speech / civil liberties:** Low-risk section on its own — § 912 already requires false pretense plus action or demand, so parody and commentary remain protected. Contrast with adjacent Subtitle E (§ 141) work if offices raise concerns.
