# SEC. 256. UPDATE OF STATE IN-DEMAND INDUSTRY SECTOR AND OCCUPATION LISTS — summary

**One-line:** For five years post-enactment, WIOA Title I-B states and local workforce boards "shall consider" the AI-related labor-market forecasts produced under Subtitle B of Title II when refreshing their in-demand industry/occupation lists, backed by $3M in technical assistance over FY26–30.

## What it does

Beginning no later than one year after enactment and continuing for the next four years, every state and every local workforce development board that receives Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) Title I-B funds must "consider" the AI-workforce information and forecasts generated under Subtitle B of Title II (the Bureau of Labor Statistics and AI Workforce Research Hub outputs commissioned upstream in this same subtitle) when updating the state's in-demand industry sector and occupation lists defined in WIOA § 3 (29 U.S.C. 3102). The Secretary of Labor "shall" provide technical assistance — resource compilations, data-harmonization guidance — to help states plug the new federal forecasts into existing list-update workflows. Congress authorizes $3,000,000 total for FY 2026–2030 to carry the section out.

## Key provisions

- **Trigger window:** one year post-enactment through year five (§ 256(a)) — hard five-year sunset built in.
- **Covered actors:** every state and every local board receiving WIOA subtitle B of title I funds (§ 256(a)) — effectively all 50 states plus DC/territories plus ~550 local workforce development boards.
- **Operative verb:** "shall consider" (§ 256(a)) — the weakest mandate shape in the drafter's toolkit.
- **Technical assistance:** Secretary "shall" provide compilations, harmonization guidance, and "other guidance as appropriate" (§ 256(b)).
- **Appropriation:** $3,000,000 for FY 2026–2030 (§ 256(c)) — roughly $600K/year across all states and local boards combined.

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** All WIOA Title I-B recipient states and local boards. No revenue/size threshold — the WIOA funding relationship is the hook, and it covers essentially the entire state and local public workforce system.
- **Empowered actors:** Secretary of Labor (technical assistance role only; no enforcement authority created).
- **Beneficiaries:** Workers in AI-exposed occupations, in theory — if the federal forecasts actually change what training programs states fund. In practice, uncertain (see below).

## Cross-references

- **Depends on / paired with:**
  - § 244 (BLS AI workforce data collection) — a source of the "information or forecasts" that must be considered.
  - § 246 (AI Workforce Research Hub) — another upstream producer of the forecasts.
  - § 252 (state occupational data grants) — upstream data infrastructure.
  - Without §§ 244/246/252 producing usable forecasts on time, § 256 has nothing to make states consider.
- **Statutory anchor:** WIOA § 3, 29 U.S.C. 3102 (definition of "in-demand industry sectors and occupations"); WIOA Title I subtitle B (29 U.S.C. 3151 et seq.) sets the population of covered recipients.

## Notable statutory language

> "shall consider the information or forecasts produced under subtitle B of title II when updating its lists of in-demand industry sectors and occupations" (§ 256(a))

The verb is load-bearing. "Consider" is met by any documented review — a state can note the federal forecast, reject it, and remain compliant.

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **No enforcement hook.** WIOA Title I-B has no "you must actually incorporate this data" mechanism; there is no cure period, no clawback, no reduction in formula funds for states that "consider then discard." Compare § 111-style civil penalties elsewhere in the bill — § 256 has none of that architecture.
- **Technical assistance is mandatory in verb but discretionary in scope.** "Shall provide" attaches to "compilations of resources, information on harmonization of data, and other guidance as appropriate" (§ 256(b)) — the "as appropriate" swallows most of the obligation.
- **$3M / 5 years / all states + ~550 local boards is trivial** — roughly $12K per state per year for federal technical assistance, before any pass-through. Not enough to fund a single FTE at Labor to staff the effort, let alone state-side capacity.
- **Sunset without a review clause.** The mandate expires in year five with no requirement that Labor or GAO evaluate whether states actually changed their lists. The next Congress inherits no evidence base for extending or hardening it.
- **Silent on local boards' independent duty.** Subsection (a) names both states and local boards, but subsection (b) technical assistance flows only to "States." Local boards get no direct federal support to do the work.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Worker / labor:** Supporters will describe this as "wiring AI-forecast data into every state's in-demand list." Honest read: it invites states to look at the data. A stronger version would tie a percentage of Title I-B formula funds to demonstrable incorporation of AI-forecast occupations into the in-demand lists — or require the Secretary to publish an annual scorecard of which states materially updated their lists post-forecast. As drafted, states retain full discretion to rubber-stamp the same list they had last year.
- **Innovation / anti-patchwork:** Not much loaded here — the section reinforces the existing state-run WIOA architecture rather than federalizing it.
- **State AG / enforcement:** No cause of action, no state-AG role, no private right.
