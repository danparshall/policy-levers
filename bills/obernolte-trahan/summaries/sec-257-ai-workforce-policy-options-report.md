# SEC. 257. AI WORKFORCE POLICY OPTIONS REPORT — summary

**One-line:** Directs the AI Workforce Research Hub (with BLS) to publish, within 18 months of enactment, a public report inventorying existing federal authorities to address tech-driven workforce disruption, mapping gaps against the pace of AI disruption, and presenting a menu of policy options drawn from domestic and international precedents — covering UI, portable benefits, tax code, and wage insurance.

## What it does

Not later than 18 months after enactment, the AI Workforce Research Hub established in § 243, "in consultation with the Bureau of Labor Statistics," must publish a public report (§ 257(a)(1)). The report has four mandatory contents: (A) an inventory and assessment of existing federal statutory authorities and administrative mechanisms for technology-driven workforce disruption; (B) a gaps analysis between those authorities and "the pace of workforce disruption attributable to the adoption of artificial intelligence technologies" (§ 257(a)(2)(B)); (C) an assessment of economic and tax policy mechanisms — enumerated as UI and other wage-replacement, portable benefits, Internal Revenue Code provisions covering retraining / reemployment / income support, and "other social safety net mechanisms" (§ 257(a)(2)(C)); and (D) a range of policy options drawn from domestic precedents, international precedents (current or past), and contemporary proposals — with UI modernization, portable benefits, wage insurance, and retraining/transition support explicitly named (§ 257(a)(2)(D)).

## Key provisions

- **18-month deadline** for a single public report (§ 257(a)(1)).
- **Authors:** AI Workforce Research Hub (§ 243) in consultation with BLS.
- **Explicit fiscal-policy toolbox named:** UI modernization, portable benefits, IRC provisions on retraining/reemployment/income support, wage insurance, "other social safety net mechanisms" (§ 257(a)(2)(C)–(D)).
- **"International precedents, either current or past"** (§ 257(a)(2)(D)(ii)) — a deliberate door to Nordic flexicurity, German Kurzarbeit, and comparable non-US models.
- **No appropriations** in § 257 itself; the Hub must execute this on the zero-new-money mandate of § 243(d)(2).
- **No recommendation requirement** — the section asks for a "range of policy options," not a preferred design.

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** None — this is a report.
- **Empowered actors:** AI Workforce Research Hub (Secretary of Labor); BLS as consulted party.
- **Beneficiaries:** Congress and executive-branch policymakers looking for a pre-vetted menu of AI-workforce responses; researchers and worker advocates who gain a citable federal document that legitimizes wage insurance / portable benefits / international models as options on the table.

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** "Artificial intelligence" (§ 101(a)(3)). "Secretary" (contextually Secretary of Labor throughout Title II Subtitle B; cf. § 241(a)(1)).
- **Depends on / paired with:**
  - **§ 243** — the Hub that must author this report. § 243(d)(2)'s "no additional amounts are authorized" means this deliverable is unfunded overhead on DOL discretionary accounts. § 243(e)'s 4-year sunset gives the Hub a runway only slightly longer than § 257's own 18-month deadline.
  - **§ 255** — the rapid AI adjustment assistance program study. § 255 is program design for a single new intervention (with $1M authorized over FY26–30 and 2-year publication); § 257 is the broader policy-landscape survey covering existing authorities plus the full menu.
  - **§ 244, § 246, § 247, § 252** — labor-market data streams the Hub would draw on for the gaps analysis in § 257(a)(2)(B).

## Notable statutory language

> "an analysis of gaps between those existing authorities and mechanisms and the pace of workforce disruption attributable to the adoption of artificial intelligence technologies" (§ 257(a)(2)(B))

> "portable benefit arrangements that are not tied to a single employer and that follow individuals across jobs, industries, and forms of work" (§ 257(a)(2)(C)(ii))

> "a range of policy options for addressing the gaps identified pursuant to subparagraph (B), including options drawn from — (i) domestic precedents … (ii) relevant international precedents, either current or past; and (iii) categories of mechanisms reflected in contemporary policy proposals …" (§ 257(a)(2)(D))

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **Menu, not recommendation.** The statute requires "a range of policy options" — it does not require the Hub to recommend one, cost them out, or rank them. That is the pivotal design choice. A menu format lets Congress cherry-pick; it also lets the report sit on a shelf. Compare § 255, which is scoped tightly enough (a single program's design questions) that its output is directly actionable.
- **Unfunded by design.** § 257 authorizes no appropriations of its own, and § 243(d)(2) explicitly bars new money for the Hub. The report's depth is therefore capped at whatever ETA/BLS discretionary staff time DOL chooses to reallocate. A gaps analysis of the entire federal workforce-adjustment stack is a serious research undertaking — plausibly infeasible at zero marginal budget without contracting out.
- **Timing tension with the Hub sunset.** § 243(e) sunsets the Hub 4 years after enactment. § 257 requires publication at 18 months. The Hub can therefore deliver the report, but the follow-through — Congress asking clarifying questions, requesting updates, or commissioning implementation studies against the menu — has ~30 months of Hub life remaining before the authoring entity dissolves.
- **"Contemporary policy proposals" is undefined.** § 257(a)(2)(D)(iii) opens the door to UBI, sovereign wealth funds, and job guarantees as much as it opens the door to UI modernization. What actually appears depends on DOL / Hub discretion. Congressional oversight during drafting will matter more than the text does.
- **No BLS reciprocity.** § 257 puts BLS in a consultative role only. No matching duty is placed on BLS to prioritize the report or share microdata beyond what standard interagency practice already allows.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Worker / labor:** § 257 is the flagship policy deliverable of Title II Subtitle B — the "what should we actually do" document. Its value hinges on whether it produces a menu Congress picks from or a bookshelf report cited only in future white papers. Two mitigations worth pushing during markup: (1) require cost estimates and distributional analysis for each option, and (2) require an accompanying recommendation, or at minimum a shortlist. Without either, the section's teeth match its funding — none.
- **Innovation / anti-patchwork:** Neutral. § 257 neither preempts state workforce experimentation (states running their own portable-benefits pilots retain full authority) nor limits federal action.
- **Safety / catastrophic-risk:** Loads weakly but nontrivially. If the Hub interprets "the pace of workforce disruption attributable to the adoption of artificial intelligence technologies" (§ 257(a)(2)(B)) to include high-displacement scenarios — the tail cases the § 243 scenario-planning mandate hints at — this report is the natural place for a serious federal document to discuss automation-response mechanisms at scale. If the Hub interprets it as steady-state churn, the tail case will not appear.
