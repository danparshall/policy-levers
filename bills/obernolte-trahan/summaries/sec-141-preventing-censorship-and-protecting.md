# SEC. 141. PREVENTING CENSORSHIP AND PROTECTING FREE SPEECH — summary

**One-line:** Directs the Secretary of Commerce to study federal-agency "jawboning" of AI platforms on content moderation, information prioritization, and output generation, and to deliver a report — including recommendations for a statutory private-redress cause of action — within one year of enactment.

## What it does

Study only, with a mandated recommendation package attached. Within 180 days of enactment, the Secretary of Commerce must open a study (§ 141(a)(1)) on federal-agency interactions with AI platforms covering content moderation, information prioritization, and output generation, their First Amendment impact, and the role of "jawboning." Within one year of enactment, the Secretary must deliver a report to House Energy & Commerce and Senate Commerce (§ 141(b)(1)) that includes explicit "[r]ecommendations for legislation to provide individuals with means to seek redress" from federal agencies for censoring or suppressing lawful expression on AI platforms (§ 141(b)(2)(D)). No private right of action is created by § 141 itself; the section builds the record for one.

## Key provisions

- **Study scope (§ 141(a)(1)(A)–(G)):** federal-agency interactions on content moderation, information prioritization, output generation; First Amendment impact; jawboning's role in amplifying, enforcing, or mitigating effects; legal-framework gaps; agency transparency and documentation practices; technical feasibility of auditing/tracing moderation decisions attributable to jawboning; effects on lawful expression from perceived or actual jawboning.
- **Consultation (§ 141(a)(2)):** AI platform developers listed first, then civil-liberties/free-speech orgs, academics in constitutional and administrative law, SMEs, consumer advocates, and federal-agency heads.
- **Report contents (§ 141(b)(2)):** risk from jawboning; remedies "available to an individual who alleges jawboning"; statutory/enforcement gaps; and recommendations for legislation covering standards for finding jawboning, injunctive/damages/declaratory relief, agency transparency and recordkeeping requirements, oversight roles (including IGs and independent review bodies), frivolous-litigation protections, and "[c]onsiderations related to national security, public safety, and lawful government speech" (§ 141(b)(2)(D)(vi)).
- **Whose conduct is in scope:** all "Federal agenc[ies]" as defined by 5 U.S.C. § 551 (§ 141(c)(2)) — every executive-branch entity except those § 551 excludes (Congress itself, federal courts, D.C. courts, and a handful of enumerated bodies). Executive-branch actors including agencies with IC or DHS equities are covered.
- **"AI platform" is broad (§ 141(c)(1)):** any computational system that "generates, curates, ranks, recommends, or moderates" — sweeps beyond frontier LLMs to recommender systems and moderation stacks.
- **"Jawboning" is broad (§ 141(c)(3)):** "official or unofficial" federal efforts to influence, pressure, coerce, or induce restriction/suppression/promotion/alteration of lawful expression, including via "[d]irect or indirect communication," "[a] threat of regulatory action or another adverse consequence," or "[a]n implied expectation relating to Governmental authority."

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** none directly — study-only. If Congress adopts the eventual recommendations, federal executive-branch agencies become the regulated party (unusually for GAAIA, which mostly regulates industry).
- **Empowered actors (interim):** Secretary of Commerce, as study author.
- **Beneficiaries (framed):** individuals alleging censorship of "lawful expression" on AI platforms — the recommendations must include standards, remedies (injunctive, damages, declaratory), oversight, and transparency mechanisms tailored to them.

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** § 141 supplies its own definitions (§ 141(c)) rather than borrowing from § 101 — "AI platform," "Federal agency," "jawboning," and "legal framework" are local. The § 101 taxonomy of "frontier developer" / "covered model" does not govern § 141.
- **Depends on / paired with:** self-contained. No downstream section fires off the § 141 report; the report is designed as raw material for a follow-on bill.

## Notable statutory language

> "[R]ecommendations for legislation to provide individuals with means to seek redress from the Federal Government for an action of a Federal agency in censoring or suppressing lawful expression on such a platform or dictating, influencing, or materially altering information provided by such a platform." (§ 141(b)(2)(D))

> "'[J]awboning' means an official or unofficial effort by an officer, employee, or agent of the Federal Government, to influence, pressure, coerce, or induce an individual or private sector entity to restrict, suppress, promote, or alter lawful expression or content of such individual or private sector entity … including through … [a] threat of regulatory action or another adverse consequence … [or] [a]n implied expectation relating to Governmental authority." (§ 141(c)(3))

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **"Lawful expression" carries the load.** The redress recommendation runs only to "lawful expression" (§ 141(b)(2)(D)) — CSAM, true threats, and other categorically unprotected speech are outside the frame by definition. But whether biological-uplift instructions, election-interference synthetic media, or targeted harassment count as "lawful" for redress purposes is left to the eventual recommendations. § 141(b)(2)(D)(vi) instructs the Secretary to include "[c]onsiderations related to national security, public safety, and lawful government speech" — a partial safety carve-out awareness, but soft (a consideration, not a limit).
- **"Unofficial effort" + "implied expectation" is a broad trigger.** The jawboning definition reaches informal contacts and implicit signaling of regulatory leverage, not just formal takedown demands. Depending on how "implied expectation" is operationalized, routine agency-industry meetings on abuse-mitigation could qualify.
- **No private right of action here.** § 141 is study-and-recommendation; any cause of action requires follow-on legislation. Members should not describe § 141 as "creating" a redress right.
- **Timeline realism.** 180 days to open, one year to report — tight for a study consulting seven categories of stakeholder, and Commerce is not the natural home for First Amendment doctrine (DOJ/OLC would be). No funding is appropriated in § 141.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Free speech / civil liberties:** § 141 is the *Missouri v. Biden* / *Murthy v. Missouri* concern ported from social-media platforms to AI. Fifth Circuit held for plaintiffs on jawboning claims before SCOTUS remanded on standing in 2024. § 141 does not resolve that doctrinal fight — it builds a legislative record to codify what the Court declined to constitutionalize. Civil-liberties groups (FIRE, ACLU, NCLA) will read this favorably.
- **Safety / catastrophic-risk:** cuts against the frame that federal agencies should be able to compel or coax platform-level content controls on high-risk outputs (CBRN uplift instructions, targeted disinformation, non-consensual intimate imagery). The § 141(b)(2)(D)(vi) "considerations" instruction acknowledges the tension but does not resolve it. Safety advocates should note that the redress recommendations, if enacted, would apply to *executive-branch requests* — not to statutorily authorized rulemaking (e.g., a Congress-enacted CBRN-output prohibition would not be jawboning). The distinction between "agency pressure" and "statutory command" is where safety-side interventions land.
- **National security:** IC/DHS engagement with AI companies on foreign influence operations, CSAM, and terrorism content is squarely inside the study scope (5 U.S.C. § 551 covers DHS; IC components mostly do too, with narrow exceptions). This is either a healthy transparency exercise or a chilling-effect problem depending on the reader.
- **Industry positioning:** § 141 gives AI companies rhetorical cover to refuse informal federal requests — a real interest for frontier developers who currently field a steady stream of agency inquiries on abuse mitigation. That interest may or may not align with their long-term equities on formal safety regulation.
