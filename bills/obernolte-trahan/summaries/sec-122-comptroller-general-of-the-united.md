# SEC. 122. COMPTROLLER GENERAL IDENTIFICATION OF RISKS AND OBSTACLES RELATING TO AI AND FEDERAL AGENCIES — summary

**One-line:** Directs GAO to deliver Congress a one-year report cataloging federal statutes, regulations, and voluntary standards that impede AI innovation — with an explicit spotlight on infrastructure and energy rules — and to recommend legislative or administrative changes to speed innovation.

## What it does

Within one year of enactment (§ 122(a)), the Comptroller General must submit a report to Congress on "regulatory impediments to innovation in artificial intelligence systems." The report catalogs federal statutes, regulations, and federally-influenced voluntary standards affecting AI innovation and competition; evaluates federal AI adoption; and closes with recommendations for legislative or administrative action "to increase the rate of innovation in artificial intelligence systems" (§ 122(b)(5)). No rulemaking, sunset, or automatic follow-on is triggered by the findings.

## Key provisions

- **One-year deadline** from enactment for the GAO report (§ 122(a)).
- Report must include federal statutes/regulations that "directly affect innovation" and cross-size competitive ability (§ 122(b)(1)(A)).
- Report must cover federally-contributed voluntary standards and best practices (§ 122(b)(1)(B)).
- Evaluation of federal AI adoption progress and use of AI to improve government services (§ 122(b)(2)).
- Examples of AI-assisted service delivery "including toward combating fraud" (§ 122(b)(3)).
- Dedicated call-out for "Federal statutes and regulations that relate to infrastructure and energy and unduly burden artificial intelligence systems" (§ 122(b)(4)).
- Recommendations for legislative or administrative action to increase the innovation rate (§ 122(b)(5)).

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** None directly — this is a study section, no new obligations on private actors.
- **Empowered actors:** GAO (report author); Congress (recipient); indirectly, any executive-branch office likely to be targeted by follow-on deregulatory legislation.
- **Beneficiaries:** AI developers and infrastructure operators (energy, siting, permitting) whose burdens the report is designed to surface; agencies looking for cover to expand AI adoption.

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** "artificial intelligence system" — presumably per the definitions subtitle in § 101 (not verified against § 101 in this pass).
- **Depends on / paired with:** Reads as the deregulatory pressure valve paired with the regulatory buildout elsewhere in Title I. Section-by-section (line 63) groups it as "GAO Report on Regulatory Impediments to AI Innovation" — matching that framing.

## Notable statutory language

> "Federal statutes and regulations that relate to infrastructure and energy and unduly burden artificial intelligence systems." (§ 122(b)(4))

The word "unduly" is doing work here — it pre-loads the report's framing toward finding burdens rather than assessing them neutrally.

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **Study-only, no teeth.** There is no trigger obligating Congress or agencies to act on the recommendations, no sunset of identified statutes, no report-and-repeal mechanism. GAO reports are advisory; this one is no exception.
- **Framing is one-directional.** § 122(b)(5) asks for recommendations "to increase the rate of innovation" — not for balanced recommendations that might, e.g., identify oversight gaps or find that a burden is well-calibrated. The scope forecloses conclusions that any current regulation is appropriately protective.
- **No consultation requirement.** GAO is not directed to consult labor, civil-society, or safety-focused stakeholders — only to catalog burdens.
- **"Companies of all sizes" hedge** (§ 122(b)(1)(A)) gestures at competition/small-business concerns but doesn't require the report to distinguish burdens that fall differentially on incumbents vs. entrants.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Innovation / anti-patchwork:** This is the section supporters will cite as evidence GAAIA is not just regulatory buildout — a formal GAO cataloging exercise gives Republicans a deregulatory story to point to during floor debate. Honest framing: for a bipartisan bill with this much new authority elsewhere in Title I to hold Republican votes, it needs a deregulatory vehicle, and § 122 is it.
- **Safety / catastrophic-risk:** Effectively neutral in the near term (no obligations), but the one-directional framing of recommendations means the report cannot recommend strengthening oversight even where GAO might find it warranted.
- **National security:** Energy/infrastructure emphasis (§ 122(b)(4)) aligns with the "beat China on datacenter buildout" framing that dominates 2026 AI-competitiveness discourse. Expect the report to become an anchor for follow-on permitting-reform legislation.
