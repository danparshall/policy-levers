# SEC. 241. INFORMATION COLLECTION AND DISCUSSION — summary

**One-line:** Requires Labor to run a 60-day public comment window on implementing the Act's data/forecasting/workforce tools, then convene an initial expert workshop and annual follow-ups that must critically evaluate BLS's AI-workforce assumptions and produce a Congress-facing report naming at least five datasets or metrics worth building within two years.

## What it does

Within 45 days of enactment, the Secretary of Labor posts a request for comment on how to implement the Act's data-collection, forecasting, and workforce-notification tools; comments run 60 days and are posted publicly within 120 days (§ 241(a)). Within 180 days the Secretary convenes an initial multi-stakeholder workshop covering the RFC topics, and within 45 days of the workshop submits a report to Congress that summarizes the discussion and identifies at least 5 datasets, metrics, or analyses producible within 2 years that would offer the highest value at reasonable cost (§ 241(b)(1)(G)(i)(II)). At least one annual workshop follows each fiscal year (§ 241(b)(2)). All of this sunsets 5 years after enactment (§ 241(c)) and is funded at $2M for FY26–FY30 (§ 241(d)).

## Key provisions

- RFC topics include cost-effective researcher access to Census/BLS microdata (§ 241(a)(3)(B)), AI-workforce forecasting best practices (§ 241(a)(3)(C)), and value-of-information ranking (§ 241(a)(3)(E)).
- Initial workshop invitee list is prescriptive: economists; academic/nonprofit AI technical experts; industry and labor tech experts; scored-forecasting/expert-elicitation practitioners; and reps from Census, OSTP, CEA, CAISI, BEA, labor orgs, State/Federal grant entities, WIAC, and GAO (§ 241(b)(1)(B)).
- **Viewpoint-representation mandate:** Secretary "shall include experts who hold materially different perspectives on the important disagreements or uncertainties … such as the degree and rate of artificial intelligence's impact on labor demand" (§ 241(b)(1)(C)).
- **Critical evaluation exercise:** at least one workshop exercise must critically evaluate BLS's analytical assumptions on AI-workforce impact (§ 241(b)(1)(E)).
- Workshop participants "shall seek to quantify or rank" expected value of information (§ 241(b)(1)(F)).
- Report is public on the day of Congressional submission and disseminated to every state workforce agency (§ 241(b)(1)(G)(ii)).
- Subsequent annual workshops **waive** the composition and viewpoint-representation requirements (§ 241(b)(2)).

## Who it affects

- **Empowered actors:** Secretary of Labor (convenes, determines participants, writes report); indirectly BLS (whose assumptions are up for critique).
- **Beneficiaries:** researchers seeking microdata access, state workforce agencies (get the report), labor economists doing AI-impact forecasting, and anyone downstream of the tools § 244 / § 246 / § 251 / § 252 will build.

## Cross-references

- **Depends on / paired with:** § 244 (job-flow statistics for AI-sensitive occupations), § 246 (data-sharing), § 251 (WARN Act amendments adding 29 U.S.C. 2102(e) AI-related statements), § 252 (occupational prediction intervals, including the § 252(b)(4) benchmark forecast).
- **External statutes referenced:** Privacy Act of 1974 (5 U.S.C. 552a); WIOA (29 U.S.C. 3101 et seq.).

## Notable statutory language

> "The workshop shall feature at least one exercise to critically evaluate the analytical assumptions of the Bureau of Labor Statistics regarding the impact of artificial intelligence on the workforce." — § 241(b)(1)(E)

> "The Secretary shall include experts who hold materially different perspectives on the important disagreements or uncertainties … such as the degree and rate of artificial intelligence's impact on labor demand." — § 241(b)(1)(C)

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **"Critical evaluation" — teeth or theater?** The bill requires the exercise but not any output from it. No requirement that BLS respond, no requirement that identified assumption problems appear in the report to Congress, no requirement that BLS revise anything. Whether this bites depends entirely on who the Secretary invites and whether the workshop report chooses to name specific BLS models (e.g., the Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics projections, the Employment Projections program's implicit AI-substitution assumptions). The statutory hook exists; the actionable output does not.
- **Report has one real output requirement.** § 241(b)(1)(G)(i)(II) requires identification of ≥5 datasets/metrics/analyses producible within 2 years — this is the one place the workshop is forced to commit to something specific. But nothing in § 241 requires Labor, BLS, Census, or Congress to actually build any of the five. It's a wish list with no appropriation attached.
- **Secretary controls participation.** § 241(b)(1)(B) says the Secretary "may limit the workshop to invited participants" and "shall invite" the enumerated categories — but within each category the Secretary picks. No obligation to invite specific outside institutions (e.g., no requirement that the labor-org reps come from actual unions rather than pro-employer 501(c) groups labeled as "labor organizations"). Viewpoint diversity is required (§ 241(b)(1)(C)) but is self-certified by the Secretary — no external review of whether "materially different perspectives" were actually included.
- **Annual workshops drop the guardrails.** § 241(b)(2) explicitly waives the composition (B) and viewpoint-diversity (C) requirements for subsequent workshops. The Secretary "shall determine the participants … including choosing participants with diverse viewpoints" — but with (B) and (C) waived, this is discretionary language, not a mandate. Under a hostile Secretary, workshops 2–4 could become invitation-only echo chambers before the 5-year sunset.
- **5-year sunset (§ 241(c))** vs. the § 244 pilot and § 252 forecasting program, which are ongoing — the deliberative process ends before the data programs it's meant to inform mature.
- **$2M / 5 years (§ 241(d))** is $400K/yr — enough to run a workshop with catering and honoraria; not enough to fund the underlying data work the workshop identifies. Which is consistent with this being a scoping mechanism, not a production one.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Worker / labor:** This is the deliberative front-end of the whole Subtitle B labor-data buildout. Its real value is procedural: it forces a public record of what the AI-workforce measurement gaps are, and forces at least one on-the-record challenge to BLS's assumptions. That's more than most study sections in AI bills deliver. But the mechanism relies entirely on the Secretary of Labor exercising good faith in participant selection — the statutory guardrails are weak enough that a Secretary uninterested in real critique could stage-manage the outcome. **Push:** Canary should watch (a) who Labor invites to the initial workshop, (b) whether the "5 datasets" list in the report is specific enough to appropriate against, and (c) whether the annual-workshop composition drift starts in year 2.
- **Innovation / anti-patchwork:** Supporters will frame this as evidence-based policymaking — Labor listens before regulating. It's low-risk for industry: no mandates flow from the workshop, only a report.
- **Safety / catastrophic-risk:** Largely orthogonal — this is labor-market measurement, not model-risk work. The one bridge is § 241(b)(1)(G)(i)(II)'s reference to "model capability benchmarks" as an eligible metric category, which technically lets the workshop recommend capability-benchmark investments through a labor-econ vehicle.
