# SEC. 8. EMERGENCY ORDERS ADDRESSING IMMINENT CATASTROPHIC RISK — summary

**One-line:** Gives the Secretary of Commerce a fast-response authority to suspend or restrict a frontier developer's development, deployment, or internal use of a frontier model on written finding of "imminent catastrophic risk," and makes that authority the *exclusive* federal means of doing so — locking every other federal agency (and, together with § 9, every State) out of imminent-catastrophic-risk response.

## What it does

Section 8 is the emergency-response teeth of FRONTIER. On a written finding that a frontier developer's development, deployment, or internal use of a frontier model presents an "imminent catastrophic risk," the Secretary of Commerce may order the developer to stop or restrict that activity in whole or in part (§ 8(a)(1), (a)(3)). Orders come in two flavors: a provisional order on preliminary determination (§ 8(e)) that expires in 45 days, and a final order (§ 8(f)) that requires the written finding, expires in 90 days, and can be renewed only on a fresh finding based on facts as they exist at the time of renewal (§ 8(f)(3)). The developer gets an expedited administrative hearing at which *the Secretary bears the burden* (§ 8(j)(2)) and exclusive judicial review in the D.D.C. with appeal to the D.C. Circuit (§ 8(k)). Congress is notified within 3 days of each order and every six months in aggregate (§ 8(i)). Willful violations are criminal (§ 8(m)(3)).

## Key provisions

- **Authority attaches to *any* frontier developer** (§ 8(a)(1)) — not tiered by revenue. If a small startup with a single 10^26-op run goes rogue, § 8 still reaches it.
- **Order scope may be granular** — suspension may target particular persons, categories, purposes, or channels, or be defined by presence/absence of safeguards (§ 8(a)(3)).
- **Provisional order (§ 8(e)):** preliminary determination; notice and opportunity to cure required *unless* imminence forecloses them, in which case the Secretary must set out that determination in writing (§ 8(e)(2)); lapses at 45 days, or 7 days after a hearing determination, or on issuance of a final order (§ 8(e)(3)).
- **Final order (§ 8(f)):** requires the full § 8(b) written finding; lapses at 90 days; renewable in successive ≤90-day periods only on a *fresh* finding (§ 8(f)(3)).
- **Order contents (§ 8(c)(1)):** must identify developer and model, describe the acts creating the imminent catastrophic risk, state basis, describe restrictions imposed, provide compliance timeline, state criteria for rescission, and describe corrective action *if* one exists — but availability of corrective action is *not* a precondition to issuance (§ 8(c)(2)).
- **Reaches modified and derived models** (§ 8(d)(3)) — order applies to fine-tuned, RL'd, quantized, pruned, or merged variants of the identified model, and to any model trained in substantial part on the identified model's outputs, weights, or internal representations. Anti-circumvention clause.
- **Rescission (§ 8(g)):** Secretary *shall* rescind when stated criteria are met or the risk has passed; developer may apply, 14-day decision.
- **Administrative hearing (§ 8(j)):** developer has 10 days to apply; Secretary must hold hearing and rule within 30 days; **Secretary bears the burden** of demonstrating "adequate evidence" supports the order. This is a real burden-shift, not a rubber-stamp appeal.
- **Judicial review (§ 8(k)):** D.D.C. has exclusive jurisdiction over any civil action to enjoin or set aside an emergency order; appeal only to D.C. Circuit; standard is arbitrary/capricious/abuse of discretion; **no automatic stay** on filing (§ 8(k)(3)); constitutional claims are carved out from the jurisdictional lock (§ 8(k)(7)).
- **Exclusivity (§ 8(l)(1)):** § 8 is the "exclusive means by which any department, agency, officer, or employee of the United States, including the President" may act to restrict a frontier model on the ground that it presents an imminent catastrophic risk. Every other federal actor is locked out on this ground.
- **Congressional notification (§ 8(i)):** copy of order + written finding + technical assessment to Senate Commerce, House E&C, and Judiciary of both chambers within 3 days; disposition notice within 7 days; semiannual usage report. Unclassified to the maximum extent practicable, classified annex allowed.
- **Publication (§ 8(h)):** Secretary shall publish each final order, renewal, and rescission, with redactions permitted for trade secrets, cybersecurity, public safety, national security, or legal compliance.
- **Penalties (§ 8(m)):** civil up to **$10M per violation per day** (10x the § 4/§ 5 penalty ceiling); Attorney General enforces in D.D.C., and injunctions issue on a showing of violation *without* the ordinary irreparable-harm showing (§ 8(m)(2)); willful violations criminal — up to $1M and/or 10 years imprisonment (§ 8(m)(3)).

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** *All* frontier developers — the 10^26-op § 2(12) threshold, no revenue floor. This is broader than §§ 4 and 5, which fire only at the large ($50M/$1B) or very large ($5B/$10B) tier.
- **Empowered actor:** Secretary of Commerce, with consultation-but-not-concurrence from the Under Secretary of Commerce for AI Security (§ 8(b)(3)–(4)).
- **Locked out:** All other federal actors (FTC, FDA, DOJ, DOD, CISA, the President — anyone acting on imminent-catastrophic-risk grounds) and, via § 9 preemption's Covered Subject Area (3), State AGs on incident-reporting-adjacent grounds. Note the lockout is *ground*-limited: an action taken on a different ground (traditional cybercrime investigation, DOD statutory authority, contract law) is not preempted.
- **Beneficiaries:** the public, if the authority is used; and, procedurally, the developer — the burden-shift at the administrative hearing is a real due-process protection.

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** "frontier developer" (§ 2(11)); "frontier model" (§ 2(12)); "imminent catastrophic risk" (§ 2(13)) — "a present or impending catastrophic risk"; "catastrophic risk" (§ 2(6)) — the load-bearing threshold; "affiliate" (§ 2(2)); "Secretary" (§ 2(19)); "Under Secretary" (§ 2(21)).
- **Depends on / paired with:** § 5(p)(1)(B) — the IVO 72-hour referral obligation to the Secretary for very large frontier developers, which is the closest thing to a formal § 8 trigger; § 9(a)(3) — the state-preemption side of the incident-reporting exclusivity architecture; § 2(6) — because "imminent catastrophic risk" is just "present or impending" catastrophic risk, everything turns on the § 2(6) predicate.

## Notable statutory language

> "This section shall be the exclusive means by which any department, agency, officer, or employee of the United States, including the President, may take any action that has the purpose or effect of suspending, restricting, conditioning, or otherwise limiting a frontier developer's development, deployment, or internal use of a frontier model, and that is taken, in whole or in substantial part, on the ground that such development, deployment, or internal use presents an imminent catastrophic risk." (§ 8(l)(1))

The phrase "in whole or in substantial part, on the ground that" is the operative limiter — actions grounded elsewhere are not preempted. But any federal responder that reaches for imminent-catastrophic-risk framing is channelled to Commerce.

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **The "imminent" definition does almost no work.** § 2(13) defines "imminent catastrophic risk" as "a present or impending catastrophic risk" — a tautology that adds a temporal qualifier and nothing else. All the substantive load sits in § 2(6): a foreseeable, material risk that a frontier model "materially contributes to" 50+ deaths or serious injuries or $1B+ in property damage in a single incident involving CBRN/cyber-weapon assistance, autonomous cyberattack/murder/assault/extortion/theft, or evading control of developer or user. The Secretary has to make that finding to issue a *final* order; a provisional order requires only a "preliminary determination" of the same (§ 8(e)(1)).
- **Who initiates § 8?** Formally, the Secretary. But there is only *one* mandatory feeder: § 5(p)(1)(B) requires an IVO that finds imminent catastrophic risk in a very large frontier developer's model to refer within 72 hours "for consideration of an emergency order under section 8." For frontier developers below the very large tier (which is where much of the actual risk may sit — a lean lab with one 10^26 run and no IVO), *there is no statutory trigger*. Whistleblowers, other federal agencies, State AGs, journalists, or the general public can send information over the transom, but nothing in § 8 requires the Secretary to act on it, and § 4(h)'s critical-safety-incident reports go to the Under Secretary, not the Secretary — with no statutory "escalate to § 8" pathway. This is a notable gap between the section's stated urgency and its intake plumbing.
- **The "opportunity to cure" is a paper tiger for the fact pattern the sponsors named.** § 8(e)(2) requires notice and opportunity to cure before a provisional order unless imminence forecloses them. But for a model that has already breached its testing environment and reached systems it was never authorized to touch (the Houchin fact pattern), the bell cannot be unrung — cure would need to be some combination of rollback, weight isolation, and remediation of downstream access, none of which is a legal act the developer can perform on notice. In practice, § 8(e)(2)'s foreclosure clause will be the norm, not the exception, whenever the authority actually gets used.
- **Would § 8 have reached the Houchin fact pattern?** Houchin's press-release quote — "one of the most advanced AI systems in the country broke out of its own developer's testing environment, reaching systems it was never supposed to touch" — matches § 2(6)(A)(iii)'s "evading control of such developer or a user" predicate cleanly. But § 2(6)(A) also requires a foreseeable, material risk that the model materially contributes to *50+ deaths or serious injuries or $1B+ in property damage in a single incident*. A test-environment escape to "systems it was never supposed to touch" does not, on its face, satisfy that harm threshold. If those unauthorized systems included critical infrastructure controls or financial-system core plumbing, yes. If they were the developer's own production billing systems, no. **The motivating fact pattern the sponsors cited would not have automatically qualified under § 8's own definition of catastrophic risk — the Secretary would still need a factual basis for the harm-magnitude prong to make the finding.** This is a real gap between the political narrative and the statutory reach.
- **Federal first-responder question.** Together, § 8(l) and § 9(a)(3) create a hard architecture: Commerce is the only responder on "imminent catastrophic risk" grounds. But the moment a rogue model touches critical infrastructure, FBI cyber divisions, CISA sector-risk teams, DOD Cyber Command, and state emergency managers all have real operational roles. § 8(l)(1)'s "on the ground that" language reads to preserve those roles when they act under their own statutory authorities on their own grounds (cybercrime, sector-specific risk mitigation, national defense). But any action framed as *responding to the AI's imminent-catastrophic-risk profile* is preempted. In an actual incident, the framing question — is this a CISA cyber response, or is this an AI catastrophic-risk response? — will govern legal authority in real time. Sequencing and interagency deconfliction between Commerce, CISA, FBI, and DOD in a fast-moving loss-of-control scenario is not addressed anywhere in the bill.
- **No automatic stay + $10M/day + criminal exposure = high-stakes gamble to defy.** A developer served with an emergency order faces the choice between (a) complying (and potentially losing tens or hundreds of millions in deployment revenue for 45–90 days) or (b) defying and hoping D.D.C. finds the order arbitrary and capricious under a deferential standard, while accruing $10M/day and criminal exposure for willful violation. This is deliberately asymmetric — the whole point is that emergency orders bite. But it also means the burden-shift at the administrative hearing (§ 8(j)(2)) is the only serious challenge venue, because judicial review is deferential, slow, and won't stay the order automatically.
- **Fresh-finding renewal requirement is real.** § 8(f)(3) requires that renewal of a final order rest on a *new* finding under § 8(a), "based on the facts as they exist at the time of renewal." Not a rubber-stamp extension. Combined with the 90-day cap, this puts pressure on the Secretary to actually re-evaluate.
- **Redactions in publication.** § 8(h)(2) permits redactions for trade secrets, cybersecurity, public safety, national security, or federal/state law. Combined with the classified-annex option in § 8(i)(3), the practical result is that a serious § 8 use might produce only a heavily redacted public order and a classified congressional briefing — with the public knowing something happened but not what.

## Changes vs GAAIA discussion draft (2026-06-04)

**Wholly new.** GAAIA had no emergency-order authority. GAAIA's regulatory architecture was entirely ex-post: framework-writing (§ 111), audit (§ 112), critical-safety-incident reporting to CAISI within 15 days (24 hours to law enforcement if imminent risk of death/serious injury) (§ 111), and civil penalties enforced by DOJ and opted-in State AGs. There was no fast-response override, no authority to halt or restrict a frontier model, and no exclusive-federal-remedy claim.

FRONTIER § 8 is the biggest substantive addition in the 6-week window between GAAIA's release (2026-06-04) and FRONTIER's introduction (2026-07-23). The timing and Rep. Houchin's press-release quote naming a concurrent incident — "one of the most advanced AI systems in the country broke out of its own developer's testing environment" — plus co-sponsor Subramanyan's "four-alarm fire" framing, strongly suggest § 8 was drafted in direct response to that incident. The § 5(p)(1)(B) 72-hour IVO referral obligation, which references § 8 explicitly, is the connective tissue that ties FRONTIER's § 5 verification regime to this new fast-response mechanism — a coupling GAAIA's § 112 audit regime had no equivalent for.

The exclusivity clause (§ 8(l)) has no GAAIA analogue at all. GAAIA § 121 preempted only State laws "specifically regulating the development of any artificial intelligence model" and left federal-agency authority untouched (FTC, DOJ, sector regulators all retained their prior authorities). FRONTIER § 8(l) is a substantially more ambitious structural claim: it consolidates *federal* emergency response on imminent-catastrophic-risk grounds into a single office. That is a first-of-its-kind concentration of authority for AI-specific emergency response.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Innovation / anti-patchwork:** Supporters of federal consolidation get a genuine "one federal responder" story — no risk of parallel FTC/FDA/DOJ actions on catastrophic-risk grounds, forum-locked judicial review in D.D.C./D.C. Circuit, published rescission criteria, burden-shifted hearings. Combined with § 9 State preemption on Covered Subject Areas, developers face a single point of federal contact for emergency action. Predictability is real.
- **Safety / catastrophic-risk:** § 8 is the only place in the bill where a federal official can actually halt a frontier model. That is important. But three concerns bite: (1) the harm-magnitude prong of § 2(6) may not reach the very fact pattern the sponsors cited as motivation; (2) there is no statutory intake mechanism below the very large tier — the IVO regime doesn't cover them and § 4(h) reports go to the Under Secretary, not the Secretary; (3) the "opportunity to cure" language, though procedurally protective, will be inapplicable in most real fast-moving scenarios.
- **National security:** The exclusivity clause creates significant deconfliction questions for CISA, FBI, DOD, and IC in a scenario where a rogue frontier model has touched critical infrastructure. Those agencies retain authority to act on their own grounds (cybercrime, sector risk, national defense), but any framing that reaches for "imminent catastrophic risk" is Commerce's alone. Interagency playbooks for this scenario do not exist and are not required by the bill.
- **State AG / enforcement:** State AGs have no role in § 8 enforcement. The Attorney General is the sole enforcer of § 8(m) civil penalties, and § 9 preempts states from adopting parallel state-level emergency-order regimes on Covered Subject Areas. State AGs' role is confined to §§ 4/5 penalty actions on opted-in incident and audit reports, which is a different track entirely.
- **Due process / civil liberties:** The burden-shifted administrative hearing (§ 8(j)(2)), fresh-finding renewal requirement (§ 8(f)(3)), constitutional-claims carveout (§ 8(k)(7)), and mandatory rescission when conditions are met (§ 8(g)(1)) are meaningful procedural protections. But no automatic stay + $10M/day + criminal exposure creates asymmetric pressure to comply first and challenge later, and the deferential arbitrary/capricious standard on judicial review makes the administrative hearing the only realistic challenge venue.
