# SEC. 7. GAO REPORT ON THE MARKET FOR INDEPENDENT VERIFICATION ORGANIZATIONS — summary

**One-line:** Directs the Comptroller General to report annually to House Energy & Commerce and Senate Commerce on the IVO market, IVO independence from the AI industry, and how to preserve that independence.

## What it does

Section 7 imposes a recurring reporting duty on GAO tied directly to the § 5 IVO regime. Starting one year after enactment and every year thereafter (§ 7(a)), GAO must deliver a report to the two authorizing committees covering (1) the state of the IVO market — number of licensees, capacity to meet assessment demand, barriers to entry (§ 7(b)(1)); (2) the degree to which IVOs are actually independent of the AI industry and any factors threatening that independence (§ 7(b)(2)); and (3) recommendations for legislative or administrative changes to preserve independence (§ 7(b)(3)). GAO cannot revoke IVO licenses, impose penalties, or block a specific audit engagement — the section produces information, not consequences.

## Key provisions

- **Annual cadence** — first report due 1 year after enactment, then annually (§ 7(a)). Persistent oversight rather than a one-shot snapshot.
- **Two-committee referral** — House Energy & Commerce and Senate Commerce, Science, and Transportation (§ 7(a)).
- **Independence assessment mandatory** — GAO must evaluate industry independence and flag threats to it (§ 7(b)(2)).
- **Recommendations must accompany findings** — legislative or administrative (§ 7(b)(3)); Congress is the intended actor on any remedy.

## Who it affects

- **Regulated parties:** None directly. IVOs (licensed under § 5) and the very large frontier developers that retain them are the subjects of study, but the section imposes no new duties on either.
- **Empowered actors:** GAO (new recurring workload); House E&C and Senate Commerce (recipients).
- **Beneficiaries:** Congress, the Under Secretary, and downstream observers (press, civil society) who gain a public, recurring diagnostic on whether the IVO regime is being captured.

## Cross-references

- **Defined terms used:** "Independent verification organization" — relies on the § 5 licensing regime; § 7(a) references IVOs "licensed under section 5."
- **Depends on / paired with:** § 5 (creates the IVOs to be studied); § 3(b) (Under Secretary's IVO licensing rulemaking, on a 180-day clock, is what populates the market GAO reports on). Without § 5 firing, there is no market to report on.

## Drafting notes & open questions

- **No operative consequence.** GAO reports are information products. If GAO documents systemic capture — e.g., all licensed IVOs are subsidiaries of Big Four firms whose consulting arms sell frontier developers eight-figure engagements — nothing in § 7 requires anyone to act. Any remedy runs through separate legislation, § 3 rulemaking, or § 5 licensing decisions by the Under Secretary. This is a partial mitigation, not a fix.
- **Two-committee referral assumes bicameral alignment that has not happened.** FRONTIER is House-introduced only as of 2026-07-23; the press release names no Senate companion. Directing GAO to report to Senate Commerce presupposes Senate jurisdiction the bill has not yet actually claimed. This is a common drafting move (write for the eventual conference posture) but worth flagging — if a Senate companion lands in a different committee (e.g., HSGAC, Judiciary), the referral clause will need to move.
- **Capture is the load-bearing risk.** The whole § 5 regime rests on IVOs being genuinely independent of the developers they audit. Section 7 is a recognition-of-problem clause; the harder question — what triggers an intervention when GAO documents capture — is unanswered.

## Changes vs GAAIA discussion draft (2026-06-04)

**New in FRONTIER; no GAAIA analogue.** GAAIA did not create an IVO regime and therefore had nothing corresponding to § 7. GAAIA's GAO-report provisions were topical one-offs on unrelated matters: § 122 (federal regulatory impediments to AI adoption), § 321 (model weight, data center, and open source security), and § 424 (liquid cooling). None recurred annually and none touched auditor independence.

FRONTIER § 7 is a structurally different design: it pairs the new § 5 IVO regime with a recurring, targeted oversight mechanism aimed at the regime's most predictable failure mode. Positive addition. Canary should support it and should push for a companion trigger — e.g., a statutory duty for the Under Secretary to respond within X days to any GAO finding of systemic capture — to close the "recommendation with no consequence" gap.

## Policy conversation angles

- **Safety / catastrophic-risk:** The § 5 IVO regime is the FRONTIER Act's principal check on very large frontier developers. Its credibility collapses if IVOs are captured. § 7 is the only structural mechanism in the bill that surfaces capture risk publicly; safety-focused audiences should treat it as necessary-but-insufficient and press for teeth in follow-on legislation.
- **Regulatory capture / auditor-independence precedent:** The Big Four / SEC audit history and the credit-rating-agency history before 2008 are the reference class. Both regimes documented capture repeatedly in oversight reports without triggering structural reform until crisis forced it. § 7's design assumes Congress will act on GAO findings; the historical prior is that it often does not.
- **Innovation / market-structure:** § 7(b)(1) requires GAO to report on "barriers to entry" and IVO capacity. This is a useful hook for developer-side and small-lab advocates worried that IVO scarcity becomes a bottleneck on deployment. The same reporting requirement can be cited from both directions.
