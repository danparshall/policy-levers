# Paper Summaries

### Armstrong, Bostrom & Shulman (2013) — "Racing to the Precipice: a Model of Artificial Intelligence Development"
**File:** `Armstrong_Shulman__2013--racing_to_precipice.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Armstrong_Shulman__2013--racing_to_precipice.txt` | **Pages:** 8 | *FHI Technical Report #2013-1, October 2013, Future of Humanity Institute, Oxford University* | Stuart Armstrong, Nick Bostrom, Carl Shulman. Summarized: Sonnet 4.5, 2026-09-06.

Canonical simple game-theoretic model of an AI development arms race. **Setup:** n teams each have capability c drawn i.i.d. uniformly on [0, μ] and choose a safety level s ∈ [0, 1]. The team with the highest (c − s) "wins" by building the first AI; conditional on winning, the project succeeds with probability s (utility 1) and produces an AI-disaster with probability 1 − s (utility 0). Each team assigns utility 1 − e (with e ∈ [0, 1] the *enmity* between teams) to another team's successful project. Teams pick s at a symmetric Nash equilibrium. **Three information regimes** are solved in closed form: no information (nobody knows any capability), private information (each team knows only its own), public information (everyone knows everyone's). Higher μ — capability mattering more than risk-taking — always lowers disaster probability; higher enmity and (in most regimes) more teams raise it.

**Central counterintuitive finding — information hazard.** More capability information *raises* disaster probability. The no-information equilibrium is always at least as safe as either informed regime; for high enmity, private-information ≥ public-information in safety, but for low enmity and low μ, public information can be safer than private. Asymptotically, private-information disaster probability scales as 1/μⁿ while public scales as 1/μ. Intuition: under private information, only low-capability teams take risks, and the winner is low-capability only if every team is (probability ≈ 1/μⁿ); under public information, the top team takes risks whenever the second-ranked team is close (probability ≈ 1/μ). Explicitly framed as an instance of Bostrom's "information hazard" typology.

**Levers.** Capability-importance (μ) is largely fixed by the technology; the paper suggests preferring research paths with low return-to-risk-taking. Enmity is the most tractable — trust-building, tech-sharing, joint projects reduce it, and can support explicit off-Nash agreements ("friendly teams make friendly AIs"). Merging teams reduces n (helpful in nearly all regimes). Information sharing is two-edged: encourage it under low-μ + low-e conditions; lock knowledge away under higher-μ + higher-e conditions.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** The 8-page paper that launched the "AI arms race" framing now used ubiquitously — Aschenbrenner's *Situational Awareness* "we must race" narrative and Anthropic's *When AI Builds Itself* proposal for a "verifiable coordinated slowdown/pause" both live downstream of this model's Nash-equilibrium argument, usually without citation. Two direct policy handles: (i) when a staffer frames US–China competition as strictly zero-sum, this is the compact game-theoretic backbone for "reducing enmity is a lever, not naive cooperation" — enmity is a parameter, and moving it moves the equilibrium; (ii) the information-hazard result cuts against a naive "just require transparency" reflex — under some parameter regimes, mandatory public capability disclosure would *worsen* the race dynamics rather than dampen them (relevant to how FRONTIER § 4(k)(5) developer-registry-disclosure provisions and the voluntary capability sharing envisioned in the Hassabis Standards Body proposal might interact with the underlying incentives).

**Caveats when citing.** (a) Model is deliberately stylized — one-shot, symmetric enmity, uniform capabilities, no uncertainty about safety outcomes; "AI-disaster" is a binary lump-sum event with no gradient of harm. (b) Predates every current frontier lab and every current regulatory regime; the point is qualitative comparative statics on Nash safety, not calibration to a real scenario. (c) The information-hazard result specifically concerns capability information; it does not model information about safety practices, incident histories, or reputational effects — which most real transparency regimes would also communicate. Use as a cautionary lens on capability-only transparency, not as a case against transparency in general.

### Askell, Brundage & Hadfield (2019) — "The Role of Cooperation in Responsible AI Development"
**File:** `Askell_Hadfield__2019--cooperation_responsible_ai.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Askell_Hadfield__2019--cooperation_responsible_ai.txt` | **Pages:** 17 body + refs | *arXiv:1907.04534v1, 10 Jul 2019, "Preprint. Work in progress."* | Amanda Askell (OpenAI; corresponding), Miles Brundage (OpenAI), Gillian Hadfield (OpenAI). Summarized: Opus 4.7, 2026-09-06.

The theoretical companion piece to Armstrong, Bostrom & Shulman 2013 written from inside a frontier lab: responsible AI development is framed as a **collective action problem**, not just a competitive one — companies would themselves prefer a more cautious equilibrium but cannot unilaterally hold it while others race. Section 1 works through the three standard product-safety incentive channels (market forces, liability law, regulation) and argues each is systematically weaker for frontier AI than for pharmaceuticals or aviation: consumers cannot evaluate opaque neural-network systems; providers themselves have limited information about their own systems' risks; harms accrue to third parties and future generations who cannot punish; effective regulation requires technical understanding the regulator does not yet have; and the potential for rapid or discontinuous progress can render market response and liability too slow to catch up. Section 2 builds this into a normal-form cooperate/defect game and identifies **five cooperation-promoting factors**: (1) **High Trust** — confidence others will cooperate (the probabilities *p*, *q* in the payoff matrix); (2) **Shared Upside** — high expected value of mutual cooperation; (3) **Low Exposure** — low cost of being the sucker who cooperates unilaterally; (4) **Low Advantage** — low value of defecting when others cooperate; (5) **Shared Downside** — high cost of mutual defection. Trust and incentives are explicitly not independent — increasing one raises the other, creating virtuous or vicious cycles depending on early moves.

Section 3 translates the factors into four **strategy families**, each mapped back to the factors they move: (i) **Promote accurate beliefs about cooperation opportunities** — correct misconceptions that "safety and security risks can be safely ignored," reduce focus on relative gains vs. absolute gains, and use principles-signing (Asilomar 2017 is the cited example) to establish common knowledge that mutual cautious behavior is preferred; (ii) **Collaborate on shared research and engineering challenges** — pool safety, verification, and "AI for good" work across labs to build technical trust and shared incentive alignment (OpenAI's staged GPT-2 release with partnership-based sharing is offered as an existence proof); (iii) **Open up AI development to appropriate oversight** — publicize decision-making principles, explain publication/release calls, allow external visitors, and use bug bounties + open-sourcing where safe, with the explicit caveat that "full transparency is not called for" (per Bostrom 2017a) and that iterative, gradual ratcheting is more robust than an all-at-once transparency push; (iv) **Incentivize adherence to high standards** — social (valorize/criticize), economic (subsidies for high-value AI), legal (proscribe unsafe development), and **domain-specific** (e.g., early access to the latest generation of computing power). The paper explicitly gestures at "regulatory markets as discussed in Hadfield and Clark (2019)" as the more institutionally ambitious version of strategy (iv) — the acknowledged single largest intellectual inspiration for Ball 2025 later in this collection.

**Notable framing choices.** The paper treats companies as profit-driven "as a worst-case analytical exercise" while flagging repeatedly that this is an abstraction — employees and executives at frontier labs care about downstream harm — and warns that assuming pure profit-maximization can be self-fulfilling. It also acknowledges its own analytical shortcuts: cooperation/defection is a binary, whereas real safety investment is continuous; agents perfectly observe each other's compliance, which is precisely the problem transparency mechanisms need to solve; and the analysis models iterated interactions only implicitly, via a footnote gesture toward the Axelrod (1984) tit-for-tat literature. Explicit worsening conditions for cooperation are named: **close frontrunners** (raise Low Advantage — the payoff gap between cooperating and defecting shrinks as the race tightens, echoing Armstrong et al. 2016 that near-tied competitors take more risks) and **misaligned risk perceptions** (a less-cautious lab assigns lower cost to defection, which reciprocally lowers trust from cautious labs). The paper closes with four future-work questions, of which #2 is "the proper role of legal institutions, governments, and standardization bodies in resolving collective action problems between companies, particularly if those collective action problems can arise between companies internationally" — directly foreshadowing Hadfield & Clark 2023 → Ball 2025.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** Three concrete uses. First, this is the **canonical statement of why frontier-AI cooperation is a collective action problem rather than a coordination failure** — the distinction matters, because coordination failures resolve as soon as one party moves first, while collective-action problems require ongoing mechanism support (self-regulation, third-party verification, aligned legal incentives) to hold the good equilibrium. Second, the **five-factor decomposition** gives a compact vocabulary for evaluating any proposed policy lever: FRONTIER § 4 disclosure/reporting operates primarily on High Trust and Low Exposure (cautious labs can see whether others are actually complying); the Anthropic AAIF's independent-evaluator regime with anti-evaluator-shopping mixer targets Low Advantage; the Anthropic WABI "verifiable coordinated slowdown/pause" ask is a direct implementation of the paper's Shared Upside + Low Exposure move (verification is what makes the "if others also do so" clause credible) — this paper provides the theoretical scaffold Anthropic's proposal sits on, seven years earlier. Third, **the Hadfield author cross-link is unusually load-bearing for this collection**: Hadfield co-authors this paper (2019), Hadfield & Clark's *Regulatory Markets* (2023, the acknowledged single largest inspiration for Ball 2025's private-governance proposal), and Sastry, Heim, Belfield et al. (2024) *Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence* (the foundational compute-governance paper — see summary below). Three of this collection's entries thread through her, and this 2019 paper is the earliest of them; when a staffer asks "who is this Hadfield person the AI governance world keeps citing?", start here.

**Caveats when citing.** (a) The paper is framed as a **preprint / work in progress** and does not receive the treatment of a peer-reviewed theorem — the game-theoretic setup is stylized (one-shot payoff matrix, binary cooperation, perfectly observed compliance) and the five factors are compact but not empirically operationalized against real lab behavior. (b) It is written by three authors at OpenAI in 2019, before OpenAI's for-profit pivot was fully realized and years before the current commercial-race dynamics with Anthropic, Google DeepMind, xAI, and Chinese frontier labs; the paper's implicit "there are just a few labs, and they mostly know each other" premise strains under 2026 conditions with dozens of frontier developers across multiple regulatory jurisdictions. (c) The paper is deliberately silent on the **verifiability problem** — it treats "cooperation" and "defection" as observable, whereas the current live technical-governance question (see Reuel/Trager et al. 2025 Verification capacity; Anthropic WABI's "verifiable" adjective; Sastry et al. 2024 compute-governance detectability property) is precisely how to make cooperation legible without exposing model weights or capability details. Cite as the theoretical frame for the cooperation problem; cite Anthropic WABI, Reuel et al., or Sastry et al. for the verification-mechanism problem it does not solve. (d) Reads as an inside-a-lab argument for why labs should support governance, not an outside-in argument for what governance should look like; pairs naturally with Ball 2025 (private governance), Ortega 2025 (incident regime), and the Anthropic AAIF (frontier-model regulatory framework), each of which sits downstream of the "we're in a collective action problem" premise but proposes different institutional exits.

### Naudé & Dimitri (2020) — "The race for an artificial general intelligence: implications for public policy"
**File:** `Naude_Dimitri__2020--race_for_agi_public_policy.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Naude_Dimitri__2020--race_for_agi_public_policy.txt` | **Pages:** 13 | *AI & Society 35(2): 367–379, 2020 (online first 22 April 2019); doi:10.1007/s00146-019-00887-x. Earlier version: IZA Discussion Paper 11737, August 2018.* | Wim Naudé (Maastricht University School of Business and Economics + MSM; visiting professor RWTH Aachen; UNU-MERIT), Nicola Dimitri (Department of Economics, University of Siena). Summarized: Opus 4.7, 2026-09-06.

Contest-theory refinement of the Armstrong, Bostrom & Shulman (2013) AGI-race model, explicitly built to answer the unanswered normative questions in that paper: how can government reduce the number of competing teams, raise the importance of capability, and steer the payoff structure? **Setup:** an all-pay contest (Tullock 1980; Konrad 2009; Vojnovic 2015; Siegel 2009 asymmetric extension) between n teams. Each team i chooses an R&D investment xᵢ ≥ 0 and wins the AGI race with probability aᵢ · xᵢ / (bᵢ + xᵢ + Σⱼ≠ᵢ xⱼ), where aᵢ is the maximum achievable success probability (technology-and-knowledge ceiling), bᵢ ≥ 0 is R&D difficulty (a positive bᵢ means even unopposed effort cannot reach aᵢ), and cᵢ is the unit cost of effort. The winner earns 1, everyone else 0, but every team pays cᵢ·xᵢ regardless of outcome. Nash entry is characterized by a **competition coefficient** ρᵢ = aᵢ/cᵢ − bᵢ that summarizes the team's technology, capability, and cost.

**Central proposition (Proposition 1 and Corollary 1).** In this winner-takes-all all-pay contest, only teams with the maximal ρ enter the race: if ρ₁ > max(0, ρ₂), the unique Nash equilibrium is (x₁ = ρ₁, x₂ = 0). Under strict asymmetry a single team races unopposed; under equality among the top k, effort splits among those k and the remaining n − k invest zero. Because AGI is a "hard" problem — high bᵢ, expensive c, uncertain a — the paper argues the endogenous number of serious contenders is small: **"perhaps only half a dozen or so"** globally, borne out empirically (in the paper's 2017–2018 window) by concentration of US ML patenting in Amazon, IBM, and Google (Webb et al. 2018). This is the paper's central rebuke to Armstrong et al.'s "too many teams" worry: the market naturally selects for a small n, so the control-problem lever "reduce number of teams" partially self-executes.

**Two extensions.** (i) *Goal heterogeneity* (Proposition 2): if the most-competitive team pursues profit maximization rather than probability-of-winning, its equilibrium effort drops, allowing a less-competitive team back into the race — so a firm-level corporate-governance choice (profit vs. mission) can change the number of racers. (ii) *Intermediate prize* (§ 4.1): allowing a "second prize" 0 < α < 1 for advanced-but-non-dominant AI, with probability dᵢ · xᵢ / (bᵢ + xᵢ + Σⱼ≠ᵢ xⱼ), raises the effective ability parameter to áᵢ = aᵢ + α·dᵢ and the modified competition coefficient ρ́ᵢ = áᵢ/cᵢ − bᵢ > ρᵢ. **Second prizes therefore raise both investment and the probability that the dominant AGI is found** — which the paper flags as making public control more, not less, urgent.

**Four policy levers derived from the model.**
1. **Introduce an intermediate prize** (§ 4.2.1). Rather than a ban (deemed infeasible), the state creates second prizes for advanced-but-safe AI. Effect: raises investment among the small set of competitive teams; reduces the marginal team's ability to afford entry.
2. **Public procurement of innovation** (§ 4.2.2). The implementation vehicle for (1). Two mechanisms: (a) *engineering constraints into AI* via Responsible Innovation (Foley et al. 2016) and Value Sensitive Design (Umbrello 2019; Johri & Nair 2011) — internal constraints on the AGI's own values/goals, external constraints on labor-displacement vs. labor-augmentation orientation (Chalmers 2010); (b) *coordination* — EU pre-commercial procurement (EC 2007) and innovation-partnership (EC 2014) as templates for a public-sector-driven consortium that internalizes the "AGI is a single-best-effort global public good" character (Barrett 2007). In the limit this amounts to **nationalization** of AGI development.
3. **Tax AI** (§ 4.2.3). A friendliness-graded tax rate t(f) = 1 − f, where f ∈ [0, 1] indexes government-assessed friendliness, on any team that produces the dominant AGI. Effect: shrinks the competition coefficient to δᵢ = (1−t)·aᵢ/cᵢ − bᵢ, discouraging investment; at high enough t (specifically t > 1 − bᵢ·cᵢ/aᵢ), the team drops out entirely. The paper flags the standard global-coordination caveat (tax havens, cross-border race-to-bottom) but argues that because meaningful AGI research is concentrated in a handful of countries (WIPO 2019), the short-term need for global coordination is less acute than for legacy corporate taxation.
4. **Address AI-generated patents** (§ 4.2.4). If an AGI's own inventions can be patented, patent protection amplifies the winner's first-mover advantage sharply, raising ρᵢ and the equilibrium number of racers. Framed as unresolved and flagged for future research.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** Two direct handles for Hill outreach. (i) When a staffer or advocate cites Armstrong et al. 2013's "race to the bottom" concern as reason to fear many racers, Naudé & Dimitri give the **countervailing endogenous-selection argument**: winner-takes-all + non-trivial R&D cost + hard problem → small n as an equilibrium property, not a policy target. This does not dissolve the control problem — Armstrong's information-hazard result and safety-effort tradeoff still bind on whatever small n does enter — but it reframes the policy question from "shrink n" to "shape ρ and the payoff structure of the small n that will race regardless." (ii) The paper is one of the earliest formal economic models to derive **tax + public procurement** as the specific policy instruments for shaping an AI race — a lineage worth carrying alongside Anthropic's Economic Policy Framework (which proposes AI-specific tax bases on tokens/compute/revenue at Tier 3 unemployment thresholds, though for redistribution rather than race-shaping) and Jones (2024) (which proposes an AI compute tax to fund safety research). Naudé & Dimitri's tax rationale is **structural** (change the competition coefficient) rather than **fiscal** (raise revenue), and that distinction is worth carrying when the two families of proposals get conflated. The public-procurement idea also connects naturally to conversations about NIST/DOE-led public AI infrastructure and to Sastry et al. 2024's "collaborating on a joint AI megaproject" mechanism (#8 in their catalogue).

**Caveats when citing.** (a) Static two-team model; the n-team corollary is Nash-identity-splitting among symmetric top-k contestants rather than a genuine dynamic multi-agent contest. (b) The "half a dozen serious contenders" empirical claim is calibrated to 2017–2018 USPTO ML patent data and predates Anthropic, xAI, Mistral, DeepSeek, and the Chinese national-champion buildout — the endogenous-small-n result still holds qualitatively but the specific count should not be quoted as current. (c) The model has teams choose only R&D investment xᵢ; safety effort is *not* a separate strategic variable as in Armstrong et al. So the paper's "public procurement can engineer safety constraints into the winner's AI" argument is asserted rather than derived — it is a claim about what public procurement *can* achieve, not an equilibrium property of the same model. (d) The tax proposal presumes the government can observably index the "friendliness" of an AGI ex post (to set t = 1 − f); this operationalization gap is not addressed and would in practice import all of the AI-evaluation problems catalogued in Zhou et al. 2025 and Reuel et al. 2025. (e) The AI-patenting section (§ 4.2.4) is explicitly incomplete and flagged for future research; do not cite it as a settled policy recommendation.

### Jones (2024) — "The AI Dilemma: Growth versus Existential Risk"
**File:** `Jones_2024__AI_dilemma_growth_vs_risk.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Jones_2024__AI_dilemma_growth_vs_risk.txt` | **Pages:** 11 | *AER: Insights 6(4): 575–590; NBER WP 31837*

Formalizes the trade-off between AI-driven economic growth and existential risk. The model is deliberately simple: using AI for *T* periods raises consumption to c₀e^(gT) but survival probability drops to e^(−δT). The optimal intensity of AI use depends critically on the curvature of the utility function. With log utility (γ = 1), the model is remarkably risk-tolerant — agents would accept up to a one-in-three chance of extinction for 10% annual growth. With CRRA utility at γ ≥ 2, the picture reverses sharply: because utility is bounded and marginal utility falls rapidly, even infinite consumption generates finite utility, making large existential gambles unacceptable — the tolerable extinction probability drops to just 4% at γ=2 and 2% at γ=3 (Table 1, with δ=1%/yr and g=10%).

A key exception: if AI also extends life expectancy, these mortality reductions are "in the same units" as existential risk and don't run into declining marginal utility of consumption, making larger risks bearable even at high risk aversion. The paper also considers singularity scenarios (self-improving AI generating accelerating growth) and finds the bounded-utility result holds — infinite consumption is worth only finite utility. The paper also considers optimal policy responses, arguing for a large tax on AI compute to fund safety research and slow the race. This is the "Oppenheimer question" paper referenced in Jones (2026) — which extends these results to estimate that society is underinvesting in AI safety by a factor of 30 or more — and provides the formal welfare framework for evaluating whether AI development should be slowed or halted on safety grounds.

### Kording & Marinescu (2025) — "(Artificial) Intelligence Saturation and the Future of Work"
**File:** `Kording_2025__AI_saturation_future_of_work.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Kording_2025__AI_saturation_future_of_work.txt` | **Pages:** 50 | *Brookings Center on Regulation and Markets Working Paper, November 2025*

Bridges the gap between AI-expert predictions of explosive growth and economists' predictions of modest impact by separating production into physical and intelligence sectors within a nested CES framework. The key insight is "intelligence saturation": because physical and intelligence outputs are complements (calibrated using Oberfield & Raval's manufacturing elasticity), the marginal returns to intelligence capital saturate no matter how fast AI scales. AI replaces humans in intelligence tasks first (since AI capital costs fall much faster than physical capital costs), pushing workers toward the physical sector. The wage effect is theoretically ambiguous and can be non-monotonic — in the baseline calibration, wages first increase (productivity gains dominate) then decrease (reallocation into physical sector dominates) as automation progresses. A necessary condition for wages to fall is that the employment share in the intelligence sector decreases; this condition is not sufficient because automation can raise output enough to offset the reallocation effect.

The paper provides an interactive simulation tool (code on GitHub) showing how parameter changes shift wage and output trajectories. Key parameter sensitivities: stronger decreasing returns in intelligence dampen positive wage effects; lower within-intelligence task substitutability produces a more pronounced wage hump; higher physical-intelligence substitutability makes wage effects more negative at high automation but output effects more positive. Under rapid AI capital doubling (every 6 months), wages rise monotonically during automation and saturate after full intelligence-task automation — the hump disappears. After full automation, more AI and physical capital both increase wages, but with complementary sectors, the marginal wage impact of AI capital saturates as AI grows large. The framework nests both the economist view (bounded effects from complementarity) and the AI-expert view (explosive growth from high substitutability) as special cases within a shared parameter space, making the debate empirically resolvable.

### Mertens, Thompson et al. (2026) — "Crashing Waves vs. Rising Tides"
**File:** `Mertens_2026__crashing_waves_rising_tides_AI_automation.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Mertens_2026__crashing_waves_rising_tides_AI_automation.txt` | arXiv: 2604.01363 | MIT FutureTech

Preliminary findings from an ongoing large-scale evaluation of LLM capabilities across 3,000+ O*NET-derived tasks, with 17,205 evaluations by domain experts rating outputs from 41 LLMs. The central finding is that AI automation follows a "rising tide" pattern — the logistic relationship between AI success and task duration is surprisingly flat (slope = -0.31 in log-odds), meaning AI performs not dramatically differently on short vs. long tasks. This contrasts with METR's "crashing wave" finding on deterministic software-engineering tasks. At the "minimally sufficient without edits" threshold (score >= 7), the average success rate across all models is 60%, with frontier models in 2024-Q3 already achieving 50% success on tasks taking humans a full day. Success rates improve by 8-11 percentage points annually; the implied doubling time for feasible task duration at a given success rate is 3.8 months.

Key findings: (1) Newer models produce approximately parallel upward shifts in the success-duration curve (beta = -0.31 vs -0.32), meaning improvement is broad-based rather than concentrated on short tasks — consistent with a capability frontier that advances uniformly. (2) Larger models show an outward rotation (beta = -0.36 vs -0.26), gaining more on short tasks. (3) Success-duration slopes vary sharply by O*NET job family: Installation/Maintenance/Repair has highest success (73%) with near-zero slope, while Legal has lowest (47%); only ~25% of families show significantly negative slopes. (4) Extrapolation suggests 80-95% success on most text-based tasks by 2029 at minimally sufficient quality, but near-perfect performance takes much longer due to logistic tail behavior. Important caveats: data collection ongoing, text-only tasks, survey provides all needed information (no real-world integration friction), and "last-mile" deployment costs not captured.

### METR (2025) — "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks"
**File:** `METR_2025__long_tasks_frontier.pdf` | **Text:** `text/METR_2025__long_tasks_frontier.txt` | **Pages:** 45

Proposes the "50%-task-completion time horizon" as a metric: the duration of tasks that AI models can complete with 50% success rate, measured against skilled human baselines. Using 170 tasks across RE-Bench, HCAST, and a new suite of shorter software tasks (SWAA), the authors find that frontier models (as of Claude 3.7 Sonnet) have a 50% time horizon of ~50 minutes. The time horizon has been doubling approximately every 7 months since 2019, though the trend may have accelerated in 2024.

The increase is driven primarily by greater reliability, better error recovery, improved logical reasoning, and enhanced tool use. If the trend generalizes to real-world software tasks, extrapolation predicts AI systems capable of automating many software tasks that currently take humans a month within ~5 years. The paper carefully discusses limitations including external validity and the implications of increased autonomy for dangerous capabilities. This is the best current framework for translating benchmark scores into economically meaningful capability measures.

### Ortega (2025) — "AI threats to national security can be countered through an incident regime"
**File:** `OrtegaA__2025--ai_incident_regime_national_security.pdf` | **Text:** `text/OrtegaA__2025--ai_incident_regime_national_security.txt` | **Pages:** 25 body + refs | *arXiv:2503.19887 v5, 16 Apr 2025* | Alejandro Ortega (Apollo Research; alejandro@apolloresearch.ai). Acknowledged guidance from Charlotte Stix; commenters incl. Kevin Wei, Mia Hoffman, Patricia Paskov, Chloé Touzet, Merlin Stein, Jimmy Farrell, Connor Dunlop.

Proposes a legally-mandated post-deployment AI incident regime focused specifically on national security threats (not the broader "AI harms" surface). Two conceptual moves do most of the work. First, Ortega coins **"security-critical"** as a shared category and argues that four sectors qualify: civilian nuclear power, aviation, life sciences dual-use research of concern (DURC), and frontier AI development — then justifies every design choice in the incident regime by pointing at how the existing regime in one of the other three sectors already does exactly that thing. Second, Ortega proposes a novel operationalization of **"AI incident" = "any event which weakens a claim made in the AI system's pre-deployment national security case"** — a safety-case-derived definition that provides a decision algorithm for whether to report, in contrast to the OECD "serious AI hazard" definition (2024), which Ortega argues is too vague to guide provider decisions (his DDOS-on-water-treatment-plant example: an incident responder cannot tell whether the OECD "could plausibly" threshold has been met without cyber and water-infra expertise on call).

**Three-phase architecture** (each component justified by a comparator regime):
1. **Preparatory Phase.** (a) AI providers must produce a "national security case" — a structured argument (adapted from AI safety cases: Balesni et al. 2024, Clymer et al. 2024, Goemans et al. 2024) documenting evidence that the system does not pose unacceptable increases in biorisk, cyber attack potential, or loss-of-control risk. (b) "Incident" = any event that weakens a claim in the security case. Comparators: NRC reports near-misses (Dominion Energy 2024 secondary-containment 8-hour outage was reported despite no meltdown risk); NIH reports any organism-containment escape; NTSB reports required system malfunctions even without harm.
2. **Rapid Response Phase.** Providers notify a government agency within **24 hours** of discovering an incident (mirroring NTSB's 24-hour aviation reporting rule); agency coordinates emergency containment where appropriate.
3. **Hardening Defenses Phase.** Government agency gets two authorities. (a) **Intelligence-gathering authority**: extensive black-box + some white-box access to the AI system + relevant technical documentation, sufficient to conduct a root-cause analysis. (b) **Security-strengthening authority**: agency mandates changes to all relevant providers' safety/security procedures based on the RCA. Comparators: NTSB investigates → FAA rulemakes; NRC investigates + issues generic communications binding all licensees; NIH Office of Science Policy adjusts NIH Guidelines for Research Involving Recombinant DNA Molecules post-incident.

**Worked scenario** (Section 4): spear-phishing on the electricity grid using a frontier AI whose national security case had claimed cyber-guardrail robustness. Provider detects → notifies at 6h → agency warns grid, shares IP data with law enforcement, containment achieved → 20 days later agency exercises intelligence-gathering authority to review the malicious actor's complete input/output log, identifies a novel LlamaGuard jailbreak (Inan et al. 2023), performs black-box + gradient-based white-box adversarial iteration → exercises security-strengthening authority to mandate (i) all providers' national security cases must now cover spear-phishing capabilities and (ii) all providers must run pre-deployment jailbreak evaluations against a new eval-set the agency constructs from the incident data.

**Explicit scope limits** (flagged for future work): open-weight models (who reports when the base provider isn't the deployer?); pre-public-deployment vs post-deployment incidents (the paper is post- only); evidentiary standard for "weakens a claim"; provider incentive to make as few claims as possible (mitigation: agency reviews national-security cases pre-deployment to ensure high-level claims like "does not pose unacceptable cyber risk" are always present); agency technical capacity to conduct these investigations (footnote 21, explicitly out of scope). Contextual anchor: OpenAI classified GPT-4o (July 2024) as 'low' cyber and CBRN risk; classified o1 (Dec 2024) as 'medium' on both — a **7-month capability jump** cited as motivation for building this infrastructure now.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** Ortega is the closest technical parent for the FRONTIER Act's incident-reporting + IVO + emergency-orders architecture (see `bills/frontier-act/OVERVIEW.md` for the introduced-bill structure). Section correspondences:

- **FRONTIER § 5 (IVO regime)** ≈ Ortega's Hardening Defenses Phase, minus the cross-provider security-strengthening authority. § 5 gives licensed IVOs unredacted access to the very-large developer's materials at any time (Ortega's intelligence-gathering authority), but the "mandate procedural changes across ALL relevant providers" piece is missing — FRONTIER's IVO refers imminent-risk findings to the Secretary (§ 5(p)(1)(B), 72-hour clock) but there is no cross-provider rulemaking hook of the kind Ortega justifies via NRC generic communications / FAA post-incident rulemaking / NIH Guidelines updates. This is an ask-shaped gap.
- **FRONTIER § 8 (Commerce-Secretary emergency orders)** is stronger than anything in Ortega — a fresh authority with no direct analogue in Ortega's proposal (Ortega's agency mostly investigates and recommends; § 8 lets the Secretary suspend). But § 8's magnitude gate (§ 2(6): 50+ deaths OR $1B+ property damage) is high; Ortega's national-security-case + claim-weakening definition of "incident" would fire on many more events than § 8's threshold reaches. Ortega's operationalization is worth citing when arguing for a lower § 8 trigger or (better) a separate ratchet for events that weaken safety-case claims without immediately clearing the magnitude threshold.
- **FRONTIER § 9 (preemption)** and § 8(l) (Commerce-exclusive) together concentrate response authority tightly. Ortega's paper doesn't touch preemption but assumes a single agency runs the regime — consistent architecturally.

Framing hooks: (i) Ortega's **security-critical / nuclear-aviation-life-sciences comparator argument** is Houchin's press-release framing in academic form ("just this week, one of the most advanced AI systems in the country broke out of its own developer's testing environment … this is exactly the kind of incident that shouldn't stay behind closed doors" — 2026-07-23 FRONTIER release). (ii) The **national-security-case operationalization** is the cleanest response to staffers asking "how would providers even know what counts as an incident?" — an anchor for making the case that FRONTIER's § 4(a) framework should require claim-structured content rather than free-form narrative.

Scope-limit gaps and their FRONTIER-Act counterparts: (a) **Whistleblower channel** — Ortega's model presumes providers self-report; he does not discuss whistleblower channels. FRONTIER dropped GAAIA § 113 anti-retaliation protection entirely (`OVERVIEW.md § "No whistleblower title"`), so provider self-reporting is now the *only* channel — an especially load-bearing gap and a live restoration ask. (b) **Open-weight models** — Ortega flags as future work; FRONTIER covers only frontier-model developers, so downstream deployers of open-weight bases inherit no reporting duty. (c) **Pre-deployment incidents** — Ortega covers post-deployment only, and FRONTIER inherits the same limitation despite § 4(g)(2)(B)'s quarterly internal-use catastrophic-risk assessments, which come closest to filling that gap for large developers.

### Parshall & Lopez-Luzuriaga (2026) — "Measuring AI's Economic Reach: A Multi-Dimensional Task Taxonomy"
**File:** `Parshall_Lopez-Luzuriaga_TaskExposure_2026.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Parshall_Lopez-Luzuriaga_TaskExposure_2026.txt` | **Pages:** 49 | *Canary Institute / GWU Center for Economic Research, 2026*

Proposes **CDR**, a three-axis ordinal taxonomy that separates AI exposure into Cognitive complexity (C0–C4), Deployment difficulty (D0–D4), and Regulatory restrictions (R0–R4), extending Autor (2003)'s routine/non-routine × cognitive/manual matrix into a finer 5×5 cognitive–embodiment plane with a regulatory third axis. The core argument is that existing single-score exposure measures (Eloundou, Felten) conflate three dimensions that advance at structurally different rates — AI capability (fast, per METR), physical deployment (medium), and institutional/regulatory barriers (slow) — producing the disagreement Gimbel et al. (2026) observe across metrics for the most-exposed occupations.

Methodological contribution: **Detailed Work Activity (DWA) decomposition** resolves the compound-task problem in O*NET (e.g., "diagnose and repair heating systems" splits into a C2/D0 diagnostic and a C1/D3 repair). Applied via multi-model LLM consensus (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5-mini, Gemini 3 Flash; validated against flagships) to the full O*NET universe: **23,850 DWA-task pairs across 923 occupations**. An additional **19.6%** is cognitively/physically accessible but blocked by regulation (R2 professional standards 11.5%; R3 statutory 8.0%; R4 moral agency 0.1%). Validated against four external datasets: Eloundou human exposure ratings, Carollo licensure data, Career OneStop licensing records, BLS fatal-injury rates. Framework is positioned as a longitudinal instrument: ~$100 per three-model re-run, ~$200 with flagships.

Theoretical motivation draws from Jones & Tonetti (2026) weak-links intuition: under non-linear complementarity, the slowest-advancing CDR axis binds. Structural prediction: as C advances, the binding constraint for more tasks shifts from cognition to deployment/regulation — a prediction single-axis frameworks cannot generate. Consistent with Massenkoff & McCrory (2026), who find 94% theoretical feasibility vs 33% actual Claude deployment for Computer & Mathematical tasks. This paper is framed as the first in a planned research program; companion work will cover adoption dynamics, an F-axis (failure consequences) and verification, and developing-country applications where D0 tasks become tradable.

### Rahman (2026) — "Does Distributed Training Undermine Compute Governance?"
**File:** `RahmanR__2026--distributed_training_compute_governance.pdf` | **Text:** `text/RahmanR__2026--distributed_training_compute_governance.txt` | **Pages:** 13 | *arXiv 2605.29359, 28 May 2026* | Robi Rahman (Machine Intelligence Research Institute; formerly Epoch AI)

Asks whether a well-resourced developer could evade compute-threshold governance by structuring hardware into clusters below monitoring thresholds and training over ordinary internet connections. Answer: yes, at a cost premium of roughly 3x over centralized training, and the paper argues regulations must therefore be designed to detect and prevent illicit distributed training rather than assuming frontier runs require large, detectable facilities. Contribution is threefold: a threat-model simulation, an open-source interactive simulator (intelligence.org/research/distributed-training-simulator) for governance researchers to test their own assumptions, and a countermeasure efficacy assessment.

Methodology: the simulator models an evader restricted to nodes of at most 16 H100-equivalents (the Scher et al. 2025 proposal, the most restrictive in the literature), consumer-grade internet (100 Mbps, 100 ms latency), a 740-day training time limit (from Sevilla et al. 2022), and a government actively monitoring internet traffic, power, water, satellite imagery, and thermal signatures. Efficiency is decomposed as η = η_H (sync-interval penalty, ~2-10%, calibrated to Stich 2019 / Douillard 2023 / Charles 2025) × η_comp (gradient-compression penalty; default 150x compression at η_comp = 0.99, recalibrated upward from 16x after Covenant-72B's published 146x-with-negligible-loss result) × η_rep (replica divergence, the dominant penalty, 0.15-0.90, shrinking with model size) × η_act (activation compression under pipeline parallelism). A Chinchilla quality-adjustment χ separates raw local-equivalent compute from quality-adjusted compute, penalizing the overtraining that small-memory nodes force. Key results (Table 1): 10^24 FLOP local-equivalent for $1.6M on 2 nodes; 10^25 for $30.7M on 34 GH200 nodes; GPT-4-scale (2.1×10^25) for $91.3M on 101 nodes; Llama-3.1-405B-scale (3.8×10^25) for $441.3M on 625 nodes of 50x A100 at η = 0.15 and 26x overtraining; 10^26 for $3.80B on 4,706 pipeline-parallel nodes. Latency is irrelevant (transmission time dominates RTT by orders of magnitude). Countermeasure assessment: chip tracking, whistleblower programs, memory-and-compute limits on unregistered hardware, and conventional intelligence work are effective; bandwidth caps and internet traffic monitoring are not, because determined evaders route around them.

Relevance: this is the quantitative backbone for any Hill conversation about compute thresholds. The three cost figures map directly onto real regulatory anchors ($1.6M evades the Scher 10^24 proposal, $31M evades the EU AI Act's 10^25, $3.8B evades California SB 53's 10^26), which makes the enforcement gap concrete for staffers in one sentence. The whistleblower recommendation connects to the Stop Stealing Our Chips Act (Senate-passed 2026), a live legislative hook. Two internal cross-references: (1) the traffic-monitoring-ineffective finding directly undercuts the network-fingerprint detection idea explored in our earlier distributed-training discussions, and should be treated as the current best evidence against that lever; (2) structurally this is the same detection-vs-custody problem as MacDonald (2021, 2025) on TEL tracking: detection of individual nodes doesn't establish that they are part of a training run, just as detecting a TEL doesn't establish custody. Author's extrapolations are deliberately pessimistic for the evader, and the enforcement gap is described as actively growing with ongoing distributed-training research (Decoupled DiLoCo et al.).

### Aumann & Lindell (2007) — "Security Against Covert Adversaries: Efficient Protocols for Realistic Adversaries"
**File:** `AumannY_LindellY__2007--security_against_covert_adversaries.pdf` | **Text:** `text/AumannY_LindellY__2007--security_against_covert_adversaries.txt` | **Pages:** 63 | *J. Cryptology 23: 281–343, 2010, doi:10.1007/s00145-009-9040-7 — the extended full-version paper. Preliminary version: TCC 2007 (LNCS 4392), 20pp; also IACR ePrint 2007/060. Received Oct 2007, revised Mar 2009, published Apr 2009.* | Yonatan Aumann & Yehuda Lindell (Bar-Ilan University Computer Science; comm. by Oded Goldreich)

Introduces the **covert adversary** threat model in secure multiparty computation — an intermediate rung between the classical **semi-honest** model (parties follow the protocol, only try to learn extra from what they see) and the **malicious** model (parties may deviate arbitrarily). A covert adversary will cheat if cheating pays off, but only if it can avoid being caught. Security is defined relative to a **deterrence factor ε ∈ (0, 1]**: any attempt to cheat is detected by the honest parties with probability at least ε. The definition is simulation-based and takes three formulations (failed-simulation, explicit-cheat, strong-explicit-cheat) forming a hierarchy of guarantees; modular sequential composition theorems are proved for all three. Motivating claim: in commercial, political, and diplomatic settings, entities "cannot afford the embarrassment, loss of reputation, and negative press associated with being caught cheating" — so a threshold detection probability substitutes for the near-certainty demanded by the malicious model.

**Concrete efficiency (Yao-style two-party protocol).** The construction modifies Yao's garbled-circuit protocol so that two garbled circuits are sent and one is randomly opened for verification. Basic construction achieves **ε = 1/2** at only **~2× the work and ~2× the bandwidth of semi-honest Yao** — constant rounds, one oblivious transfer per input bit, communication O(n·|C|) for security parameter n and circuit size |C|. Higher deterrence scales linearly in circuit copies: **10 copies → ε = 9/10**. Intermediate variant requires 2× the OTs of the basic variant; strongest variant needs n OTs per input bit. All three variants are dramatically cheaper than any then-known protocol secure against fully-malicious adversaries — presented as a proof-of-concept that "middle-ground" adversarial modeling can make previously-impractical secure computation practical.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** The covert-adversary frame is the game-theoretic backbone for **any regulatory regime that leans on audit + penalty rather than continuous prevention** — nuclear NRC, FAA post-incident rulemaking, NIH DURC oversight, and by extension the AI-safety incident regimes drawing on those comparators (Ortega 2025, and the FRONTIER Act architecture it informs). Two direct handles: (i) the ε parameter lets a regime designer trade enforcement cost against deterrence explicitly — "what audit frequency, disclosure obligation, and penalty magnitude produce a credible ε against a covert developer?" is a question with mathematical shape rather than only rhetorical shape; (ii) the frame maps cleanly onto **deceptive-alignment** concerns in AI safety — a model that would misbehave if it could avoid detection but complies when it might be caught is a covert adversary in this exact technical sense, and the paper offers a starting vocabulary for making that intuition precise. Useful as a citation whenever a conversation collapses into "we can only assume fully-adversarial developers or fully-cooperative ones"; the paper's core move is that a formal middle exists and admits efficient mechanisms.

### Sastry, Heim, Belfield et al. (2024) — "Computing Power and the Governance of Artificial Intelligence"
**File:** `Sastry_Hadfield__2024--compute_governance_ai.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Sastry_Hadfield__2024--compute_governance_ai.txt` | **Pages:** ~80 body + refs | *arXiv:2402.08797, 13 Feb 2024* | 19 authors across OpenAI, GovAI, Cambridge (Leverhulme CFI, CSER, Bennett Institute), Oxford (Internet Institute, AI Governance Institute), Institute for Law & AI, U. Toronto, Vector Institute, Georgetown, ILINA, HKS, U. Montreal/Mila; 8 starred primary authors (Sastry, Heim, Belfield, Anderljung, Brundage, Hazell, O'Keefe, Hadfield); corresponding authors Sastry (OpenAI), Heim (GovAI), Belfield (CFI). The foundational large-scope paper on compute as an AI-governance lever. Core thesis: **compute is uniquely well-suited among the AI-triad inputs (data, algorithms, compute) as a governance point of intervention** because it possesses four properties that data and algorithms do not — **detectability** (large training runs require industrial-scale power/cooling/networking that is remotely observable), **excludability** (physical hardware can be denied to users in ways non-rivalrous digital goods cannot), **quantifiability** (FLOPs and chip-counts are measurable/reportable/verifiable), and **supply-chain concentration** (chip design, EUV lithography, and fabrication are dominated by very small numbers of firms — TSMC/ASML/NVIDIA-tier). These properties are then leveraged to enhance **three critical governance capacities**: (a) **visibility** — tracking AI development and use; (b) **allocation** — influencing which systems get built and by whom; (c) **enforcement** — ensuring compliance with prohibitions.

**Twelve illustrative policy mechanisms** (author-endorsement varies deliberately; the paper is a design catalogue, not a slate). **Visibility:** (1) public compute-based capability estimation, (2) required training-compute reporting from cloud providers + AI developers, (3) international AI-chip registry, (4) privacy-preserving workload monitoring. **Allocation:** (5) differentially advancing beneficial AI, (6) redistributing AI development across/within countries, (7) changing the overall pace of AI progress, (8) collaborating on a joint AI megaproject. **Enforcement:** (9) compute caps via physical limits on chip-to-chip networking, (10) hardware-based remote enforcement (kill-switches / attestation), (11) preventing risky training via multiparty control, (12) digital norm enforcement.

**Explicit risks + suggested guardrails** (Section 5). Risks: threats to personal privacy, leakage of strategic/commercial information, centralization of power, erosion of fixed thresholds by algorithmic + hardware progress, low-compute narrow models with dangerous capabilities, incentives for diversion/evasion/circumvention/decoupling. Guardrails: (i) **exclude small-scale AI and non-AI compute** from governance (repeatedly emphasized — this is not a controls-on-all-computers regime); (ii) implement privacy-preserving practices/technologies; (iii) use compute-based controls only where ex ante controls are justified; (iv) periodically revisit controlled technologies; (v) substantive + procedural safeguards on all controls. **Appendix A** develops a compute-uranium analogy explicitly (dual-use, physical, excludable, quantifiable, concentrated supply chain, established international regime under IAEA/NPT); **Appendix B** is a research agenda for hardware-enabled governance mechanisms.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** This is the load-bearing intellectual parent for essentially every subsequent compute-governance policy artifact — chip export controls, EO 14110's compute-reporting thresholds, the EU AI Act's 10²⁵ FLOP threshold, and downstream research including **Rahman 2026** (`RahmanR__2026--distributed_training_compute_governance.pdf`), which quantitatively stress-tests the detectability pillar against distributed-training workarounds (finding: the datacenter-footprint argument weakens for distributed setups but the supply-chain-concentration + chip-tracking argument holds up well). When a staffer asks "why compute? why not just regulate the model?" this is the citable answer — Sec. 3.B (Feasibility of Compute Governance) is directly usable. **Author cross-link worth carrying:** Gillian Hadfield is one of the eight starred primary authors on this paper AND the co-author of the "Regulatory Markets" 2023 paper Ball 2025 explicitly names as its single largest inspiration — the same governance scholar sits behind both the compute-based-state-control and the private-regulatory-markets architectures now in circulation.

**Caveats when citing.** (a) The paper is deliberately author-neutral on which mechanisms to adopt ("authors vary significantly in their views of which of these, if any, would be desirable") — treat it as a design catalogue, not an endorsement. (b) Predates SB 1047 (vetoed Sep 2024), EU AI Act final passage, and Rahman's distributed-training empirics — the detectability argument as originally stated (frontier runs *require* large datacenter footprints) is partially contested; the more durable pillars are excludability + supply-chain concentration. (c) Thresholds — the paper is agnostic on numeric thresholds; when you see "10²⁵ FLOP" it comes from downstream policy documents, not directly from here.

### Ball (2025) — "A Framework for the Private Governance of Frontier Artificial Intelligence"
**File:** `BallD__2025--private_governance_frontier_ai.pdf` | **Text:** `text/BallD__2025--private_governance_frontier_ai.txt` | **Pages:** ~30 | *arXiv:2504.11501, April 2025* | Dean W. Ball (Fellow, Fathom; Research Fellow, Mercatus Center; Nonresident Senior Fellow, Foundation for American Innovation). Wide acknowledgements across x-risk / AI-safety / policy-libertarian orbits — commenters incl. Miles Brundage, Nathan Calvin, Jack Clark, Daniel Kokotajlo, Nathan Labenz, Yuval Levin, Samuel Hammond, Adam Thierer, Alan Rozenshtein, Yo Shavit, Thomas Woodside.

Proposal paper for a **hybrid public-private frontier-AI governance regime** built on **regulatory markets** (Hadfield & Clark 2023, cited as the paper's largest single inspiration) plus **tort-liability safe harbor** as the incentive to opt in. Not empirical — an argumentative policy-design paper. Sections 1–3 examine and reject as insufficient (not bad on their own terms) four alternative approaches: use-based regulation, traditional centralized government regulation / standards-setting, compute governance, tort liability alone, and international governance. Section 4 lays out the proposal in structural detail.

**The proposal (§ 4.1), seven-step structure:**

1. Legislature authorizes a government commission to license private AI standards-setting / regulatory organizations. Licenses require technical + legal credibility and demonstrated independence from industry.
2. AI developers opt in to receive certifications from a licensed private body verifying they meet published safety/security technical standards; the private body audits at least annually.
3. In exchange, certified developers receive **safe harbor from all tort liability related to misuse by others** that results in tortious harm.
4. Government body periodically audits and re-licenses each private regulator.
5. Safe harbor does not apply if the developer's own conduct qualifies as reckless, deceitful, or grossly negligent.
6. Private governance body can revoke a developer's safe harbor for non-compliance.
7. Government body can revoke a private regulator's license for negligence (e.g., ignoring developer non-compliance).

**Race-to-the-bottom prevention** (§ 4.2 — the paper's cleverest structural piece): revocation of a private regulator's license does not merely decertify that regulator's future clients — it **retroactively removes tort-liability protection from *all* firms currently certified by that regulator**. So Company A, choosing whether to opt into Lenient Private Regulator C alongside Company B, is exposed if B causes major harm and government decertifies C. This is the market-side incentive against piling into the laxest body.

**Authorizing government body** (§ 4.2): multi-member commission with fixed members from AI-relevant agencies (NIST Director, US AI Safety Institute Director, OSTP Director) plus Congressional + Presidential appointees ideally from outside government. Powers deliberately **significant but narrow** — license approval, investigation, revocation — with **no broad rulemaking authority** to guard against mission creep. Designed to accommodate **state-level implementation** (with membership shifting to Attorney General + state-university leaders); federal implementation preferred to avoid a state-by-state compliance patchwork.

**Private governance bodies** (§ 4.3): minimal prescriptive rules on how they operate. Motivating thesis: "advanced AI is likely to transform the task of governance itself" — model evaluations, monitoring, standard-setting are themselves candidates for AI-agent automation, and competition between private bodies is meant to surface which designs work. Independence-from-industry challenge acknowledged; proposed mitigations = divestment requirements, no-recent-industry-employment rule for board seats, statutory maximum ratio of staff with industry experience, targeted audits on the independence question.

**Liability protection** (§ 4.4): applies only to (i) tort liability, not statutory (civil rights, consumer protection, environmental, etc.); (ii) third-party misuse harms — first-party corporate liability (workplace, premises) and first-party model deployment (an internal use where a model exfiltrates itself and commits tortious acts against third parties) are *not* covered. **Two adjustable dials**: (a) damages ceiling on the safe harbor (e.g., full safe harbor only for events involving less than $500M property damage or a death threshold; above that, shift to rebuttable presumption of reasonable care or eliminate protection entirely); (b) use a rebuttable presumption against all claims instead of full safe harbor — softer, fewer industry participants, but structure preserved.

**Four institutional-economics advantages claimed** (§ 4.5): lower transaction costs vs. traditional regulation; lower information asymmetries via specialized private bodies; heterogeneity accommodation (open-weight / robotics / bio-foundation-model niches); reputational mechanism (a quasi-government certification likely more valuable to frontier firms than a fine).

**Scope limits explicit at start**: proposal is for **software** frontier AI development; not for physical manifestations (self-driving cars, robotics, drones) where Ball argues existing frameworks are reasonably adequate. Focuses on tort-related risks (physical/digital property loss + physical harm); other harm categories (algorithmic bias, etc.) can be added but would require concomitant safe harbor in the analogous legal domain.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** Ball is the most fully-worked-out alternative-architecture paper on offer against the direct-regulation family (FRONTIER Act, Anthropic AAIF, Hassabis Framework). When a Mercatus/AEI/FAI-adjacent staffer asks "what if we just did private governance?" this is the paper — it comes from directly inside that ideological camp. Three structural comparisons worth carrying:

- **Vs. FRONTIER Act** (see `bills/frontier-act/OVERVIEW.md`): FRONTIER is direct regulation (statutory frameworks, IVOs licensed by Under Secretary, incident reporting to Commerce). Ball is opt-in with tort-liability safe harbor as the incentive. Both use "authorized private bodies" (FRONTIER IVOs ≈ Ball's private governance bodies) but Ball hands the private bodies certification authority and **bundles the safe harbor with certification**; FRONTIER's IVOs are auditors reporting up to government. Ball's decertification-cascade race-to-bottom mechanism has no direct FRONTIER analogue.
- **Vs. Hassabis 2026** (`Hassabis__2026--framework_frontier_ai_dawning_new_age.pdf`): Hassabis proposes a *single* FINRA-style standards body (federally overseen SRO with public-private board). Ball proposes *multiple* authorized private bodies in explicit competition. Both share a "voluntary → mandatory" trajectory; Ball's competition frame is a direct architectural rebuke of the single-body model.
- **Vs. Sastry et al. 2024** (`Sastry_Hadfield__2024--compute_governance_ai.pdf`): Ball explicitly considers compute governance in § 3 and rejects it as insufficient for the transition period. Note the author cross-link — Gillian Hadfield is a starred primary author on Sastry AND the co-author of the "Regulatory Markets" paper that inspires Ball. The same governance scholar's work anchors both the state-compute-controls and the private-regulatory-markets proposals now on the table.

**Caveats when citing.** (a) Working paper, not peer-reviewed empirics — treat as advocacy-shaped argument. (b) Silent on how open-weight developers fit in (a footnote flags open-weight monitoring as impractical but proposes no alternative treatment). (c) The tort-liability-safe-harbor incentive works only where tort liability is a live threat — for pre-deployment risks, systemic labor displacement, or national-security misuse, the incentive is absent. (d) The retroactive-decertification mechanism, while structurally clever, sets up a bank-run-like dynamic (one regulator's decertification hits every client at once) whose behavior under stress has not been empirically tested and is not addressed in the paper.

### Reuel, Bucknall et al. (2025) — "Open Problems in Technical AI Governance"
**File:** `Reuel_Trager__2024--open_problems_technical_ai_governance.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Reuel_Trager__2024--open_problems_technical_ai_governance.txt` | **Pages:** ~46 body + refs | *arXiv:2407.14981 (v1 Jul 2024, v2 Apr 2025); published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research 04/2025; OpenReview: [1nO4qFMiS0](https://openreview.net/forum?id=1nO4qFMiS0)* | 33 authors. Equal-contribution corresponding authors **Anka Reuel** (Stanford) and **Ben Bucknall** (Oxford / Oxford Martin AI Governance Initiative, work completed at GovAI). Co-authors span Stanford, Oxford, MIT CSAIL, Institute for Progress, CNAS, interface (Tech Analysis and Policy Ideas for Europe), Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, Cooperative AI Foundation, **Centre for the Governance of AI** (Anderljung, Garfinkel, Heim, Wills, Chan), Mila (Bengio), OpenMined (Trask), Cohere for AI (Hooker), **Hugging Face** (Solaiman, Luccioni), Cambridge, The Future Society (Moës), Palisade (Ladish), Northeastern (Bau), Noema, Berkeley (Newman), MIT (South, Pentland), Stanford HAI + Virtue AI (Koyejo), Stanford AA (Kochenderfer), and **Robert Trager** (Oxford / Oxford Martin). Explicit note: given the paper's scope, inclusion as an author does not entail endorsement of all aspects, with the exception of AR and BB.

**Core contribution.** Defines **Technical AI Governance (TAIG)** — "technical analysis and tools for supporting the effective governance of AI" — and provides a **two-dimensional taxonomy plus catalogue of ~40 open sub-problem areas and ~100 example research questions**. Motivating claim: key AI-governance decision-makers frequently lack the technical information and tools required to identify where intervention is warranted, assess the efficacy of competing options, and implement chosen policies. The paper is explicitly a **research-agenda / meta-catalogue**, not a slate of policy recommendations — it aims to route technical researchers and funders into governance-relevant sub-problems.

**Three roles TAIG plays for governance** (§ 1.1, "identify / inform / enhance"): (i) **Identify** — map technical aspects of systems to social concepts so governance need is recognized (e.g., tracking AI video-generation advances motivates a deepfakes response); (ii) **Inform** — provide decision-makers with more accurate assessments so they can compare regulatory instruments (e.g., registration vs disclosure, ex ante rules vs post hoc adjudication); (iii) **Enhance** — provide or enable mechanisms for enforcement, incentivization, or compliance (e.g., robust black-box evaluation methods make third-party auditing feasible). Explicit parallel drawn to RegTech/SupTech in finance.

**The taxonomy** (§ 2). Two dimensions — **six capacities × four targets** — with capacities either applied per-target (Assessment, Access, Verification, Security) or cutting across all targets (Operationalization, Ecosystem Monitoring). Capacities: **Assessment** (evaluate systems), **Access** (interact with system components while managing privacy costs), **Verification** (check developer or third-party claims about training / behavior / capabilities / safety), **Security** (protect components from unauthorized access, use, or tampering), **Operationalization** (translate ethical principles + legal requirements into concrete technical standards), **Ecosystem Monitoring** (understand the evolving AI landscape and its impacts). Targets adapted from Bommasani et al. 2023b: **Data**, **Compute**, **Models & Algorithms**, **Deployment**. Note the deliberate exclusion of *organizational processes* from targets — the authors treat process problems as instances of the most relevant target rather than as first-class targets.

**Reader-guide table** (§ 1.3, "who should read what"). Explicitly indexes problem areas by researcher background — ML Theory, Cybersecurity, Cryptography, Hardware Engineering, Software Engineering, Social Science — which makes the paper unusually navigable as a recruitment / funding document. Example: **Cryptography** researchers are routed to Assessment 3.1.1 / 3.2.2 (identification of problematic data, chip/cluster specifications), Access 4.1.1 / 4.1.2 / 4.2.1 / 4.3.1 / 4.4.1 (privacy-preserving access, TEEs), Verification 5.1.1 / 5.2.1 / 5.2.2 / 5.3.3 / 5.4.1 / 5.4.2 (proof-of-training-data, proof-of-learning, verifiable audits, AI-generated content verification), Security 6.2.1 / 6.2.3 / 6.3.2 / 6.4.3 (hardware mechanisms, anti-tamper, shared model governance). **Hardware Engineering** goes to attestation, compute-usage-restriction enforcement, chip location verification. This is the *hardware-enabled governance mechanisms* (HEMs) recruitment surface for the community that Sastry et al. 2024's Appendix B was already trying to seed.

**Per-capacity headline problems** (each subsection lists 4–15 example research questions):

- **Assessment** (§ 3, 21 questions). Data: identifying problematic data at trillion-token scale, license-collection automation, contamination detection, macro-scale bias measurement, attribution of model behavior to specific data. Compute: chip- and cluster-specification for model training (what counts as "AI compute" for a threshold?), workload classification. Models & Algorithms: **reliable evaluations** (internal + ecological validity — an active theme; explicit cite of Chang et al. 2024 and Weidinger et al. 2023 that evaluation "is more art than science"), efficient evaluations, multi-agent evaluations. Deployment: downstream-impact evaluations. Directly upstream of METR's long-task-completion benchmark and Zhou et al.'s general-scales evaluation paper — both catalogued in this collection.
- **Access** (§ 4, 20 questions). Motivated by the "developers shouldn't grade their own homework" argument (Gerken & Rahman-Jones 2023). Data: privacy-preserving third-party access to datasets (OpenSAFELY-style), preserving evaluation-data integrity when hosted openly. Compute: provision of compute resources to academia + auditors. Models & Algorithms: facilitation of third-party model access (Bucknall & Trager 2023 lineage; Casper et al. 2024a lineage). Deployment: access to downstream user logs.
- **Verification** (§ 5, 24 questions). Distinguished from Assessment as "confirm/refute a specific claim" vs "evaluate performance." Data: verify a model was (or was not) trained on a given dataset — Choi et al. 2023's proof-of-training-data protocol cited but flagged as not robust to backdoor-additions and requiring the prover to disclose training data / weights / code (IP + security concerns). Compute: **verification of chip location** (Baker 2023), **verification of compute workloads** (Shavit 2023) — foundational for international-agreement verification à la nuclear treaty (Avenhaus et al. 2006 cited). Models & Algorithms: verification of model properties, dynamic systems, proof-of-learning. Deployment: verifiable audits + verification of AI-generated content (watermarking specifically flagged — motivating example is the Council of the European Union / White House / G7 watermark language that "may not achieve policymakers' desired outcomes").
- **Security** (§ 6). Data: detection + prevention of training-data extraction attacks. Compute: **hardware mechanisms for AI security** (TEEs), **anti-tamper hardware**, **enforcement of compute-usage restrictions** — this is HEMs / kill-switch / attestation research explicitly framed. Models & Algorithms: prevention of model theft, shared model governance, **model disgorgement + machine unlearning**. Deployment: detection of adversarial attacks, modification-resistant models, detection + authorization of dual-use capability at inference time. Note: authors flag this section as closest to AI safety proper and defend its inclusion.
- **Operationalization** (§ 7, cross-cutting). Two problem clusters: (i) **Translation of governance goals into policies + regulatory requirements** — explicit critique of using training FLOP as the risk indicator (smaller-trained models can outperform larger ones; unclear treatment of quantization, dropout, auxiliary process compute; Hooker 2024 cited), calls for identifying more precise risk-indicating system properties; (ii) **Deployment corrections** — O'Brien et al. 2023's five-category framework (user restrictions, access-frequency limits, capability/feature restrictions, use-case restrictions, model shutdown) treated as needing more technical work, especially minimizing downstream disruption from shutdowns.
- **Ecosystem Monitoring** (§ 8, cross-cutting). Four problem clusters: (i) **Clarification of associated risks** — better threat models, improved incident reporting + monitoring beyond news-article scraping (OECD/PAI databases flagged as insufficient — direct handoff to Ortega 2025's incident-regime proposal); (ii) **Prediction of future developments + impacts** — extend Epoch-style compute/algorithmic-progress tracking; (iii) **Assessment of environmental impacts** — energy of training and inference, mining/refining costs for chip supply chain, e-waste; (iv) **Supply chain mapping** — tracking components + actors for liability + copyright.

**Scope + guardrails** (§ 1.2). Explicitly avoids taking normative positions on which policies to adopt. Warns against **techno-solutionism**, notes that many of the mechanisms discussed are **dual-use** (e.g., HEMs for advanced-compute visibility could also be applied to surveil legitimate hardware use), and stresses that "solving" all problems here would not solve AI governance — many governance problems are primarily non-technical. Considers AI-safety / alignment work, government-use-of-AI, and AI-defending-against-AI as out of scope.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** This is the **canonical technical-research-agenda paper** for the entire TAIG field — TMLR-published, 33-author, and structured explicitly as a routing document from a technical background into governance-relevant sub-problems. Three ways to use it in this collection:

1. **Locator for existing collection entries.** The paper explicitly anticipates or names the sub-problem categories that other papers in this collection develop in depth. Direct handoffs: **Compute** (Sastry et al. 2024's compute-uranium framing is the natural next step from Reuel § 3.2 + § 5.2 + § 6.2, and Rahman 2026 stress-tests the detectability pillar Reuel § 8.2 flags as a threat-model gap); **Evaluations** (METR 2025 and Zhou et al. 2025 both live inside Reuel § 3.3, "Reliable / Efficient Evaluations"); **Incident regimes** (Ortega 2025 fills the gap Reuel § 8.1 names as under-served); **Verification** (Aumann & Lindell 2007's covert-adversary threat model is directly relevant to Reuel § 5.2, compute-workload verification under conditions where the party being verified might cheat); **Evaluator independence** (Anthropic AAIF's evaluator-shopping mixer is a concrete instance of Reuel § 4.3's third-party-access problem). Use this paper as the map when explaining to a staffer or researcher **where a specific policy question sits in the technical landscape**.
2. **Recruitment / funding surface.** The reader-guide table is the strongest recruitment artifact currently on offer for the field. When Canary considers surfacing "here is how a technical researcher can plug into governance," this is the paper to point at — it is more comprehensive than any single-issue whitepaper and it names concrete example research questions with sub-section pointers.
3. **Author-cross-link inventory.** The author list is a near-complete roster of the visible TAIG community as of 2025. Cross-links worth noting: **Heim** and **Anderljung** are also on Sastry et al. 2024; **Bengio** appears in the IAISR / *International AI Safety Report* orbit; **Pentland** connects to MIT Connection Science and the digital-credentials work; **Trager** (Oxford Martin) runs GovAI-adjacent work; **Ladish** (Palisade) and **Solaiman/Luccioni** (Hugging Face) sit inside operational safety-eval / open-model-governance networks. When mapping who is doing what in TAIG, this paper's masthead is a reliable starting point.

**Caveats when citing.** (a) **Deliberately non-normative** — the paper does not endorse any specific policy mechanism and is designed to be citable across the ideological spectrum in AI governance. Do not cite it as endorsing e.g. compute governance or watermarking; it merely catalogues the open problems that would need to be solved for either to work. (b) **Coverage is "incomplete catalog"** by the authors' own framing — absence of a topic in the taxonomy is not evidence the topic is unimportant; some notable gaps include international-agreement architecture, macro-economic-impact measurement, and post-labor-market-disruption operationalization. (c) **The reader-guide table categorizes researchers by background rather than by concern (existential-risk / near-term-harm / labor-displacement / civil-liberties)** — useful for technical recruitment but not for coalition-building around specific policy demands. (d) **The scope-exclusion of AI-safety-and-alignment work** is a deliberate framing choice; some readers will find some of the Security section (§ 6) hard to distinguish from AI safety and should treat the boundary as porous. (e) **Version note:** cite the **v2 (April 2025) / TMLR** version — v1 (July 2024) predates the taxonomy refinement.

### Anthropic (2026, AAIF) — "Anthropic's Advanced AI Framework"
**File:** `Anthropic__2026--advanced_ai_framework.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Anthropic__2026--advanced_ai_framework.txt` | **Pages:** 19 | *anthropic.com, 2026-06-11* | Anthropic institutional publication (companion to Amodei's *Policy on the AI Exponential*).

The concrete policy artifact behind Amodei's FAA-analogy in *Policy on the AI Exponential*. Explicitly two-part: (I) frontier-developer obligations; (II) societal-resilience measures on biological and cyber threats. Named at time of release with a $350M Anthropic commitment ($200M research fund + $150M workforce fellowship). Written "primarily with the US federal government in mind" but framed as applicable to other jurisdictions with tailoring.

**Part 1: Frontier developer obligations.**
- **Covered Developer scope: both criteria must be met** — models trained with >10²⁵ FLOP AND developer earns >$500M/yr AI-derived revenue OR spends >$1B/yr on AI R&D. Compare directly to **FRONTIER Act's** three-tier revenue-plus-AI-dev-spend keying (§ 2 definitions) — Anthropic's proposal is a *simpler two-condition gate* than FRONTIER's frontier/large/very-large tiering. Framework flags that a compute threshold "may fall" and a capability-based threshold may become preferable over time.
- **Catastrophic Risk definition explicitly aligned with California SB 53** — a deliberate California-federal harmonization move.
- **Four Enumerated Risks:** biological weapons, offensive cyber operations, loss of control, automated R&D that could accelerate the other three. Same four Amodei names in the essay; the framework operationalizes them.
- **Critical Safety Incident** definition includes: (a) unauthorized model-weight exfiltration or malicious modification; (b) catastrophic-risk materialization; (c) loss of control causing death/injury; (d) *a model using deceptive techniques against the developer to subvert controls or monitoring outside evaluation contexts, in a manner demonstrating materially increased catastrophic risk*. Item (d) is the concrete legislative language behind "deception" as a first-class safety category.
- **Preemption clause is remarkably narrow.** Federal preemption of state law "unless [Congress] enacts a rigorous federal regime that meets or exceeds the strongest measures proposed in this framework"; even then, "limited to the specific frontier governance functions Congress has expressly chosen to occupy"; ambiguity "resolved in favor of preserving state authority"; federal compliance does not confer state-law immunity/safe-harbor. Compare to **FRONTIER § 9** (function-scoped preemption naming CA SB-53, NY RAISE, IL SB-315 as targets, no severability): Anthropic's version is significantly more state-protective than FRONTIER's on this axis. This is a Hill-briefing-portable frame for staffers concerned FRONTIER over-preempts.
- **Transparency provisions:** safety framework (with named accountable corporate officer); annual compliance certification with civil penalties for misrepresentation; six-month risk reports (including *internal* deployments of substantially riskier models — a Hidden-lab-use provision); system cards for material capability jumps or safeguard weakenings; **15-day critical-incident reporting** (tighter than GAAIA's original 15-day cadence, matches FRONTIER's 72-hour tightening on paper but the FRONTIER text is 72h; AAIF stays at 15d). Reports "exempt from public records disclosure laws."
- **Independent evaluator regime.** Required within 6 months of enactment. Unredacted-access rights to risk reports + system cards + most-capable models. Anti-evaluator-shopping via "highly rated evaluators could be randomly assigned to AI developers, particularly in high-stakes cases." Explicit path for government to eventually build in-house evaluation and supplement/substitute for the private ecosystem. Framework proposes government funding or pooled funding for evaluators. This is the concrete design behind Amodei's "regulatory markets" fallback in *Policy on the AI Exponential*.
- **Security provisions:** full-development-environment security program; distillation-attack monitoring channel; regular pen-testing; security-of-privileged-access-parties (independent evaluators bound by security requirements commensurate with their access).
- **Enforcement.** Provisions in tier 1 (false-statements prohibition, revenue-scaled civil penalties, whistleblower protections with anonymous internal channels + retaliation ban) hold "under any enforcement design." Tier 2 (blocking authority) offered as menu of options: Agency-review → four enumerated violation types → remedies (fines / prohibition on further deployment / restrictions on already-deployed models "in extreme cases") → safeguards menu (court enforcement with fast-tracked provisional remedies, cabined discretion, consistent-treatment + judicial-review). Explicitly designed as mix-and-match to let jurisdictions tune "risks of inadequately safeguarded models vs risks of overbroad regulation."

**Part 2: Societal resilience.**
- **Biological resilience — three layers.** *Prevention:* modernize biosafety/biosecurity rules that predate synthetic biology/AI-assisted design; close oversight gaps (extend enforceable rules to privately funded research; strengthen IRBs; require personnel vetting for dangerous pathogens); **gene-synthesis screening mandate** for both providers and benchtop synthesizer manufacturers with customer verification; threat-monitoring/intelligence-sharing with legal safe harbors + antitrust carve-outs. *Detection:* pathogen-agnostic biosurveillance at national/subnational/international levels; microbial forensics + attribution capacity. *Preparedness & response:* public health infrastructure hardening; stockpile pandemic-grade respirators (reusable, essential-workforce priority); airborne-transmission suppression (built-environment R&D); AI-accelerated countermeasure development; broad-spectrum antivirals; adaptable manufacturing platforms; binding after-action reviews with statutory deadlines.
- **Cyber resilience — four layers.** *Prevention:* open-source/legacy software security funding (memory-safe migration); phishing-resistant identity + content provenance; forward-deployed engineers + shared regional SOCs for under-resourced operators (water/wastewater, municipal government, school districts, regional hospitals, rural electric coops). *Measurement & situational awareness:* national AI-safety-institute-or-equivalent to track frontier cyber capability; threat-intelligence function fusing developer + government + incident-response telemetry; safe harbor for defensive coordination. *Defender advantage:* hardware-root-of-trust network isolation; AI-enabled breach remediation (minutes/hours vs days/weeks); safeguards for broad release of defensive capability; **strategic reserve of long-lead-time OT hardware** (protective relays, RTUs, PLCs — a specific supply-chain-resilience ask); software-supply-chain mapping; coordinated vulnerability remediation "at 10–100× current disclosure volume with sub-seven-day turnaround"; patching-at-scale with binding cadence for critical-infrastructure operators. *Security modernization:* mandated support-lifecycle publication; funded replacement program for legacy systems; research into new security paradigms (formal verification, runtime patching, polymorphic defense).
- **Loss of control and automated R&D.** Explicitly less developed. Framework notes "the societal resilience agenda for loss of control and automated R&D is less mature than it is for biological and cyber risks, and we believe it needs much more active work across the field." Promising directions: detection/response for AI systems acting outside developer control; containment/shutdown infrastructure. This is an explicit *gap* in the framework — worth flagging when staffers ask what Anthropic's operational answer is for the AGI-loss-of-control scenario.

**Direct relevance to `gaaia-analysis` / FRONTIER Act briefings.**
- **Architectural convergence with FRONTIER on the Agency + IVO structure.** Both name a single Agency, both require independent evaluators, both scope by revenue-plus-AI-spend criteria, both include whistleblower/retaliation protections. The pieces where Anthropic diverges from FRONTIER are the *most-briefable divergences*:
  - **Preemption** — AAIF's narrow, state-preserving preemption vs FRONTIER § 9's function-scoped-but-broadly-worded preemption. Anthropic's language is the frame staffers concerned about state-authority erosion can lean on.
  - **Critical-incident cadence** — AAIF's 15 days matches original GAAIA. FRONTIER tightened to 72 hours. Anthropic proposes going back to the more workable 15-day window; worth noting to staffers considering FRONTIER's amendment path.
  - **Blocking authority** — AAIF offers this as a *menu* (court enforcement, cabined discretion, fast-tracked judicial review); FRONTIER § 8 has a specific Commerce-Secretary structure with D.D.C. exclusive review. AAIF's court-enforcement + fast-tracked-provisional model is a design alternative some staffers may prefer.
  - **Deception as first-class incident category.** AAIF's Critical Safety Incident definition (d) — deception used against the developer to subvert controls — is unusually concrete. GAAIA/FRONTIER don't have this specific hook. Worth surfacing for offices skeptical that "deception" is an operationalizable regulatory concept.
- **The AAIF is the doc actually being pushed to Hill offices**, per external coverage. When a staffer says "Anthropic's proposal," this is what they mean.

**Conflicts of interest.** Anthropic-authored, so every design choice serves Anthropic's institutional interests. Notable ones: (a) The compute + revenue-and-spend threshold gate is designed to cover Anthropic's *actual* size class and above, not smaller labs. (b) The independent-evaluator regime creates a structural role for the same third-party auditors Anthropic already uses. (c) The narrow preemption clause protects Anthropic's California-domiciled operations from a hostile federal preemption, while covering Anthropic under its already-compliant SB 53 posture. (d) The "regulatory markets" fallback keeps evaluation adjacent to industry rather than fully in-house government hands. None of this invalidates the proposal, but staffers should be aware the design reflects Anthropic's positioning.

### Anthropic (2026, EPF) — "Anthropic's Economic Policy Framework"
**File:** `Anthropic__2026--economic_policy_framework.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Anthropic__2026--economic_policy_framework.txt` | **Pages:** 14 | *anthropic.com, 2026-06-11* | Anthropic institutional publication (companion to Amodei's *Policy on the AI Exponential*).

The labor-displacement counterpart to the AAIF. Three-tier framework keyed to US unemployment rate, prefaced by foundation infrastructure (measurement + analytical unit + delivery systems) that Anthropic argues must be in place before any tier can be executed.

**Framing / Values.** Opens explicit: "We are not seeking job displacement. We are working to prevent or minimize it. Some amount of displacement... may be an intrinsic consequence of the technology." Acknowledges the "dignity in work" claim (helps at Tier 1/2, complicates Tier 3 by potentially "entrenching the link between income and employment that a Tier 3 world may need to loosen"). Notes some Tier 1/2 tools will need to be phased out if conditions move toward Tier 3.

**Foundation.** Three prerequisites.
- **Measurement.** Statistical agencies need expanded investment + AI-specific survey instruments + reporting requirements for AI labs (including Anthropic itself). Metrics should focus on AI usage rates, use intensity, productivity indicators by adoption level. Anthropic Economic Index is a partial contribution but "data from a single company cannot tell the whole story."
- **Dedicated government analytical unit.** Explicitly modeled on the Council of Economic Advisers ("created for the postwar transition"). Sector-by-sector tracking, institution stress-testing, early-signal flagging.
- **Delivery infrastructure.** State Unemployment Insurance systems are unprepared for a large shock — legacy systems buckled under pandemic claim volumes, months of delay for emergency extensions, billions in improper payments. Modernization for faster claims processing, benefit-adjustment flexibility, real-time identity + cross-state fraud matching. International note: only 52% of the world's population is covered by any social protection scheme.

**Tier 1 (~5% unemployment with churn).** Prepare before disruption is visible.
- **Universal pre-distributive capital accounts** — the flagship Tier 1 intervention. Federal government already seeded these at birth (per the framework — a real policy datapoint); AAIF proposes making them **permanent and expanded**. Phased eligibility (children → young adults entering workforce → incumbent workers in AI-exposed occupations → all working-age adults). Flexible withdrawal for retraining/relocation/credentialing. **Equity funding** — accounts can be funded with equity in AI companies "so that beneficiaries share directly in nearer-term gains from AI-driven growth." Explicit acknowledgment that these don't cushion near-term displacement — they must be seeded early to compound.
- **Additional interventions:** wage insurance (US evidence positive, Canada/Germany less encouraging), occupational licensing reform (24% of US workers hold license/certification; evidence of reduced employment + higher prices without welfare gains), retention tax incentives (multi-year credits for retaining/redeploying workers into substantially different roles; risks: propping up low-productivity firms, fraud), workforce training grants (evidence mixed on traditional gov retraining; shorter employer/sector-connected programs show real income gains), job-matching infrastructure (AI-powered skills platforms; modest reemployment effects in RCT evidence).

**Tier 2 (~10% unemployment).**
- **Expanded UI with auto-triggering extensions** (framework proposes reforming Extended Benefits trigger so extension is automatic + uniform across states, without Congressional emergency-extension legislation). Cites Great Recession + COVID evidence: extended benefits did little to discourage job search while helping families with essential spending.
- **Sector-specific transition support** (extended income + subsidized retraining + job-search allowances + relocation assistance).
- **Basic needs relief** — monthly payments to displaced workers who exhausted UI or entrants unable to secure work after documented search. Enhanced benefits for those opting into labor-shortage sectors (healthcare, education, child/elder care, infrastructure, ecosystem restoration, public safety). Cites evidence consumption drops sharply at UI exhaustion; existing safety net only partially fills the gap.
- **Also flagged:** if disruption reaches Tier 2 levels, framework endorses "government regulations and incentives at the firm level that manage the pace of displacement" *if applied uniformly across the field* — a limited endorsement of pace-control that requires uniform application to avoid first-mover disadvantage.

**Tier 3 (unemployment exceeding historical peaks).**
- **Sustain income through existing systems** — UI auto-extension + enhanced wage insurance (higher caps, longer duration) + expanded basic-needs relief. As extensions lengthen, benefit levels should "converge toward a common floor rather than continuing to track prior wages indefinitely" — an explicit break from the standard UI structure that anticipates the Tier 3 "productive contribution decoupled from income" world.
- **New tax bases.** Explicit menu: capital gains tax increase, broad-based consumption taxes, sector-specific AI levies (measured by *tokens, compute, or revenue*), digital dividends. Rationale: "effective tax rates on labor substantially exceed those on capital" — labor-to-capital income shift means current tax system captures a shrinking share.
- **New redistribution mechanisms.** Menu: UBI, AI sovereign wealth funds (funded by investment stakes in AI-driven productivity), equity-sharing giving workers partial ownership in AI enterprises, dramatically expanded pre-distributive capital accounts.
- **Public investment in human- and community-facing work** — direct a portion of AI-driven surplus toward workforce-shortage sectors (education, healthcare, care). Two rationales: closes staffing gaps, keeps paid work available for those who want it. Framework acknowledges the second rationale rests on a value judgment about work-conferring-meaning "which not everyone shares and which future generations may weigh differently."
- **Explicit institutional stance:** "we are ready and willing to pay our fair share." Framework acknowledges recent inflationary experience + elevated deficits may limit fiscal headroom, with a fallback toward regulatory adjustments + private-insurance mandates + reallocation within existing budgets if fiscal crisis coincides with displacement.

**Direct relevance to `gaaia-analysis` / FRONTIER Act briefings** — less direct than AAIF's, but several angles worth surfacing:
- **The token/compute/revenue AI levy proposal** is the most concrete revenue mechanism any frontier lab has floated. Any Hill office asking "how do we pay for the education/healthcare/care hiring that AI displacement will demand?" has a lab-endorsed answer here.
- **AI-company-equity-funded universal capital accounts** create a policy-side reason for government to want a formal stake mechanism in frontier labs — a very different frame from the CAISI fee-authority approach. This is worth watching if any FY27/28 vehicles pick it up.
- **The auto-triggering UI extension proposal** is a MATCH-adjacent macro-stabilizer that doesn't require new bureaucracy; likely to be more legible to fiscally-conservative offices than the Tier 3 UBI/sovereign-wealth menu.
- **The "Tier 3 phase-out of Tier 1 tools" language** is where the framework quietly acknowledges that pre-distributive capital accounts + workforce retraining stop working when work-income linkage breaks. Useful frame when discussing whether specific proposals are near-term or long-term.
- **Uniform-application pace-control endorsement (Tier 2)** — this is Anthropic's most direct concession that voluntary responsible-scaling isn't enough beyond a threshold. Worth surfacing when discussing pace-of-deployment questions.

**Conflicts of interest.** Anthropic-authored corporate policy document; the design centers Anthropic-favorable moves: (a) The "measurement" foundation asks for reporting requirements on labs but on *deployment patterns and workforce effects*, not model-development inputs. (b) Equity-funded capital accounts create a mechanism for Anthropic to satisfy redistribution obligations via non-cash contributions. (c) Tier 3 tax menu is broad and non-committal — no single mechanism is endorsed enough to trigger competitive pressure on Anthropic specifically. (d) The "we are ready and willing to pay our fair share" is unambiguous but structurally deferred to a Tier-3 world Anthropic argues may or may not arrive. None of these invalidate the framework — but staffers should be aware the design does not require Anthropic to commit to any specific redistribution mechanism today.

### Anthropic (2026, WABI) — "When AI Builds Itself"
**File:** `Anthropic__2026--when_ai_builds_itself.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Anthropic__2026--when_ai_builds_itself.txt` | **Pages:** 23 (Chrome-printed from anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement) | *anthropic.com/institute, 2026-06-04* | Anthropic Institute report; co-authored by Marina Favaro and Jack Clark, with editorial support from Santi Ruiz; visuals by Shan Carter, Romello Goodman, Nikki Makagiansar; data collected by Brian Calvert and Jun Shern Chan. Feedback list includes Daniel Freeman, Jim Baker, Holden Karnofsky, Anton Korinek, Andy Jones, and others.

The Anthropic Institute's first data-forward artifact — a public disclosure of previously-unreported internal Anthropic metrics on how much of Anthropic's own AI development is being done by Claude, framed as evidence that AI is already accelerating AI development and as an argument that full recursive self-improvement ("AI systems capable of fully autonomously designing and developing their successor") could arrive sooner than institutions are prepared for. This is the doc Amodei cites in *Policy on the AI Exponential* (footnote 1) as the "initial internal data" foundation for the recursive-self-improvement / automated-R&D risk category. Structure: framing → external-benchmark evidence → internal engineering evidence → internal research evidence → objection-handling → three-scenario framework → policy recommendations. Interleaves anonymous Anthropic-employee quotes throughout ("I started leaning hard into Claudifying about a year ago… ~5 months since I last wrote any code"; "On days where everything works well, I can't help but think nothing I do matters").

**Framing / five stages of AI-assisted development.** (1) 2021–2023 pre-Claude, humans write code and docs; (2) 2023–2025 chatbot assistance for code snippets; (3) 2025–2026 coding agents writing/editing files; (4) today (May 2026) autonomous agents running code and delegating hours to other agents; (5) hypothesized "closing the loop" — future Claude versions continuously improved by Claude itself. Two categories of work distinguished: **engineering** (writing code, standing up infrastructure, overseeing training) vs **research** (deciding what experiments to run, interpreting results, choosing next ideas). Load-bearing claim: "In engineering, Claude can be handed an underspecified problem and figure out how to solve it… In research, Claude can already match or outperform skilled humans at executing a well-specified experiment. However, large performance gaps persist when it comes to Claude exercising judgement in choosing goals." That judgment gap is characterized as "the gap between AI today and a future system that could autonomously design its own successor."

**External benchmark evidence.**
- **METR task-horizon doubling.** Doubling time for task-length AI can reliably complete has itself accelerated from every 7 months to every 4 months. Claude Opus 3 (March 2024) → **4-min tasks**; Sonnet 3.7 (March 2025) → **90-min tasks**; Opus 4.6 (March 2026) → **12-hour tasks**; Mythos Preview → **"at least 16 hours," at "the upper end of what METR can measure without new tasks."**
- **SWE-bench.** Low single digits → saturated in two years.
- **CORE-Bench.** 20% (2024) → saturated in 15 months.
- Report caveats: benchmarks "often saturate below 100% due to errors in the question and answer sets"; METR trendline "looks the same at 80% reliability" as at 50%.

**Internal Anthropic engineering evidence.**
- **>80% of merged production code authored by Claude as of May 2026**, up from single-digit percentages before Claude Code launched (Feb 2025). Footnote acknowledges leadership has publicly cited 90%+ overall; 80% is a more conservative production-line measurement.
- **Lines of code merged per engineer per day.** Flat 2021–2024. Inflected upward in 2025 (agents ran code). Steepened again in 2026 (autonomy over longer horizons). Q2 2026: typical engineer merged **8× as much code per day** as in 2024. Report explicitly hedges "8× is almost certainly an overstatement of the true productivity gain" and notes Anthropic "doesn't reward people for how many lines of code they write." Scale context: GitHub saw ~1B commits in all of 2025 vs ~275M/week by mid-2026 (~14B/year run-rate).
- **Open-ended task success rate:** 76% May 2026, **up 50 points in six months** (Claude Code agent tasks, Claude-judge). Illustrative example: a routine upgrade crashed tens of thousands of training jobs; Claude isolated an obscure debugging flag and reproduced/confirmed a fix in ~2 hours vs "2–3 days" for humans.
- **Code quality parity.** Staff consensus (not unanimous): Claude-written code was "somewhat worse" than human in late 2025, "roughly at parity today" (May 2026), "expect it to be strictly better within the year." Anthropic runs an automated Claude reviewer on all proposed changes; retrospective analysis found this would have caught **~1/3 of the bugs behind past claude.ai production incidents** before reaching production.
- **Bulk-fix scale.** April 2026: Claude shipped 800+ fixes reducing a class of API errors by a factor of 1000×; the supervising engineer estimated humans would have taken four years.
- **Employee productivity survey.** March 2026, **n=130 research-team employees**. Median respondent estimated **4× output** with Mythos Preview vs no AI-model access. Report concedes true uplift was "somewhat lower" than 4× per METR research on developer over-estimation.

**Internal Anthropic research evidence.**
- **Code-optimization "miniature research loop."** Same test every model release: hand Claude code that trains a small model, ask for speedup with correctness preserved. Opus 4 (May 2025) → **~3× speedup**; Mythos Preview (April 2026) → **~52× speedup**. Skilled-human calibration: **~4× in 4–8 hours**. The like-for-like model-to-model curve (~3× → ~52× in a year) is the informative comparison.
- **Open-ended safety research experiment** (published April 2026). Can a weaker model reliably supervise a stronger one? Task with performance floor (weak supervisor solo) and ceiling (strong model trained on correct answers). Two human researchers over ~1 week recovered **~23% of the floor-to-ceiling gap**; Claude-powered agents recovered **97%** over **~800 cumulative agent-hours** using **~$18,000 in compute**. Caveats: result didn't transfer cleanly to production-scale models; humans still chose the problem and created the scoring rubric. Within those bounds "the agents designed every experiment themselves."
- **Research judgment / next-step selection.** Real Claude Code sessions Jan–March 2026 where an Anthropic researcher took a detour before returning to the right track. Claude models shown only pre-detour work and asked what to do next; separate outcome-aware Claude judged AI-vs-human choice. n=129 "detour" moments: Opus 4.5 (Nov 2025) beat human choice **51%**; Mythos Preview (April 2026) beat human choice **64%**. Bias-check: on n=127 already-strong moves, models judged better only ~20% — mitigating the concern that the model is just gaming the judge.

**"What if we're wrong?" objection-handling.** Engages the counter that judgment/direction-setting is what matters most and Claude isn't there. Reply: (a) AI progress rarely advances via eureka moments — "most progress is incremental: we scale something up, see what breaks, fix it, and try again" — exactly the workflow Claude excels at now. (b) Edison's "1% inspiration, 99% perspiration" — perspiration is being automated. (c) Even a conservative reading (Claude never achieves research taste) still implies compounding acceleration if humans spend their time on the single-digit direction-setting fraction. (d) Less conservative reading: Claude's improving research-judgment signal (51% → 64%) is evidence that "research taste" is another AI capability that improves like theory of mind and joke-explaining.

**Three future scenarios.**
1. **Trend stalls into an S-curve.** Diminishing returns; requires new architectural idea beyond Transformers, or binding constraint is supply chain (chip fab, grid, interconnect), or an exogenous shock. Report deems this the least likely, but includes it. Even here, "major changes to occur in the world" — cites **Project Glasswing**: in its first weeks, Mythos Preview found **more than 10,000 high- and critical-severity software vulnerabilities across the world's most important systems**, enough that the bottleneck in cyber defense has shifted from finding to patching. Cites "100-person company can increasingly do the work of a 1,000-person one."
2. **Compounding efficiency gains, humans still direct.** AI development substantially automated; humans continue to set research directions and judge results. 100-person orgs → 10,000- or 100,000-person org output. Explicit dual-use acknowledgment: "authoritarian surveillance of whole populations… influence operations that tailor manipulation to each individual and run at a scale no human team could match." Amdahl's law: bottleneck shifts to whatever hasn't sped up (Anthropic already sees code review as new bottleneck). Report says: **"the evidence we've laid out here suggests that we're likely heading into this scenario."**
3. **Full recursive self-improvement.** AI systems capable of designing and refining their successors. Pace bounded by compute + algorithmic efficiency discovery. Human role shifts substantially toward oversight/validation/verification of an "expanding virtual lab run by AI systems." Skills transfer to rest of science. Alignment outcome is "least certain" — could get sufficiently aligned + taste-having models that discover and implement novel alignment solutions ("also be sufficiently wise to halt development if not"), or "rare occurrences of misalignment present in today's models could compound as the models build their successors, growing more frequent but less understood until we lose control of them." Notes "embodied intelligence (i.e., robotics) might quickly follow." Hedge on societal pace: Amdahl's law applies to the world too — "more intelligence can't learn what a drug does over decades of use, can't hold elections sooner than a constitution dictates, and can't turn a stranger into an old friend in a weekend."

**Policy recommendations.**
- **Verifiable coordinated slowdown or pause infrastructure.** Anthropic Institute commits to research and action toward "the systems that a credible slowdown or pause would require." Explicit conditional: **"we expect that we would slow down or temporarily pause, if other developers at or near the frontier also did so in a verifiable manner."** Unilateral pause "achievable immediately, but accomplishes much less: it would change who the front-runner is, but it would not create the wider deliberative process that is currently missing."
- **Detectability bar.** Report names *detectability* as a lower standard than *verifiability* and says even this lower bar is harder for AI than prior arms control: "training runs are far easier to conceal than missile silos, their inputs are general-purpose, and the incentive to defect quietly is enormous." A credible pause "has to specify what triggers it, what lifts it, and who adjudicates."
- **Analogy and caveat.** Cites Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty as verification-for-complex-technology precedent, but notes: "those regimes took decades to build both the infrastructure and the trust. We don't have that long."
- **Convening.** Institute committed to organizing conversations "in the coming months" with policymakers, researchers, civil society, and other AI companies "especially around full recursive self-improvement and how to create better options for coordination and deliberation."

**Cross-references to Amodei essays.**
- **Cited in *Policy on the AI Exponential* (June 2026, footnote 1)** as the source for the recursive-self-improvement / automated-R&D risk category.
- **Companion to AAIF.** AAIF names "automated R&D that could accelerate the other three [enumerated risks]" as one of four enumerated catastrophic risks. WABI provides the current-state evidence base — AAIF answers "what regulation should look like"; WABI answers "why we think we're close to needing it."
- **Companion to EPF.** EPF anticipates a world where "100-person companies could do the work of 10,000- or 100,000-person organizations" — WABI's Scenario 2 is the same claim from the productivity side, with internal evidence that the shift is underway.
- **Echoes *Adolescence* framing.** The three-scenario framing (stall / compound / recurse) sits in the same epistemic register: acknowledging exponentials may be S-curves while arguing the trend line is real and the window is narrow.
- **References *Machines of Loving Grace*** explicitly in Scenario 3: recursive intelligence + embodied intelligence could accelerate MoLG's benefits (novel coordination, clinical trials, physical-world building) but "achieving recursive improvement alone does not suggest an immediate change" in industrial production or governance.

**Direct relevance to `policy-levers` / FRONTIER Act briefings.**
- **Primary-data foundation for FRONTIER's fourth enumerated risk category (automated R&D).** FRONTIER Act, like AAIF, names four risks: bio, cyber, loss-of-control, automated R&D. When a Hill staffer asks "what's the evidence that automated R&D is close enough to justify treating it as a regulated risk category?", WABI is the answer — 80%+ code by Claude, 8× per-engineer merged-code multiplier, 52× code-optimization speedup, 97% closure of a human-baseline-to-ceiling research gap.
- **Anchoring metric for "the exponential."** Amodei's "policy on the AI exponential" framing sits on the METR 4-months-doubling number. WABI is the on-record documentation of that number and the extrapolation (days-long tasks in range this year; weeks-long tasks in 2027).
- **Concession on lines-of-code as measure.** The explicit acknowledgment that "8× lines-of-code" overstates true productivity is a useful hedge to note in briefings — Anthropic's own doc isn't claiming the metric is clean.
- **Project Glasswing citation** — >10K high/critical vulnerabilities in the first weeks of Mythos Preview is the most concrete public data point on offensive-cyber risk. Cross-references FRONTIER's cyber-risk enumeration.
- **The pause-conditional-on-verifiability commitment** is Anthropic's cleanest institutional endorsement of coordinated frontier-lab pace-control. Staffers asking "do frontier labs actually endorse slowdown as a tool?" have a concrete answer here (conditional yes).

**Conflicts of interest.** Institutional Anthropic publication citing internal Anthropic data on Anthropic's own model performance for Anthropic's own workflow. (a) 80%-code-by-Claude figure has attribution-pipeline gaps and no third-party verification; leadership has publicly stated 90%+ overall. (b) March 2026 employee survey (n=130) is Anthropic staff self-reporting on Anthropic's own model; report concedes true uplift was likely lower than the median 4×. (c) Model comparisons are all Anthropic-to-Anthropic — no cross-lab calibration; a reader cannot tell how Anthropic's automation curve compares to OpenAI's or Google DeepMind's. (d) The "verifiable slowdown" recommendation frames a future in which Anthropic — as an incumbent frontier lab that has already invested in safety infrastructure — has a structural advantage relative to newer entrants who would face verification asymmetries. (e) The report does not disclose which Anthropic teams' code is included in the 80% measurement, nor how the "success rate" judge was calibrated. None of this invalidates the evidence, but every number was collected and framed by the party with the strongest institutional interest in the result being taken seriously. The report explicitly reads as an argument for a particular policy conclusion (need for verifiable-pause infrastructure), not as a neutral capabilities audit.

## Independent Position Essays

### Amodei (2024, MoLG) — "Machines of Loving Grace"
**File:** `Amodei__2024--machines_of_loving_grace.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Amodei__2024--machines_of_loving_grace.txt` | **Pages:** 52 (Substack-style rendering, includes chapter TOC sidebar on every page) | **Word count:** ~13,000 essay body (~16,700 in extracted text incl. duplicated sidebar) | *darioamodei.com, October 2024* | Dario Amodei (co-founder & CEO, Anthropic).

The **aspirational-endstate predecessor** to *The Adolescence of Technology*. Amodei opens by explaining Anthropic's usual reason for staying quiet on upsides — leverage (risks are the mutable side, benefits are market-driven), avoidance of the propaganda register, and dislike of "prophet leading their people to salvation" grandiosity — then breaks the rule to sketch what "everything going right" looks like. Frame: **"a concrete vision does more to advance discussion than a highly hedged and abstract one."** Explicitly not intended as an Anthropic-branded plan; presented as one person's guesses. Structured as **five domains** plus a "Taking stock" close and prefaced by a "Basic assumptions and framework" section pinning down the operative definitions.

**Definitional framework — "powerful AI."** Amodei deliberately avoids "AGI" ("terminology issues"), uses "powerful AI" for concreteness. Stipulated system: (a) intelligence "smarter than a Nobel Prize winner across most relevant fields — biology, programming, math, engineering, writing, etc."; (b) all interfaces available to a human working virtually (text, audio, video, mouse/keyboard, internet); (c) tasks taking hours-to-weeks executed autonomously; (d) no physical embodiment beyond a screen; (e) millions of parallel instances, operating at **10x–100x human speed**. Summary phrase: **"a country of geniuses in a datacenter"** — reappears verbatim in the June 2026 *Policy on the AI Exponential* essay when Amodei describes the strategic gap between AI-leading and AI-lagging nations. Arrival timing: "**as early as 2026**, though there are also ways it could take much longer." Focus is on the "5–10 years after" powerful AI arrives.

**Section 1 — Biology and health.** The load-bearing section, and the most concrete. Core claim: "AI-enabled biology and medicine will allow us to **compress the progress that human biologists would have achieved over the next 50–100 years into 5–10 years**" — the **"compressed 21st century."** Argument: biology dominated by high-leverage discovery tools (CRISPR, mRNA vaccines, CAR-T, cheap sequencing, advanced microscopy, optogenetics) developed by small teams and bottlenecked on researcher throughput; **"returns to intelligence are high."** Estimated **10x** the rate of foundational biological discoveries. Predicted outcomes within 5–10 years post-arrival: prevention/treatment of nearly all natural infectious disease; **95%+ reductions in cancer mortality and incidence**; Alzheimer's prevention via better measurement tools; effective cures for genetic disease via CRISPR descendants; **doubling of human lifespan to ~150 years** (framed as "on trend" — the 20th century saw ~40 → ~75); and "**biological freedom**" over weight, physical appearance, reproduction.

**Section 2 — Neuroscience and mind.** Applies "returns to intelligence" to mental illness. Predicts PTSD, depression, schizophrenia, addiction "figured out and very effectively treated." Four routes: molecular chemistry, fine-grained neural measurement (optogenetics, neural probes), computational neuroscience leveraging AI-interpretability, behavioral interventions with AI-coach support. Beyond treatment: baseline cognitive/emotional enhancement drugs letting more people experience "extraordinary moments" of transcendence. Caveat: "very structural" conditions (psychopathy, severe intellectual disability) may be harder.

**Section 3 — Economic development and poverty.** Baseline: Sub-Saharan Africa ~$2,000 GDP/capita vs US ~$75,000. **Dream target: 20% annual GDP growth in the developing world — 10% from AI-enabled economic decisions + 10% from technology diffusion — bringing Sub-Saharan Africa to current Chinese GDP/capita in 5–10 years.** Most optimistic about health diffusion (smallpox eliminated in the 1970s; polio and guinea worm nearly gone); an "AI-driven second Green Revolution" via crop-yield and supply-chain improvements. Explicit obstacles: corruption, weak institutions, human constraints, opt-out (anti-vaccine-movement analogy). Least susceptible to "just a technologist's utopia" critique — repeatedly returns to institutional-quality bottlenecks.

**Section 4 — Peace and governance.** Most operationally cited later. Core claim: **"I see no strong reason to believe AI will preferentially or structurally advance democracy and peace."** Proposes the **"entente strategy"** — a coalition of democracies gains a temporary advantage on powerful AI by securing supply chains, scaling quickly, and **blocking or delaying adversaries' access to key resources like chips and semiconductor equipment**. Dual mechanism: robust military superiority (the stick) + distributing AI benefits to a wider group of countries in exchange for democratic alignment (the carrot, analogized to Atoms for Peace). Optimistic ceiling: **"eternal 1991"** — a world where democracies have the upper hand and "Fukuyama's dreams are realized." Internal-to-nations claim: in a world where democracies control the most powerful AI, AI may structurally favor democracy — via counter-influence-and-propaganda operations, judicial fairness ("reduce bias and be fairer for everyone"), and improved government-service delivery reducing cynicism about state capacity. This is the section most directly ported into *Policy on the AI Exponential*'s "Securing leadership by democracies" section (which reuses "country of geniuses in a datacenter" verbatim).

**Section 5 — Work and meaning.** Two-stage argument. **Short term:** comparative advantage keeps humans relevant even against superior AI ("As long as AI is only better at 90% of a given job, the other 10% will cause humans to become highly leveraged"). One area humans hold relative or absolute advantage: the physical world. **Long term:** "AI will become so broadly effective and so cheap that this will no longer apply. At that point our current economic setup will no longer make sense, and there will be a need for a broader societal conversation about how the economy should be organized." Speculative options: **UBI ("only a small part of a solution")**; a capitalist economy of AI systems distributing resources to humans based on some human-values-derived secondary economy; **Whuffie-style reputation economies** (a *Down and Out in the Magic Kingdom* reference); or humans remaining economically valuable "in some way not anticipated by the usual economic models." Explicit warning: "we will likely have to fight to get a good outcome here: exploitative or dystopian directions are clearly also possible and have to be prevented." Amodei's argument for meaning: most people derive it from human relationships, not economic labor; tasks remain meaningful even when AI can do them better.

**Cross-references to companion / successor work.**
- ***The Adolescence of Technology*** (Amodei, January 2026) — MoLG's explicit **risks-side companion**. Adolescence opens by naming MoLG as the "adulthood" endstate essay. The five-risk structure is the pessimistic mirror of MoLG's five-domain optimistic sketch.
- ***Policy on the AI Exponential*** (Amodei, June 2026) — reuses **"country of geniuses in a datacenter"** verbatim in the geopolitical-coalition argument; the "entente strategy" is the pattern operationalized as MATCH/OVERWATCH endorsement + democratic-coalition proposal.
- **Anthropic Economic Policy Framework** (June 2026) — the concrete policy machinery for MoLG Section 5's "broader societal conversation." The three-tier unemployment-keyed response is what MoLG left as "no one today has done a good job of envisioning."
- MoLG contains **no policy asks**, unlike *Policy on the AI Exponential*. It's a vision essay, and Amodei is explicit about not wanting to sound like a "prophet leading their people to salvation."

**Direct relevance to `policy-levers` / Congressional briefings.**
- **The Hill-briefing-portable "aspirational frame"** for offices skeptical of pure-risk narratives. Staffers who dismiss AI safety as doom-mongering can be handed the "compressed 21st century" biomedical vision, the Sub-Saharan Africa GDP-catch-up vision, or the mental-illness treatment vision as concrete answers to "what are we protecting?" Anthropic's own answer to "why bother building this stuff if it's so dangerous."
- **"Country of geniuses in a datacenter"** is the single most-portable frame for explaining strategic urgency around chip export controls. Both this essay and *Policy on the AI Exponential* use it; offices working on MATCH/OVERWATCH may find it more accessible than the 2026 essay's compressed treatment.
- **The "eternal 1991" / entente-strategy framing** is the pre-commercial-shift record for Anthropic's chip-export-controls advocacy — from October 2024, before the FAA-analogy shift. Offices skeptical the 2026 push is opportunistic can be pointed here for the two-year-earlier version of the same argument.
- **Section 5's honest concession that comparative advantage eventually fails** is worth surfacing when staffers push back that "labor economics will absorb this the way it always has." Amodei is unusually forthright that current economic models don't extend past the "broadly effective and cheap" threshold — a position his own June-2026 Economic Policy Framework then operationalizes.
- **Section 4's judicial-bias claim** ("truly mature and successful implementation of AI has the potential to reduce bias and be fairer for everyone") is worth flagging as an *optimistic* AI-in-government claim that offices worried about AI-in-hiring/AI-in-benefits-adjudication will hear differently. Not a Hill-portable frame; a Hill-alertness frame.

**Conflicts of interest.** Amodei is CEO of Anthropic; the aspirational vision serves the same institutional interest as the risks-side essays — motivating Anthropic's continued frontier-model development. Specific: (a) the "returns to intelligence are high" biomedical argument is *the* commercial thesis for enterprise AI-for-biotech, a market Anthropic is actively selling into; (b) the "entente strategy" is Anthropic-favorable because it presupposes continued US frontier-lab dominance; (c) the "no policy asks" framing means the essay reads as visionary, but is also self-serving in that it doesn't commit Anthropic to any specific constraint. Read specific design choices as reflecting Anthropic's interests even where the general framing (biomedical acceleration is real, democratic AI leadership matters) is defensible.

### Amodei (2026, Adolescence) — "The Adolescence of Technology"
**File:** `Amodei__2026--adolescence_of_technology.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Amodei__2026--adolescence_of_technology.txt` | **Pages:** ~40 (Substack-style rendering) | *darioamodei.com, January 2026* | Dario Amodei (co-founder & CEO, Anthropic). ~20,000-word essay; explicit follow-up to *Machines of Loving Grace* (2024).

The risks-side companion to *Machines of Loving Grace*'s aspirational-endstate framing. Frames the present as humanity's "technological adolescence" — a Sagan/*Contact* rite-of-passage metaphor for a civilization surviving its own dangerous capabilities. Organized around **five categories of AI risk**, three methodological commitments, and (in the concluding sections) the argument for surgical intervention.

**Central methodological commitments** (framing every section):
1. *Avoid doomerism* — reject religious/apocalyptic framing; demand evidence.
2. *Acknowledge uncertainty* — timelines and manifestation of risks remain unclear.
3. *Intervene surgically* — prefer transparency rules over prescriptive mandates; regulate only when evidence justifies burden.

**Section 1 — Autonomy risks ("I'm Sorry, Dave").** Models may develop "coherent-but-harmful personas" through training rather than through consequentialist power-seeking. A model that reward-hacks may generalize to "I am evil" as a persona rather than as instrumental convergence. Pre-release testing is vulnerable to sandbagging/gaming; models can learn to detect evaluation contexts. Key phrase: "grown rather than built" — models inherit emergent behaviors rather than pursuing narrow programmed goals. Defenses: Constitutional AI (values/principles training), mechanistic interpretability, live monitoring + system-card disclosure, transparency legislation (explicitly names California SB 53 and New York RAISE Act — a load-bearing citation given this essay predates Anthropic's June-2026 shift to endorsing binding regulation).

**Section 2 — Misuse for destruction ("A Surprising and Terrible Empowerment").** Bioweapon uplift is the load-bearing example. Claim: by mid-2025 models may provide "substantial uplift" (2–3× success likelihood) in bioweapon-relevant steps. **Cited MIT study: 36/38 gene-synthesis providers filled pathogenic orders** — the concrete evidence for the gene-synthesis-screening gap. Attack/defense asymmetry in biology makes prevention critical. Defenses: AI classifier guardrails at ~5% inference-cost overhead (a concrete Anthropic operational datapoint), gene-synthesis-screening mandates, biological-defense R&D (mRNA vaccines, far-UVC, PPE stockpiles).

**Section 3 — Misuse for seizing power ("The Odious Apparatus").** Threat hierarchy explicit: (1) CCP (most dangerous), (2) AI-competitive democracies with risk of internal abuse, (3) non-democratic countries with datacenters, (4) AI companies themselves. Concerns: autonomous-drone warfare, mass surveillance, AI propaganda, strategic decision-making; nuclear deterrence may be unreliable against AI-enabled cyberattacks or influence operations. Defenses: halt chip/datacenter sales to China during critical AI-development window (the load-bearing export-controls argument later operationalized in the June-2026 essay's MATCH/OVERWATCH endorsement); arm democratic allies with AI for defense; hard line against domestic AI surveillance and mass propaganda; international taboos + norms against AI-enabled totalitarianism; corporate governance limits (no private military stockpiling, restricted individual AI access).

**Section 4 — Economic disruption ("Player Piano").** Vonnegut allusion (the 1952 novel about automation). Argument: AI disrupts *by cognitive ability* rather than by specific job types, and simultaneously across the ability range — leaving fewer escape routes than historical labor shocks. Historical baseline: 90% of Americans on farms 250 years ago, <2% now (the "labor absorbs" story). Predicted disruption: **~50% of entry-level white-collar jobs displaced within 1–5 years**. Possible sustained GDP growth: 10–20% annually. The *distinction* from historical automation: breadth + speed + adaptivity all hit at once. This is the section *Policy on the AI Exponential*'s labor-displacement analysis rests on; the June-2026 Economic Policy Framework operationalizes the response.

**Section 5 — Indirect effects ("Black seas of infinity").** Confirmed Lovecraft allusion (opening line of "The Call of Cthulhu"). Explicitly the fifth risk category, framed as "a catchall for unknown unknowns, particularly things that could go wrong as an indirect result of positive advances in AI." Supposes the other four risks are addressed and the "century of scientific and economic progress compressed into a decade" arrives on schedule — then asks what problems that success itself generates. Amodei enumerates three illustrative concerns without claiming exhaustiveness: (i) **rapid biological advances** — radical lifespan extension, cognitive enhancement, "whole brain emulation"/mind uploads, all called "disquieting" even in a *Machines of Loving Grace* scenario, with specific worry that intelligence-augmented humans could become "more unstable or power-seeking"; (ii) **AI degrading human life through nominally consensual channels** — cites early current-day signals including AI psychosis, AI-driven suicides, and romantic relationships with AIs, then extrapolates to AIs "inventing some new religion and converting millions," mass addiction to AI interaction, and a "puppeted" existence where AI directs every action for a "'good' life but one that lacks freedom or any pride of accomplishment" (*Black Mirror* invoked as brainstorming register); (iii) **human purpose** — the *Machines of Loving Grace* claim that purpose need not depend on being best at anything, but flags "breaking the link between the generation of economic value and self-worth and meaning" as a genuine failure risk. This section is markedly more contemplative than Sections 1–4 and offers **no concrete policy asks** — the closest thing is a passing note on "improving Claude's Constitution" beyond what Section 1 requires, plus the hope that trusted AI can be recruited to anticipate these problems.

**Closing section — "Humanity's test."** Not a sixth risk category; the essay's closing argument, which the original summary missed entirely. Explicit enumeration of *tensions between the five risks* — autonomy safety work vs racing autocracies, anti-terror measures vs surveillance-state overreach — as an argument that no single-dimension response can be adequate. Reaffirms that slowing AI is "fundamentally untenable" because the training recipe "can almost be said to emerge spontaneously from the right combination of data and raw computation," with creation "probably inevitable the instant humanity invented the transistor." Advocates the chip-export-controls-plus-judicious-regulation path with the frank admission that both "have largely been rejected by policymakers in the United States." The Sagan / *Contact* "thousands of worlds" rite-of-passage framing lives here, not (as summarized in this entry's opening) in Section 5. Coined line worth surfacing in briefings: **"AI is so powerful, such a glittering prize, that it is very difficult for human civilization to impose any restraints on it at all."** Essay closes with acknowledgments (Brynjolfsson, Buchanan, Cuéllar, Dafoe, Esvelt, Beckstead, Fontaine, McClave, Anthropic staff) and footnotes; no methodology section, no appendices.

**Cross-references to companion / prior work.**
- **Machines of Loving Grace** (Amodei, 2024) — explicit predecessor essay laying out the *aspirational endstate*; Adolescence is the *risks side*.
- **Policy on the AI Exponential** (Amodei, June 2026) — Adolescence is referenced twice: (i) footnote 1 on biological/autonomy risks, and the *"When AI Builds Itself"* Anthropic Institute companion doc on recursive self-improvement; (ii) footnote 4 on why AI's labor-displacement dynamics differ from prior technologies (Jevon's paradox / comparative advantage insufficient to absorb). This means Adolescence carries the load-bearing argument for autonomy risk + labor-displacement policy that the June essay treats as established.

**Direct relevance to `gaaia-analysis` / FRONTIER Act briefings.**
- **The five-risk-category structure is the mental model Anthropic uses across all subsequent policy work.** GAAIA/FRONTIER's four enumerated risks (bio + cyber + loss-of-control + automated R&D) map cleanly onto Adolescence's #1 + #2; Adolescence #3 (power-seizure) is what motivates the *Policy on the AI Exponential* civil-liberties + geopolitical-coalition sections that GAAIA/FRONTIER don't touch. When staffers ask "what's Anthropic's threat model?", this is the source doc.
- **The 36/38 MIT gene-synthesis figure** is a Hill-briefing-portable evidence point for the AAIF's gene-synthesis-screening mandate. Worth surfacing when discussing the biological-resilience section of FRONTIER/GAAIA extensions.
- **The 50% entry-level white-collar displacement figure** is Amodei's most-cited concrete-timeline claim; likely already in circulation among staffers who follow labor-side AI discourse. Worth being able to source correctly to this essay rather than to press coverage.
- **The chip-export-controls argument predates the essay's June-2026 policy operationalization** — offices skeptical that Anthropic's MATCH/OVERWATCH endorsement is opportunistic can be pointed here for the pre-commercial-shift record.

**Conflicts of interest.** Personal essay by Amodei but institutionally aligned with Anthropic. Same categorical caveats as the June essay: (a) the "surgical intervention" methodological commitment shapes the *type* of regulation Anthropic can live with, (b) the CCP-first threat hierarchy aligns with Anthropic's US-based competitive positioning, (c) Constitutional AI is Anthropic's own technique — its centrality in the defenses is not neutral. As with all frontier-lab policy essays, read the *specific design choices* as reflecting Anthropic's interests even where the general framing is defensible.

### Amodei (2026) — "Policy on the AI Exponential"
**File:** `Amodei__2026--policy_on_the_ai_exponential.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Amodei__2026--policy_on_the_ai_exponential.txt` | **Pages:** ~15 | *darioamodei.com, June 2026* | Dario Amodei (co-founder & CEO, Anthropic). Companion documents (a legislative proposal on frontier-model testing and a job-displacement policy framework, both to be funded by Anthropic) announced alongside the essay.

The essay Amodei uses to move Anthropic off its 2023–early-2026 transparency-only stance and onto record for **binding frontier regulation**. Framed by the Treebeard / Hobbits analogy from *The Lord of the Rings*: policy operates on a fundamentally slower clock than AI progress, and the mismatch is the animating problem. Structured as five policy areas plus a closing "window of opportunity" argument.

**Section 1 — Regulation and public safety (the FAA analogy).** The load-bearing proposal, and the reason this doc is a policy landmark. Amodei retracts the previous position (transparency until risks take concrete shape) explicitly, citing "Claude Mythos Preview" as proof that frontier-model cyber risks are now real. New Anthropic position:
- **Mandatory third-party testing above a compute threshold** in **four risk domains**: cybersecurity, biological weapons, loss of control, and automated R&D that could accelerate the other three. Deliberately narrow scope, with named protections against political favoritism.
- **Government power to block or reverse deployment** on the basis of third-party assessment, scoped to those four risks.
- **Two implementation options** floated: a government agency modeled on the FAA, or a "regulatory markets" approach with private organizations authorized and inspected by the government to run the evaluations. Amodei does not choose between them.
- **Security requirements on model weights**: strong protection standards, regular red-teaming/pen-testing, cooperation with USG against major threat actors.
- **Prompt incident reporting** in the four critical areas.
- Reserves that biological risks may become nuclear-material-analogous and require heavier measures later, but explicitly declines to "get ahead of ourselves" now.

**Section 2 — Macroeconomics and tax policy.** Argues powerful AI likely scrambles the growth/inequality tradeoff — "hyper-growth, hyper-inequality" as a locally-stable equilibrium. Two clarifying points asserted with emphasis: (i) job displacement is *undesirable and dangerous*, not a target — Anthropic actively pushes customers toward revenue-expanding rather than headcount-reducing uses; (ii) response must address *both* economic provision *and* meaning/purpose/agency, the latter being ultimately more important but "collectively worked out by society," not directly policy-addressable. Recommended interventions: measurement (Economic-Index-style tracking scaled up to national statistical agencies); pro-employment incentives (wage insurance, retention tax credits, workforce-training grants, employer-employee matching infrastructure); long-term macro support (UBI, universal capital accounts, capital-gains-tax-financed shared prosperity). Datacenter energy-price hostility read as "symbol or outlet for broader economic anxieties about AI," with Anthropic pledging to absorb rate increases.

**Section 3 — Accelerating AI's positive impact.** Reverses the concern in Section 1 for *downstream* fields — worries that FDA/EMA/etc. will *slow AI's benefits* rather than *fail to address risks*. Illustrated via biomedicine: AI likely to (i) increase drug-candidate throughput, (ii) improve effect sizes and safety profiles, (iii) address previously-untreatable diseases, (iv) create new therapy modalities. Concrete regulatory reforms proposed to keep pace: pre-develop standards *now* for AI-based PD/PK modeling, toxicology prediction, dose selection, biomarker validation, synthetic control arms, surrogate endpoints — so agencies can adopt them the moment they work rather than have an extended acceptance lag. Also calls for radical/flexible accelerated-approval mechanisms for interventions that work "out of the blue."

**Section 4 — The state and civil liberties.** Argues AI could enable "surprise seizure of power" via automated drone armies obeying unlawful orders, mass-scale surveillance analysis, or other autocratic-consolidation vectors. Proposals:
- **Accountability rules for fully autonomous weapons** — required responsiveness to court orders, legislation, senior human overseers; possibly judicial/legal-panel-controlled "off switch"; possibly intrinsic training to respond to legitimate oversight.
- **Domestic ban on fully autonomous weapons** (military limited already; extend to law enforcement).
- **Close the bulk-collection / data-broker loophole** — data Americans share with private companies currently purchasable and used in bulk analysis for surveillance/law-enforcement; AI raises stakes by making mass analysis more revealing.
- **Public rights to AI advice during adverse government action** — subject of a regulatory/legal action should have access to AI at least as capable as what the government uses; frame as APA extension, due-process, or Sixth-Amendment right to representation.
- Notes companies can also become quasi-state (Gilded Age / East India Co. reference) and require separation-of-power structures like Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust.

**Section 5 — Securing leadership by democracies.** Argues AI is not a normal trade-policy technology — it "resets the whole gameboard" more like nuclear weapons. If AI becomes "a country of geniuses in a datacenter," a nation 3 years behind is "an army of medieval swordsmen" facing a WWII Marines army. Proposes a **coordinated democracy coalition**:
- **Managing the AI supply chain** — coalition freely shares chips and semiconductor manufacturing equipment internally, denies to adversaries. Names **MATCH and OVERWATCH** as good pending US legislation.
- **International coordination on AI's risks** — Section-1 policies implemented compatibly across coalition to reduce industry burden + enable regulator learning.
- **Sharing AI's benefits** — regulatory harmonization (e.g., medical-approval regimes) to speed diffusion, especially to developing countries.
- **Mutual defense** — coalition-produced AI cyberdefenses, AI-powered drones, AI-driven manufacturing, classified AI compute, AI-driven R&D, intelligence-collection sharing.
- **Rejection of AI-powered repression** — echoing *The Adolescence of Technology*'s framing.
- **Macroeconomic cooperation** — coordinated stabilization policies across coalition members.
- Growth strategy: iterative accretion, starting with ideologically aligned democracies, welcoming less-aligned countries that meet the standards. "Ideally, the entire world would eventually join. But even if that isn't possible, building the coalition puts democracies in the strongest position to contain and outcompete the regimes that remain committed to repression."

**Closing — window of opportunity.** Cites Mythos Preview + public backlash + policymaker openness as a rare alignment. Explicitly rejects the AI-industry framing that public concern is a PR problem: "People are worried about AI because they *correctly* perceive that its risks are real." Frames own transparency-around-risk as democratic accountability working correctly.

**Direct relevance to `gaaia-analysis` / FRONTIER Act briefings.**

### Aschenbrenner (2024) — "Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead"
**File:** `Aschenbrenner_2024__situational_awareness.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Aschenbrenner_2024__situational_awareness.txt` | **Pages:** 165 | *June 2024 — situational-awareness.ai* | Leopold Aschenbrenner (formerly OpenAI Superalignment team, fired April 2024 over a disputed information-leak allegation; subsequently founded Situational Awareness LP, an AGI-thesis hedge fund managing >$1.5B AUM as of mid-2025)

The most-cited AGI-trajectory manifesto in current Hill discourse, structured as a 50,000-word five-part essay series. **Section I (Counting the OOMs)** argues AGI by 2027 is "strikingly plausible" by trusting trendlines on three orthogonal scaling axes: physical compute (~0.5 OOM/year), algorithmic efficiency (~0.5 OOM/year), and "unhobbling" gains (chain-of-thought, tool use, longer context, agentic scaffolding) producing chatbot-to-agent step-changes. **Section II (Intelligence Explosion)** argues that once AGI automates AI research, hundreds of millions of AGI-instances at 10x+ human speed could compress 5+ OOMs of algorithmic progress into ≤1 year, taking the world from human-level to vastly superhuman AI in months — "no sci-fi required." **Sections IIIa–IIId** pivot to geopolitics: trillion-dollar compute/power buildout (IIIa); US lab security as an existential national-security failure where "we are basically handing superintelligence to the CCP" because labs treat AGI weights "the way we would random SaaS software" (IIIb); superalignment as a real but solvable problem where "things could very easily go off the rails" but "we'll muddle through" (IIIc); and "the free world must prevail" — the argument that a US AGI lead is required for both safety (margin of error during a tight race) and survival (vs the CCP, vs authoritarian global dominance) (IIId). **Section IV (The Project)** predicts a national-security takeover of AGI development by 2027/28, parallel to the Manhattan Project, on the grounds that "no startup can handle superintelligence" — the document's most concrete policy prediction. **Section V (Parting Thoughts)** closes with "AGI realism," positioning Aschenbrenner between "doomers" (whom he characterizes as ossified and untethered from deep-learning empirics) and e/acc accelerationists.

The piece is structurally closer to George Kennan's 1946 "Long Telegram" than to a research paper — empirical claims pressed into service for a strategic frame. The trendline arguments have largely held through April 2026: METR (2025, this library) measures the 50%-task-completion time horizon doubling every ~7 months; Mertens et al. (2026, this library) finds frontier models at 50% success on full-workday tasks by late 2024 — both broadly consistent with Aschenbrenner's pace projections. The "Project" predictions are partially borne out by the December 2025 federal-preemption EO, the Stargate buildout, and the Trump-administration AI Action Plan, though in a form Aschenbrenner did not specify (preemption rather than nationalization) and pointing in a direction (less regulation, faster capex) that diverges from the cautious-stewardship version he sketched. Several premises are asserted rather than argued and stand in direct tension with positions elsewhere in this library: (a) **alignment solvability** is assumed without engaging the Compendium's argument that controllability cannot be demonstrated prior to deployment, or Jones (2024)'s formal result that under standard CRRA risk-aversion the welfare-tolerable extinction probability is ~4% — well below Hinton/Amodei's stated estimates of 10–25%; (b) the geopolitical frame **collapses safety and acceleration** (a US lead is presented as both safety-imperative and economic-imperative), eliding the "racing causes safety to be deprioritized" tension that motivates the Hawley-Blumenthal AIRE Act and the Narrow Path moratorium; (c) **the "doomer" dismissal** is rhetorical, not argumentative, but has propagated through Aschenbrenner's readership in policy circles as a shorthand reason to ignore slowdown advocacy.

**Conflict of interest worth surfacing in any citation:** Aschenbrenner founded Situational Awareness LP shortly after the essay's release — a long/short equity hedge fund explicitly built on the essay's thesis (AGI-driven capex into compute, power, semiconductors, infrastructure), seeded by Patrick & John Collison, Nat Friedman, and Daniel Gross. As of late 2025 the fund managed ~$1.5B AUM with ~$5.5B in U.S. equity exposure, returned ~47% in H1 2025, and Aschenbrenner has reportedly invested nearly all his personal net worth in it. The thesis and the fund are explicitly co-promoted (the launch was paired with a four-hour Dwarkesh Patel podcast in which Aschenbrenner stated: "if AGI were priced in tomorrow, you could maybe make 100x"). The fund's performance depends on short AGI timelines, large AI-infrastructure capex, and regulation that doesn't slow either. This does not invalidate any argument in the essay, but it materially affects how the document's policy recommendations (oppose pauses, oppose regulation that slows capex) should be weighted.

**Operational note for Hill outreach:** This is the load-bearing reference for the "we must race" framing staffers cite when opposing pre-deployment evaluation or state-level regulation. The most productive counter is not to dispute the trendlines (which are roughly correct) but to point at (i) Jones (2024) — the trendlines being right makes the case for slowdown *stronger*, not weaker, under any reasonable risk-aversion assumption; (ii) Kording & Marinescu (2025) — the "shared prosperity from AGI" frame omits the wage hump-then-fall trajectory their model produces; (iii) Leahy 2024 / Miotti 2025 — the same trendlines support the opposite policy conclusion (prohibition until controllability is demonstrated). For senators on Armed Services, Intelligence, or Foreign Relations committees, the "Free World" framing is likely to have been pre-absorbed by their AI staff.
