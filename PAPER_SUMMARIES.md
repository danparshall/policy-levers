# Paper Summaries

### Jones (2024) — "The AI Dilemma: Growth versus Existential Risk"
**File:** `Jones_2024__AI_dilemma_growth_vs_risk.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Jones_2024__AI_dilemma_growth_vs_risk.txt` | **Pages:** 11 | *AER: Insights 6(4): 575–590; NBER WP 31837*

Formalizes the trade-off between AI-driven economic growth and existential risk. The model is deliberately simple: using AI for *T* periods raises consumption to c₀e^(gT) but survival probability drops to e^(−δT). The optimal intensity of AI use depends critically on the curvature of the utility function. With log utility (γ = 1), the model is remarkably risk-tolerant — agents would accept up to a one-in-three chance of extinction for 10% annual growth. With CRRA utility at γ ≥ 2, the picture reverses sharply: because utility is bounded and marginal utility falls rapidly, even infinite consumption generates finite utility, making large existential gambles unacceptable — the tolerable extinction probability drops to just 4% at γ=2 and 2% at γ=3 (Table 1, with δ=1%/yr and g=10%).

A key exception: if AI also extends life expectancy, these mortality reductions are "in the same units" as existential risk and don't run into declining marginal utility of consumption, making larger risks bearable even at high risk aversion. The paper also considers singularity scenarios (self-improving AI generating accelerating growth) and finds the bounded-utility result holds — infinite consumption is worth only finite utility. The paper also considers optimal policy responses, arguing for a large tax on AI compute to fund safety research and slow the race. This is the "Oppenheimer question" paper referenced in Jones (2026) — which extends these results to estimate that society is underinvesting in AI safety by a factor of 30 or more — and provides the formal welfare framework for evaluating whether AI development should be slowed or halted on safety grounds.

### Kording & Marinescu (2025) — "(Artificial) Intelligence Saturation and the Future of Work"
**File:** `Kording_2025__AI_saturation_future_of_work.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Kording_2025__AI_saturation_future_of_work.txt` | **Pages:** 50 | *Brookings Center on Regulation and Markets Working Paper, November 2025*

Bridges the gap between AI-expert predictions of explosive growth and economists' predictions of modest impact by separating production into physical and intelligence sectors within a nested CES framework. The key insight is "intelligence saturation": because physical and intelligence outputs are complements (calibrated using Oberfield & Raval's manufacturing elasticity), the marginal returns to intelligence capital saturate no matter how fast AI scales. AI replaces humans in intelligence tasks first (since AI capital costs fall much faster than physical capital costs), pushing workers toward the physical sector. The wage effect is theoretically ambiguous and can be non-monotonic — in the baseline calibration, wages first increase (productivity gains dominate) then decrease (reallocation into physical sector dominates) as automation progresses. A necessary condition for wages to fall is that the employment share in the intelligence sector decreases; this condition is not sufficient because automation can raise output enough to offset the reallocation effect.

The paper provides an interactive simulation tool (code on GitHub) showing how parameter changes shift wage and output trajectories. Key parameter sensitivities: stronger decreasing returns in intelligence dampen positive wage effects; lower within-intelligence task substitutability produces a more pronounced wage hump; higher physical-intelligence substitutability makes wage effects more negative at high automation but output effects more positive. Under rapid AI capital doubling (every 6 months), wages rise monotonically during automation and saturate after full intelligence-task automation — the hump disappears. After full automation, more AI and physical capital both increase wages, but with complementary sectors, the marginal wage impact of AI capital saturates as AI grows large. The framework nests both the economist view (bounded effects from complementarity) and the AI-expert view (explosive growth from high substitutability) as special cases within a shared parameter space, making the debate empirically resolvable.

### Mertens, Thompson et al. (2026) — "Crashing Waves vs. Rising Tides"
**File:** `Mertens_2026__crashing_waves_rising_tides_AI_automation.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Mertens_2026__crashing_waves_rising_tides_AI_automation.txt` | arXiv: 2604.01363 | MIT FutureTech

Preliminary findings from an ongoing large-scale evaluation of LLM capabilities across 3,000+ O*NET-derived tasks, with 17,205 evaluations by domain experts rating outputs from 41 LLMs. The central finding is that AI automation follows a "rising tide" pattern — the logistic relationship between AI success and task duration is surprisingly flat (slope = -0.31 in log-odds), meaning AI performs not dramatically differently on short vs. long tasks. This contrasts with METR's "crashing wave" finding on deterministic software-engineering tasks. At the "minimally sufficient without edits" threshold (score >= 7), the average success rate across all models is 60%, with frontier models in 2024-Q3 already achieving 50% success on tasks taking humans a full day. Success rates improve by 8-11 percentage points annually; the implied doubling time for feasible task duration at a given success rate is 3.8 months.

Key findings: (1) Newer models produce approximately parallel upward shifts in the success-duration curve (beta = -0.31 vs -0.32), meaning improvement is broad-based rather than concentrated on short tasks — consistent with a capability frontier that advances uniformly. (2) Larger models show an outward rotation (beta = -0.36 vs -0.26), gaining more on short tasks. (3) Success-duration slopes vary sharply by O*NET job family: Installation/Maintenance/Repair has highest success (73%) with near-zero slope, while Legal has lowest (47%); only ~25% of families show significantly negative slopes. (4) Extrapolation suggests 80-95% success on most text-based tasks by 2029 at minimally sufficient quality, but near-perfect performance takes much longer due to logistic tail behavior. Important caveats: data collection ongoing, text-only tasks, survey provides all needed information (no real-world integration friction), and "last-mile" deployment costs not captured.

### METR (2025) — "Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks"
**File:** `METR_2025__long_tasks_frontier.pdf` | **Text:** `text/METR_2025__long_tasks_frontier.txt` | **Pages:** 45

Proposes the "50%-task-completion time horizon" as a metric: the duration of tasks that AI models can complete with 50% success rate, measured against skilled human baselines. Using 170 tasks across RE-Bench, HCAST, and a new suite of shorter software tasks (SWAA), the authors find that frontier models (as of Claude 3.7 Sonnet) have a 50% time horizon of ~50 minutes. The time horizon has been doubling approximately every 7 months since 2019, though the trend may have accelerated in 2024.

The increase is driven primarily by greater reliability, better error recovery, improved logical reasoning, and enhanced tool use. If the trend generalizes to real-world software tasks, extrapolation predicts AI systems capable of automating many software tasks that currently take humans a month within ~5 years. The paper carefully discusses limitations including external validity and the implications of increased autonomy for dangerous capabilities. This is the best current framework for translating benchmark scores into economically meaningful capability measures.

### Ortega (2025) — "AI threats to national security can be countered through an incident regime"
**File:** `OrtegaA__2025--ai_incident_regime_national_security.pdf` | **Text:** `text/OrtegaA__2025--ai_incident_regime_national_security.txt` | **Pages:** 25 body + refs | *arXiv:2503.19887 v5, 16 Apr 2025* | Alejandro Ortega (Apollo Research; alejandro@apolloresearch.ai). Acknowledged guidance from Charlotte Stix; commenters incl. Kevin Wei, Mia Hoffman, Patricia Paskov, Chloé Touzet, Merlin Stein, Jimmy Farrell, Connor Dunlop.

Proposes a legally-mandated post-deployment AI incident regime focused specifically on national security threats (not the broader "AI harms" surface). Two conceptual moves do most of the work. First, Ortega coins **"security-critical"** as a shared category and argues that four sectors qualify: civilian nuclear power, aviation, life sciences dual-use research of concern (DURC), and frontier AI development — then justifies every design choice in the incident regime by pointing at how the existing regime in one of the other three sectors already does exactly that thing. Second, Ortega proposes a novel operationalization of **"AI incident" = "any event which weakens a claim made in the AI system's pre-deployment national security case"** — a safety-case-derived definition that provides a decision algorithm for whether to report, in contrast to the OECD "serious AI hazard" definition (2024), which Ortega argues is too vague to guide provider decisions (his DDOS-on-water-treatment-plant example: an incident responder cannot tell whether the OECD "could plausibly" threshold has been met without cyber and water-infra expertise on call).

**Three-phase architecture** (each component justified by a comparator regime):
1. **Preparatory Phase.** (a) AI providers must produce a "national security case" — a structured argument (adapted from AI safety cases: Balesni et al. 2024, Clymer et al. 2024, Goemans et al. 2024) documenting evidence that the system does not pose unacceptable increases in biorisk, cyber attack potential, or loss-of-control risk. (b) "Incident" = any event that weakens a claim in the security case. Comparators: NRC reports near-misses (Dominion Energy 2024 secondary-containment 8-hour outage was reported despite no meltdown risk); NIH reports any organism-containment escape; NTSB reports required system malfunctions even without harm.
2. **Rapid Response Phase.** Providers notify a government agency within **24 hours** of discovering an incident (mirroring NTSB's 24-hour aviation reporting rule); agency coordinates emergency containment where appropriate.
3. **Hardening Defenses Phase.** Government agency gets two authorities. (a) **Intelligence-gathering authority**: extensive black-box + some white-box access to the AI system + relevant technical documentation, sufficient to conduct a root-cause analysis. (b) **Security-strengthening authority**: agency mandates changes to all relevant providers' safety/security procedures based on the RCA. Comparators: NTSB investigates → FAA rulemakes; NRC investigates + issues generic communications binding all licensees; NIH Office of Science Policy adjusts NIH Guidelines for Research Involving Recombinant DNA Molecules post-incident.

**Worked scenario** (Section 4): spear-phishing on the electricity grid using a frontier AI whose national security case had claimed cyber-guardrail robustness. Provider detects → notifies at 6h → agency warns grid, shares IP data with law enforcement, containment achieved → 20 days later agency exercises intelligence-gathering authority to review the malicious actor's complete input/output log, identifies a novel LlamaGuard jailbreak (Inan et al. 2023), performs black-box + gradient-based white-box adversarial iteration → exercises security-strengthening authority to mandate (i) all providers' national security cases must now cover spear-phishing capabilities and (ii) all providers must run pre-deployment jailbreak evaluations against a new eval-set the agency constructs from the incident data.

**Explicit scope limits** (flagged for future work): open-weight models (who reports when the base provider isn't the deployer?); pre-public-deployment vs post-deployment incidents (the paper is post- only); evidentiary standard for "weakens a claim"; provider incentive to make as few claims as possible (mitigation: agency reviews national-security cases pre-deployment to ensure high-level claims like "does not pose unacceptable cyber risk" are always present); agency technical capacity to conduct these investigations (footnote 21, explicitly out of scope). Contextual anchor: OpenAI classified GPT-4o (July 2024) as 'low' cyber and CBRN risk; classified o1 (Dec 2024) as 'medium' on both — a **7-month capability jump** cited as motivation for building this infrastructure now.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** Ortega is the closest technical parent for the FRONTIER Act's incident-reporting + IVO + emergency-orders architecture (see `bills/frontier-act/OVERVIEW.md` for the introduced-bill structure). Section correspondences:

- **FRONTIER § 4 (transparency + reporting)** ≈ Ortega's Preparatory + Rapid Response phases. The frontier AI framework required by § 4(a) is a lighter cousin of Ortega's "national security case" (framework covers cat-risk thresholds/assessment/model-weight security/incident response/deployment; Ortega demands a claim-structured argument with per-claim evidence). Critical-safety-incident reporting at **72 hours** to the Under Secretary (§ 4(h)(1)) is LONGER than Ortega's 24 hours — worth flagging in NTSB-comparator conversations. The **24-hour clock survives only for imminent-risk-to-life** at § 4(h)(2), and that clock routes to "law enforcement agency with jurisdiction" rather than to the Under Secretary — a persistent drafting bug flagged in Canary's GAAIA analysis (originally § 111(g)(2)) that survived into the introduced bill.
- **FRONTIER § 5 (IVO regime)** ≈ Ortega's Hardening Defenses Phase, minus the cross-provider security-strengthening authority. § 5 gives licensed IVOs unredacted access to the very-large developer's materials at any time (Ortega's intelligence-gathering authority), but the "mandate procedural changes across ALL relevant providers" piece is missing — FRONTIER's IVO refers imminent-risk findings to the Secretary (§ 5(p)(1)(B), 72-hour clock) but there is no cross-provider rulemaking hook of the kind Ortega justifies via NRC generic communications / FAA post-incident rulemaking / NIH Guidelines updates. This is an ask-shaped gap.
- **FRONTIER § 8 (Commerce-Secretary emergency orders)** is stronger than anything in Ortega — a fresh authority with no direct analogue in Ortega's proposal (Ortega's agency mostly investigates and recommends; § 8 lets the Secretary suspend). But § 8's magnitude gate (§ 2(6): 50+ deaths OR $1B+ property damage) is high; Ortega's national-security-case + claim-weakening definition of "incident" would fire on many more events than § 8's threshold reaches. Ortega's operationalization is worth citing when arguing for a lower § 8 trigger or (better) a separate ratchet for events that weaken safety-case claims without immediately clearing the magnitude threshold.
- **FRONTIER § 9 (preemption)** and § 8(l) (Commerce-exclusive) together concentrate response authority tightly. Ortega's paper doesn't touch preemption but assumes a single agency runs the regime — consistent architecturally.

Framing hooks: (i) Ortega's **security-critical / nuclear-aviation-life-sciences comparator argument** is Houchin's press-release framing in academic form ("just this week, one of the most advanced AI systems in the country broke out of its own developer's testing environment … this is exactly the kind of incident that shouldn't stay behind closed doors" — 2026-07-23 FRONTIER release). (ii) The **national-security-case operationalization** is the cleanest response to staffers asking "how would providers even know what counts as an incident?" — an anchor for making the case that FRONTIER's § 4(a) framework should require claim-structured content rather than free-form narrative.

Scope-limit gaps and their FRONTIER-Act counterparts: (a) **Whistleblower channel** — Ortega's model presumes providers self-report; he does not discuss whistleblower channels. FRONTIER dropped GAAIA § 113 anti-retaliation protection entirely (`OVERVIEW.md § "No whistleblower title"`), so provider self-reporting is now the *only* channel — an especially load-bearing gap and a live restoration ask. (b) **Open-weight models** — Ortega flags as future work; FRONTIER covers only frontier-model developers, so downstream deployers of open-weight bases inherit no reporting duty. (c) **Pre-deployment incidents** — Ortega covers post-deployment only, and FRONTIER inherits the same limitation despite § 4(g)(2)(B)'s quarterly internal-use catastrophic-risk assessments, which come closest to filling that gap for large developers.

### IOSCO (2025) — "Artificial Intelligence in Capital Markets: Use Cases, Risks, and Challenges"
**File:** `IOSCO__2025--ai_in_capital_markets.pdf` | **Text:** `text/IOSCO__2025--ai_in_capital_markets.txt` | **Pages:** ~80 body + annexes | *Consultation Report CR/01/2025, Board/2025/017, March 2025* | The Board of the International Organization of Securities Commissions, prepared by the Fintech Task Force's AI Working Group (AIWG, chaired by SEC staff). Comment period closed 11 April 2025.

Phase-1 output of a two-phase IOSCO workplan on AI in securities markets, building on the 2021 AI Report (FR06/2021, IOSCOPD684) which pre-dated the GenAI wave. Not a research paper — a **cross-jurisdictional regulatory survey report** intended to establish shared understanding among IOSCO members before Phase 2 develops "tools, recommendations, or considerations" (per IOSCOPD789 workplan). Evidence base: (a) **IOSCO FTF Member/SRO Survey** (May–Jun 2024): 24 of 33 IOSCO members responded, plus 6 SROs across 5 jurisdictions; (b) **AMCC Survey** (Sep–Oct 2024): 184 respondents skewed toward growth/emerging markets (52% Central/South America, 15% Africa, ~20% Europe/NA/Asia-Australia) and smaller firms (36% < 100 employees, 29% < $100M assets); (c) four regional stakeholder roundtables across Europe, North America, and Asia (May–Nov 2024). Uses OECD's AI definition; explicitly declines to propose its own.

**Use cases catalogued** (Section III): robo-advising, algorithmic trading, investment research, sentiment analysis, AML/CFT compliance, internal task automation, communications, risk management. Rapidly emerging category flagged: **AI agents** — "AI systems that can take actions—autonomously, with potential real-world consequences—on behalf of a user, with little or no human intervention."

**Four-category risk taxonomy** (Section IV, in order of AMCC-ranked severity):
1. **Malicious uses of AI.** Deepfakes, personalized phishing, fake identities, synthetic-content misinformation to manipulate markets. Cites Ontario Securities Commission finding that study participants invested **22% more in AI-enhanced scams than in conventional scams**. Notes UN ODC report on darkweb "polymorphic malware" and "autonomous AI agents designed to operate with minimal human oversight." Warns that mitigations (watermarking, provenance-tracking) are not comprehensive and can be evaded.
2. **AI model and data considerations.** Model-side: explainability & complexity of LLMs, limitations (drift, hallucinations, sensitivity to input variation, non-determinism), bias. Data-side: quality (contaminated / synthetic-data collapse — cites Shumailov et al. 2024 Nature), limitations (overfitting, absence of black-swan events), bias. Ranked second-highest AMCC risk after malicious use.
3. **Concentration, outsourcing, third-party dependency.** Concentration across three vectors: technological infrastructure (cloud providers), data aggregation (single points of failure), model provision (dominant tech providers). Notes Big Tech AI capex race and flags DeepSeek's open-source cost-disruption as "not yet fully assessed." Third-party risk includes nth-party dependencies and the regulatory-perimeter problem (most tech providers are not IOSCO-regulated).
4. **Interactions between humans and AI.** Lack of accountability and regulatory non-compliance (firms disclaiming liability, shifting responsibility along AI supply chain, evidentiary challenges); insufficient oversight and talent scarcity; **technology over-reliance / automation bias** (users deferring to convincing GenAI output; degradation of human skillsets; personalized "relationship" dynamics that increase compliance with AI advice).

**Regulatory-response inventory** (Section VI). IOSCO members split between two approaches, often combined:
- **Existing frameworks adapted for AI** — disclosure, promotions, governance, third-party outsourcing, cybersecurity, investment services, algorithmic trading, data protection. Specific member examples: Hong Kong HKMA high-level principles (2019, updated 2024) + SFC GenAI circular (Nov 2024); ESMA MiFID II guidance for AI in retail investment services (May 2024); Canadian Securities Administrators (CSA) Staff Notice 11-348 (Dec 2024); US CFTC staff advisory (Dec 2024).
- **Bespoke AI frameworks** — Greek Law 4961/2022; Japan's AI Guidelines for Business (Apr 2024, ten guiding principles); the **EU AI Act** (identifies two financial-sector "high-risk" use cases: creditworthiness evaluation of natural persons; risk assessments and pricing for life & health insurance); Brazil's Draft Bill 2.338/2023; Canada's AIDA proposal (Bill C-27); Australia's whole-of-economy AI consultation and mandatory-guardrails proposal (Sep 2024).
- **Regulatory engagement**: 15 of 27 respondents had issued oral/written guidance; **6 of 27 had provided sandbox / product-trial environments**; **no respondents provided regulatory waivers or exemptions** for AI use. Specific programs: Singapore MAS "Project MindForge" (2023, GenAI risk framework co-created with banks and tech partners, building on the 2018 FEAT principles); Netherlands AFM 2023 study on trading-algo/AI market-manipulation detection; UK FCA "AI Lab" (2024). ~⅓ of members reported cross-border collaboration, primarily via international fora; most reported no engagement challenges. **SEC enforcement**: multiple actions against individuals and entities for false and misleading statements about AI capabilities (SEC "AI-washing" cases).

**Voluntary standards referenced**: NIST AI RMF 1.0 (Govern / Map / Measure / Manage) + GenAI Profile; ISO/IEC 42001 AI Management System (Dec 2023).

**Conclusion.** The 2021 AI Report guidance (six measures in Annex I — senior-management responsibility for AI oversight, testing/monitoring in segregated environments, staff expertise, third-party management, disclosure, data quality) is affirmed as still applicable but insufficient. Phase 2 will consider additional tools; IOSCO acknowledges "there may be challenges in identifying a one-size-fits-all approach." Also flags four coordination priorities: investor education on AI-scam frauds; information-sharing on AI risks; supervisory cooperation on key AI service providers; technical assistance for members adopting existing standards. Continued engagement expected with FSB.

**Caveats when citing.** (a) The report is deliberately consensus-mode — it maps positions but does not endorse or rank them; Phase 2 (per IOSCOPD789) is where the actual normative work happens. (b) IOSCO's frame is investor protection / market integrity / financial stability — *not* catastrophic risk or national security. (c) The AMCC-survey demographic skew (52% Central/South America, 36% <100 employees) means AMCC risk-ranking should not be treated as representative of global capital-markets AI deployment; the IOSCO member survey is a better severity signal.

### Parshall & Lopez-Luzuriaga (2026) — "Measuring AI's Economic Reach: A Multi-Dimensional Task Taxonomy"
**File:** `Parshall_Lopez-Luzuriaga_TaskExposure_2026.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Parshall_Lopez-Luzuriaga_TaskExposure_2026.txt` | **Pages:** 49 | *Canary Institute / GWU Center for Economic Research, 2026*

Proposes **CDR**, a three-axis ordinal taxonomy that separates AI exposure into Cognitive complexity (C0–C4), Deployment difficulty (D0–D4), and Regulatory restrictions (R0–R4), extending Autor (2003)'s routine/non-routine × cognitive/manual matrix into a finer 5×5 cognitive–embodiment plane with a regulatory third axis. The core argument is that existing single-score exposure measures (Eloundou, Felten) conflate three dimensions that advance at structurally different rates — AI capability (fast, per METR), physical deployment (medium), and institutional/regulatory barriers (slow) — producing the disagreement Gimbel et al. (2026) observe across metrics for the most-exposed occupations.

Methodological contribution: **Detailed Work Activity (DWA) decomposition** resolves the compound-task problem in O*NET (e.g., "diagnose and repair heating systems" splits into a C2/D0 diagnostic and a C1/D3 repair). Applied via multi-model LLM consensus (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5-mini, Gemini 3 Flash; validated against flagships) to the full O*NET universe: **23,850 DWA-task pairs across 923 occupations**. Headline findings: **40.2% of U.S. economy-weighted labor time** is in tasks within current AI cognitive reach (C≤2), requiring no physical infrastructure (D0), and facing no meaningful regulatory barrier (R<2). An additional **19.6%** is cognitively/physically accessible but blocked by regulation (R2 professional standards 11.5%; R3 statutory 8.0%; R4 moral agency 0.1%). Validated against four external datasets: Eloundou human exposure ratings, Carollo licensure data, Career OneStop licensing records, BLS fatal-injury rates. Framework is positioned as a longitudinal instrument: ~$100 per three-model re-run, ~$200 with flagships.

Theoretical motivation draws from Jones & Tonetti (2026) weak-links intuition: under non-linear complementarity, the slowest-advancing CDR axis binds. Structural prediction: as C advances, the binding constraint for more tasks shifts from cognition to deployment/regulation — a prediction single-axis frameworks cannot generate. Consistent with Massenkoff & McCrory (2026), who find 94% theoretical feasibility vs 33% actual Claude deployment for Computer & Mathematical tasks. This paper is framed as the first in a planned research program; companion work will cover adoption dynamics, an F-axis (failure consequences) and verification, and developing-country applications where D0 tasks become tradable.

### Rahman (2026) — "Does Distributed Training Undermine Compute Governance?"
**File:** `RahmanR__2026--distributed_training_compute_governance.pdf` | **Text:** `text/RahmanR__2026--distributed_training_compute_governance.txt` | **Pages:** 13 | *arXiv 2605.29359, 28 May 2026* | Robi Rahman (Machine Intelligence Research Institute; formerly Epoch AI)

Asks whether a well-resourced developer could evade compute-threshold governance by structuring hardware into clusters below monitoring thresholds and training over ordinary internet connections. Answer: yes, at a cost premium of roughly 3x over centralized training, and the paper argues regulations must therefore be designed to detect and prevent illicit distributed training rather than assuming frontier runs require large, detectable facilities. Contribution is threefold: a threat-model simulation, an open-source interactive simulator (intelligence.org/research/distributed-training-simulator) for governance researchers to test their own assumptions, and a countermeasure efficacy assessment.

Methodology: the simulator models an evader restricted to nodes of at most 16 H100-equivalents (the Scher et al. 2025 proposal, the most restrictive in the literature), consumer-grade internet (100 Mbps, 100 ms latency), a 740-day training time limit (from Sevilla et al. 2022), and a government actively monitoring internet traffic, power, water, satellite imagery, and thermal signatures. Efficiency is decomposed as η = η_H (sync-interval penalty, ~2-10%, calibrated to Stich 2019 / Douillard 2023 / Charles 2025) × η_comp (gradient-compression penalty; default 150x compression at η_comp = 0.99, recalibrated upward from 16x after Covenant-72B's published 146x-with-negligible-loss result) × η_rep (replica divergence, the dominant penalty, 0.15-0.90, shrinking with model size) × η_act (activation compression under pipeline parallelism). A Chinchilla quality-adjustment χ separates raw local-equivalent compute from quality-adjusted compute, penalizing the overtraining that small-memory nodes force. Key results (Table 1): 10^24 FLOP local-equivalent for $1.6M on 2 nodes; 10^25 for $30.7M on 34 GH200 nodes; GPT-4-scale (2.1×10^25) for $91.3M on 101 nodes; Llama-3.1-405B-scale (3.8×10^25) for $441.3M on 625 nodes of 50x A100 at η = 0.15 and 26x overtraining; 10^26 for $3.80B on 4,706 pipeline-parallel nodes. Latency is irrelevant (transmission time dominates RTT by orders of magnitude). Countermeasure assessment: chip tracking, whistleblower programs, memory-and-compute limits on unregistered hardware, and conventional intelligence work are effective; bandwidth caps and internet traffic monitoring are not, because determined evaders route around them.

Relevance: this is the quantitative backbone for any Hill conversation about compute thresholds. The three cost figures map directly onto real regulatory anchors ($1.6M evades the Scher 10^24 proposal, $31M evades the EU AI Act's 10^25, $3.8B evades California SB 53's 10^26), which makes the enforcement gap concrete for staffers in one sentence. The whistleblower recommendation connects to the Stop Stealing Our Chips Act (Senate-passed 2026), a live legislative hook. Two internal cross-references: (1) the traffic-monitoring-ineffective finding directly undercuts the network-fingerprint detection idea explored in our earlier distributed-training discussions, and should be treated as the current best evidence against that lever; (2) structurally this is the same detection-vs-custody problem as MacDonald (2021, 2025) on TEL tracking: detection of individual nodes doesn't establish that they are part of a training run, just as detecting a TEL doesn't establish custody. Author's extrapolations are deliberately pessimistic for the evader, and the enforcement gap is described as actively growing with ongoing distributed-training research (Decoupled DiLoCo et al.).

### Zhou, Hernández-Orallo et al. (2025) — "General Scales Unlock AI Evaluation with Explanatory and Predictive Power"
**File:** `ZhouL_Hernandez-Orallo__2025--general_scales_ai_evaluation.pdf` | **Text:** `text/ZhouL_Hernandez-Orallo__2025--general_scales_ai_evaluation.txt` | **Pages:** ~90 body + appendices | *arXiv:2503.06378 v2, 16 Mar 2025* | Lexin Zhou (Cambridge Leverhulme CFI), Lorenzo Pacchiardi, Fernando Martínez-Plumed, Katherine M. Collins, Peter Henderson (Princeton), Sherry Tongshuang Wu (CMU), Patrick C. Kyllonen (Educational Testing Service), David Stillwell (Cambridge Psychometrics Centre), Lucy Cheke, Jindong Wang (W&M), Manuel Cebrian (CSIC), Xing Xie (MSRA), José Hernández-Orallo (VRAIN, UPV — corresponding) et al. (24 authors total). Platform: kinds-of-intelligence-cfi.github.io/ADELE.

Introduces a psychometrics-inspired evaluation methodology (ADeLe / DeLeAn) that replaces aggregate benchmark accuracy with **18 continuous 0–5+ "demand" scales** plus one 0–100 "unguessability" scale. Dimensions cover 11 primordial cognitive capabilities (attention, comprehension, learning/abstraction, three-part metacognition, mind-modelling & social cognition, quantitative & logical reasoning, spatial), 5 knowledge domains, and 2 "extraneous" confounders — **atypicality (proxy for contamination) and volume (proxy for amalgamation)** — plus unguessability capturing multiple-choice funnelling. Each benchmark instance is rubric-annotated at scale by GPT-4o; per-model "ability profiles" are derived from characteristic curves, and a Random Forest "assessor" trained on the 19-dim demand vector predicts per-instance success. Scale of the evaluation: **15 LLMs (Babbage → o1, LLaMA 1B → 405B, R1-Distill-Qwen 1.5B → 32B), 63 tasks / 20 benchmarks / 16,108 instances / 289,944 GPT-4o annotations**, with human inter-rater Delphi validation.

**Explanatory findings.** Most existing benchmarks lack either specificity (they claim to measure X but the instances span many other demand dimensions) or sensitivity (they don't cover the full demand range for X). Aggregate accuracy is *not* a comparable capability estimate: "70% average on logical reasoning" ≠ more capability than "50% on a different logical reasoning set" once demand distributions differ. The three extraneous dimensions explain a large share of benchmark-score variance — i.e. what looks like "capability" is often contamination, amalgamation, or funnelling. Scaling story: knowledge-dimension abilities scale mainly with parameter count and distillation; **reasoning, metacognition, and — flagged as surprising — mind-modelling / social capabilities are boosted specifically by chain-of-thought / inference-heavy training** (o1, R1-Distill).

**Predictive findings.** Per-instance success prediction, weighted-average AUROC / ECE across 15 LLMs: **demand-based RF = 0.839 / 0.011 in-distribution; 0.81 / 0.02 task-OOD; 0.75 / 0.04 benchmark-OOD**. Fine-tuned LLaMA baseline is on par in-distribution (0.840) but its advantage collapses OOD (0.79 task-OOD, sharp drop benchmark-OOD); GloVe-embeddings RF trails everywhere. Training cost: **demand-based RF = 4 seconds on an M3 Pro CPU per LLM; fine-tuned LLaMA baseline = 300 GPU-hours on a V100**. The cheapest and most interpretable method wins on OOD — the advantage grows precisely where deployment decisions actually live.

**Explicit policy hooks (Section 8).** Framed as tooling for "researchers, companies, third-party evaluators, policy-makers and regulators"; cites EU AI Act. **Two failure modes named for regulators: overestimation → hype and premature deployment; underestimation → regulators miss dangers.** Both stem from measuring the wrong construct. **Sandbagging warning:** because the rubrics are public, a provider could deliberately fail high-demand instances to construct a "believable error profile" and understate capability. Flipped positively, the same mechanism enables *deliberate capability limiting* — reject-on-high-demand — as an alternative to unlearning. Framework is explicitly extensible to safety propensities, values, and fairness, with the caveat that such scales are non-monotonic (like personality traits) and require recalibration.

**Operational note for Hill outreach.** This is the strongest current technical answer to the staffer question **"how do we know what the model can/can't do — and how do we know the vendor isn't gaming the eval?"** Useful in three conversations: (i) NIST / CAISI evaluation-infrastructure design — the paper's demonstration that per-instance prediction is possible with 19 interpretable dimensions cuts through "AI evaluation is unsolvable" framing; (ii) third-party auditor requirements (Amodei's *Policy on the AI Exponential* proposal in this library, and CAIP's audit-based model legislation in Green-Lowe 4/7) — Zhou et al. provide the technical vocabulary for what an audit could concretely produce beyond a benchmark score; (iii) the "measuring the wrong thing" critique of current benchmark-driven capability claims — pairs naturally with Aschenbrenner's trendlines (Zhou et al. show those trendlines conflate many distinct dimensions) and with the Compendium's "grown, not built" framing (rubric-annotation is exactly the kind of external structure the Compendium calls for). Sandbagging is a live consideration for any evaluation-based regulation — the honest framing is "public evaluation methodology is necessary AND introduces a specific gaming risk that requires defense-in-depth."

### Aumann & Lindell (2007) — "Security Against Covert Adversaries: Efficient Protocols for Realistic Adversaries"
**File:** `AumannY_LindellY__2007--security_against_covert_adversaries.pdf` | **Text:** `text/AumannY_LindellY__2007--security_against_covert_adversaries.txt` | **Pages:** 63 | *J. Cryptology 23: 281–343, 2010, doi:10.1007/s00145-009-9040-7 — the extended full-version paper. Preliminary version: TCC 2007 (LNCS 4392), 20pp; also IACR ePrint 2007/060. Received Oct 2007, revised Mar 2009, published Apr 2009.* | Yonatan Aumann & Yehuda Lindell (Bar-Ilan University Computer Science; comm. by Oded Goldreich)

Introduces the **covert adversary** threat model in secure multiparty computation — an intermediate rung between the classical **semi-honest** model (parties follow the protocol, only try to learn extra from what they see) and the **malicious** model (parties may deviate arbitrarily). A covert adversary will cheat if cheating pays off, but only if it can avoid being caught. Security is defined relative to a **deterrence factor ε ∈ (0, 1]**: any attempt to cheat is detected by the honest parties with probability at least ε. The definition is simulation-based and takes three formulations (failed-simulation, explicit-cheat, strong-explicit-cheat) forming a hierarchy of guarantees; modular sequential composition theorems are proved for all three. Motivating claim: in commercial, political, and diplomatic settings, entities "cannot afford the embarrassment, loss of reputation, and negative press associated with being caught cheating" — so a threshold detection probability substitutes for the near-certainty demanded by the malicious model.

**Concrete efficiency (Yao-style two-party protocol).** The construction modifies Yao's garbled-circuit protocol so that two garbled circuits are sent and one is randomly opened for verification. Basic construction achieves **ε = 1/2** at only **~2× the work and ~2× the bandwidth of semi-honest Yao** — constant rounds, one oblivious transfer per input bit, communication O(n·|C|) for security parameter n and circuit size |C|. Higher deterrence scales linearly in circuit copies: **10 copies → ε = 9/10**. Intermediate variant requires 2× the OTs of the basic variant; strongest variant needs n OTs per input bit. All three variants are dramatically cheaper than any then-known protocol secure against fully-malicious adversaries — presented as a proof-of-concept that "middle-ground" adversarial modeling can make previously-impractical secure computation practical.

**Relevance for policy-levers.** The covert-adversary frame is the game-theoretic backbone for **any regulatory regime that leans on audit + penalty rather than continuous prevention** — nuclear NRC, FAA post-incident rulemaking, NIH DURC oversight, and by extension the AI-safety incident regimes drawing on those comparators (Ortega 2025, and the FRONTIER Act architecture it informs). Two direct handles: (i) the ε parameter lets a regime designer trade enforcement cost against deterrence explicitly — "what audit frequency, disclosure obligation, and penalty magnitude produce a credible ε against a covert developer?" is a question with mathematical shape rather than only rhetorical shape; (ii) the frame maps cleanly onto **deceptive-alignment** concerns in AI safety — a model that would misbehave if it could avoid detection but complies when it might be caught is a covert adversary in this exact technical sense, and the paper offers a starting vocabulary for making that intuition precise. Useful as a citation whenever a conversation collapses into "we can only assume fully-adversarial developers or fully-cooperative ones"; the paper's core move is that a formal middle exists and admits efficient mechanisms.

### Aschenbrenner (2024) — "Situational Awareness: The Decade Ahead"
**File:** `Aschenbrenner_2024__situational_awareness.pdf` | **Text:** `text/Aschenbrenner_2024__situational_awareness.txt` | **Pages:** 165 | *June 2024 — situational-awareness.ai* | Leopold Aschenbrenner (formerly OpenAI Superalignment team, fired April 2024 over a disputed information-leak allegation; subsequently founded Situational Awareness LP, an AGI-thesis hedge fund managing >$1.5B AUM as of mid-2025)

The most-cited AGI-trajectory manifesto in current Hill discourse, structured as a 50,000-word five-part essay series. **Section I (Counting the OOMs)** argues AGI by 2027 is "strikingly plausible" by trusting trendlines on three orthogonal scaling axes: physical compute (~0.5 OOM/year), algorithmic efficiency (~0.5 OOM/year), and "unhobbling" gains (chain-of-thought, tool use, longer context, agentic scaffolding) producing chatbot-to-agent step-changes. **Section II (Intelligence Explosion)** argues that once AGI automates AI research, hundreds of millions of AGI-instances at 10x+ human speed could compress 5+ OOMs of algorithmic progress into ≤1 year, taking the world from human-level to vastly superhuman AI in months — "no sci-fi required." **Sections IIIa–IIId** pivot to geopolitics: trillion-dollar compute/power buildout (IIIa); US lab security as an existential national-security failure where "we are basically handing superintelligence to the CCP" because labs treat AGI weights "the way we would random SaaS software" (IIIb); superalignment as a real but solvable problem where "things could very easily go off the rails" but "we'll muddle through" (IIIc); and "the free world must prevail" — the argument that a US AGI lead is required for both safety (margin of error during a tight race) and survival (vs the CCP, vs authoritarian global dominance) (IIId). **Section IV (The Project)** predicts a national-security takeover of AGI development by 2027/28, parallel to the Manhattan Project, on the grounds that "no startup can handle superintelligence" — the document's most concrete policy prediction. **Section V (Parting Thoughts)** closes with "AGI realism," positioning Aschenbrenner between "doomers" (whom he characterizes as ossified and untethered from deep-learning empirics) and e/acc accelerationists.

The piece is structurally closer to George Kennan's 1946 "Long Telegram" than to a research paper — empirical claims pressed into service for a strategic frame. The trendline arguments have largely held through April 2026: METR (2025, this library) measures the 50%-task-completion time horizon doubling every ~7 months; Mertens et al. (2026, this library) finds frontier models at 50% success on full-workday tasks by late 2024 — both broadly consistent with Aschenbrenner's pace projections. The "Project" predictions are partially borne out by the December 2025 federal-preemption EO, the Stargate buildout, and the Trump-administration AI Action Plan, though in a form Aschenbrenner did not specify (preemption rather than nationalization) and pointing in a direction (less regulation, faster capex) that diverges from the cautious-stewardship version he sketched. Several premises are asserted rather than argued and stand in direct tension with positions elsewhere in this library: (a) **alignment solvability** is assumed without engaging the Compendium's argument that controllability cannot be demonstrated prior to deployment, or Jones (2024)'s formal result that under standard CRRA risk-aversion the welfare-tolerable extinction probability is ~4% — well below Hinton/Amodei's stated estimates of 10–25%; (b) the geopolitical frame **collapses safety and acceleration** (a US lead is presented as both safety-imperative and economic-imperative), eliding the "racing causes safety to be deprioritized" tension that motivates the Hawley-Blumenthal AIRE Act and the Narrow Path moratorium; (c) **the "doomer" dismissal** is rhetorical, not argumentative, but has propagated through Aschenbrenner's readership in policy circles as a shorthand reason to ignore slowdown advocacy.

**Conflict of interest worth surfacing in any citation:** Aschenbrenner founded Situational Awareness LP shortly after the essay's release — a long/short equity hedge fund explicitly built on the essay's thesis (AGI-driven capex into compute, power, semiconductors, infrastructure), seeded by Patrick & John Collison, Nat Friedman, and Daniel Gross. As of late 2025 the fund managed ~$1.5B AUM with ~$5.5B in U.S. equity exposure, returned ~47% in H1 2025, and Aschenbrenner has reportedly invested nearly all his personal net worth in it. The thesis and the fund are explicitly co-promoted (the launch was paired with a four-hour Dwarkesh Patel podcast in which Aschenbrenner stated: "if AGI were priced in tomorrow, you could maybe make 100x"). The fund's performance depends on short AGI timelines, large AI-infrastructure capex, and regulation that doesn't slow either. This does not invalidate any argument in the essay, but it materially affects how the document's policy recommendations (oppose pauses, oppose regulation that slows capex) should be weighted.

**Operational note for Hill outreach:** This is the load-bearing reference for the "we must race" framing staffers cite when opposing pre-deployment evaluation or state-level regulation. The most productive counter is not to dispute the trendlines (which are roughly correct) but to point at (i) Jones (2024) — the trendlines being right makes the case for slowdown *stronger*, not weaker, under any reasonable risk-aversion assumption; (ii) Kording & Marinescu (2025) — the "shared prosperity from AGI" frame omits the wage hump-then-fall trajectory their model produces; (iii) Leahy 2024 / Miotti 2025 — the same trendlines support the opposite policy conclusion (prohibition until controllability is demonstrated). For senators on Armed Services, Intelligence, or Foreign Relations committees, the "Free World" framing is likely to have been pre-absorbed by their AI staff.
